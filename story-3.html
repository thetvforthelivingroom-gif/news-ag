SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<strong>SpaceX’s Starship SN20: A Suborbital Milestone and a Cautionary Tale</strong> On the morning of April 18 2024, SpaceX launched the Starship SN20 from its Boca Chica launch site in Texas. The 120‑tonne vehicle lifted off with a full twelve‑engine Raptor first stage and a single Raptor on the second stage, following the ascent profile that had been refined over a decade of iterative testing. The flight, however, ended abruptly after only five minutes when telemetry revealed a loss of thrust in the second‑stage engine, forcing an automatic abort and a controlled descent that splashed down in the Gulf of Mexico. While the vehicle never achieved orbital velocity, the test was a critical step in validating the design and demonstrating the rapid‑iteration philosophy that underpins SpaceX’s Mars ambitions. The SN20 flight was the culmination of a series of suborbital prototypes that began with SN8 in 2019 and progressed through SN16, each test adding incremental improvements in heat‑shield design, landing algorithms, and propulsion reliability. In 2022, the SN15 prototype achieved a historic splash‑down after a 15‑minute flight, marking the first successful landing of a full‑scale Starship vehicle and validating the stainless‑steel heat shield that will protect future orbital missions [1]. The SN20 test aimed to push the envelope further by extending the flight duration to 20 minutes and testing the vehicle’s ability to perform a controlled re‑entry from a higher altitude, thereby providing a more realistic assessment of the systems that will be required for orbital insertion. Despite the abort, the flight offered valuable data. Engineers noted that the second‑stage Raptor engine experienced a sudden drop in chamber pressure, likely due to a transient anomaly in the methane feed system. The automatic abort sequence, which shut down the engine and deployed the landing legs, was executed flawlessly, demonstrating the robustness of the vehicle’s guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) architecture. The controlled descent and splash‑down, while not the intended outcome, provided a real‑world test of the heat‑shield performance and re‑entry trajectory calculations that will be critical for future orbital missions [2]. SpaceX’s rapid‑iteration approach—often conducting multiple flights within a few months—has drawn both praise and criticism. Proponents argue that the iterative cycle accelerates development and reduces costs, while critics caution that it may compromise safety and reliability. The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation has responded by tightening its flight safety analysis requirements for first‑flight orbital missions, mandating more extensive data collection and simulation validation prior to launch approval [3]. NASA, which has partnered with SpaceX on the Artemis program, has also increased its oversight, insisting that any vehicle intended for crewed or cargo missions to the Moon must demonstrate a proven track record of orbital performance before contracts can be awarded [4]. Investor sentiment has reflected the tension between ambition and risk. While SpaceX’s valuation has surged in recent years, the abrupt abort of SN20 prompted a brief dip in the company’s private market valuation, as venture capital firms reassessed the risk profile of the Starship program. Analysts note that the company’s ability to secure future contracts—particularly with NASA’s Artemis program—will hinge on demonstrating reliable orbital flight. A successful orbital insertion would not only validate the vehicle’s design but also unlock access to a new class of commercial launch services, potentially reshaping the competitive landscape for heavy‑lift vehicles. Competitors such as Blue Origin’s New Glenn and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan have positioned themselves as alternatives to SpaceX’s Starship, emphasizing their own rigorous testing regimes and regulatory compliance. New Glenn’s first orbital flight is scheduled for 2025, while Vulcan’s launch window is slated for 2026. If SpaceX’s orbital ambitions stall, these competitors could capture market share, especially among customers prioritizing proven reliability over rapid development [5]. Looking ahead, SpaceX plans to conduct a thorough investigation into the second‑stage engine anomaly, with the goal of refining the methane feed system and improving engine reliability. The company has indicated that it will continue to test suborbital prototypes while incrementally extending flight duration and altitude, with a target of achieving a successful orbital insertion by the end of 2025. This timeline aligns with NASA’s Artemis Phase 2 schedule, which requires a commercial crew vehicle capable of lunar transfer by 2025, thereby positioning SpaceX to secure a significant share of the lunar supply chain if the orbital test succeeds [6]. Regulatory developments will continue to shape the trajectory of the Starship program. The FAA’s updated risk‑assessment framework may require additional flight data and safety certifications before future orbital launches, potentially extending the development timeline. NASA’s evolving safety requirements for commercial crew and cargo missions will likewise influence SpaceX’s design and testing priorities, especially as the agency seeks to diversify its launch provider base. These regulatory pressures underscore the broader industry trend toward more stringent oversight of commercial launch providers, particularly those operating in the high‑stakes domain of human spaceflight. In sum, the SN20 suborbital flight—though short‑lived—marks a pivotal moment in the race to make reusable heavy‑lift launch vehicles a commercial reality. The event underscores the delicate balance between rapid iteration, regulatory compliance, and market confidence, and it will likely influence the trajectory of SpaceX’s Mars ambitions, NASA’s Artemis program, and the broader commercial space industry for years to come. <strong>References</strong> [1] SpaceX. “Starship SN15 Test Flight.” *SpaceX Press Release*, 8 Feb 2022.
