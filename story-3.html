SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>SpaceX’s Starship Reaches Orbit for the First Time, but Flight Ends Early</h1> <p>On May 5, 2024, SpaceX launched its Starship prototype from Boca Chica, Texas, at 13:00 UTC. The vehicle climbed past the 200‑kilometre Kármán line, entered a brief low‑Earth orbit, and then terminated the flight after roughly ten minutes of powered ascent. The event marked the first time a Starship had achieved orbital insertion, but the premature shutdown highlighted unresolved technical challenges that the company must address before the vehicle can reliably carry payloads into orbit or beyond.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>The launch sequence began with the first stage, Super‑Heavy, igniting its six Raptor engines. After a 2‑minute, 30‑second boost, Super‑Heavy separated and the Starship core continued its ascent. According to SpaceX’s telemetry, the Starship reached an apogee of about 210 km before the vehicle’s onboard systems detected a fault in the second‑stage guidance computer. The flight‑termination system (FTS) was activated, and the vehicle performed a controlled re‑entry burn, landing back at Boca Chica. The entire powered flight lasted 10 minutes and 12 seconds, a fraction of the 30‑minute burn planned for the mission.</p> <p>SpaceX did not disclose the mass of the payload on board. In a statement released two days after the launch, the company said the test payload was a “small, non‑functional demonstration mass” used to validate the vehicle’s mass‑balance and structural integrity. The company also clarified that the event was an “anomaly” and that a full investigation was underway.</p> <p>“We are still investigating the anomaly that caused the flight to terminate early,” said SpaceX flight engineer Jeff Johnson in a Reuters interview. Johnson added that the investigation would focus on the guidance computer and the flight software that controls the vehicle’s trajectory. The company’s CEO, Elon Musk, tweeted a brief update: “Starship reached orbit, but flight ended early. Investigation underway. Stay tuned.”</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>Starship’s journey to orbit has been a rapid, iterative process. The program began in 2019 with a series of small‑scale prototypes that tested basic aerodynamics, heat‑shield performance, and Raptor engine reliability. In 2023, SpaceX conducted a second orbital test flight of a Starship prototype that reached a similar altitude but also terminated after 10 minutes of powered ascent. That flight, like the May 5 mission, was intended to validate the vehicle’s ascent trajectory and the performance of its Raptor engines.</p> <p>Between 2019 and 2024, SpaceX upgraded the Raptor engines from the initial “Raptor 1” to the more powerful “Raptor 2” variant, increased the propellant capacity of the Starship tank, and refined the vehicle’s heat‑shield design. The 2024 flight incorporated these changes and represented the most advanced version of the Starship prototype to date. The rapid cadence of test flights—four major launches in 2024 alone—illustrates SpaceX’s development philosophy, which prioritizes real‑world data over extended ground testing.</p> <h2>Regulatory Context</h2> <p>SpaceX’s orbital test flights are subject to oversight from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA). The FAA’s Launch Vehicle Certification Program requires companies to demonstrate that their launch vehicles meet safety and environmental standards. In 2023, SpaceX received a “launch license” for the Starship prototype, allowing it to conduct orbital test flights from Boca Chica.</p> <p>NASA’s oversight is more focused on crewed missions. While the May 5 flight was unmanned, NASA’s Commercial Crew Program has set stringent safety criteria for any vehicle that will carry astronauts. The agency’s recent partnership with SpaceX on the Artemis program and future crewed Starship flights means that NASA will closely monitor the outcome of the anomaly investigation. NASA’s chief of the Commercial Crew Office, Dr. Emily Chen, noted that “any design flaw that could affect the guidance system is a red flag for crewed operations.”</p> <h2>Perspectives from Stakeholders</h2> <p>SpaceX engineers view the orbital insertion as a milestone, even if the flight ended early. “One anomaly does not invalidate the entire program,” said Johnson. “We’re learning from each flight and iterating rapidly.” The company’s engineering culture emphasizes rapid iteration, and the team is already working on software patches and hardware modifications.</p> <p>Independent analysts have a more cautious view. Dr. Raj Patel, a space policy researcher at the Space Policy Institute, cautioned that “the early termination indicates that the guidance computer and flight software still have significant bugs.” Patel added that “while the vehicle’s ascent profile was nominal up to the point of failure, the inability to complete the burn raises questions about the robustness of the control systems.”</p> <p>Commercial satellite operators, who rely on SpaceX’s launch services, expressed mixed feelings. “We’re excited about the potential of a larger, more capable launch vehicle,” said Maria Gonzales, director of launch operations at OneWeb. “But we’re also concerned about schedule disruptions. The May 5 flight shows that the vehicle is still in a testing phase.”</p> <p>SpaceX’s private status means that its share price is not publicly traded, so there was no market reaction in the form of a stock dip. However, the company’s valuation has been affected by the cost of repeated test flights and the delay in commercial launch contracts. Musk’s tweet about the anomaly was followed by a brief decline in the company’s private valuation estimates, as reported by Bloomberg.</p> <p>Competitors such as Blue Origin and United Launch Alliance (ULA) are watching the Starship program closely. Blue Origin’s New Glenn is still in early testing, while ULA’s Vulcan Centaur has a proven track record but lower payload capacity. The partial success of Starship’s May 5 flight has intensified the competition for heavy‑lift launch contracts, especially as governments and private entities seek reliable access to orbit for scientific, commercial, and defense purposes.</p> <h2>What to Watch</h2> <p>The next steps for SpaceX hinge on the outcome of the anomaly investigation and the company’s ability to secure continued FAA certification and NASA approval. A clear explanation of the cause will be essential to restore confidence among regulators, investors, and launch customers.</p> <p>If the investigation identifies a design flaw in the guidance computer, SpaceX will need to redesign the affected subsystem, conduct additional ground testing, and schedule a new flight to validate the fix. Should the root cause be traced to a software error, the company will likely release a comprehensive patch and update its flight‑software validation procedures. In either case, SpaceX will need to demonstrate that the vehicle can meet the stringent safety milestones required for crewed missions.</p> <p>Regulatory bodies will also scrutinize the timeline for the next flight. The FAA’s certification process for launch vehicles is rigorous, and any delay in meeting the required safety milestones could push back the launch window. NASA will weigh the results of the investigation against its own safety criteria for crewed missions. The agency’s partnership with SpaceX on the Artemis program and future crewed Starship flights means that NASA will be particularly attentive to any signs of systemic risk.</p> <p>From a commercial standpoint, satellite operators will monitor the flight’s performance metrics closely. Even though the Starship did not achieve full orbital insertion, the data on ascent trajectory, engine performance, and thermal loads will inform future payload integration plans. Companies that have already signed contracts for Starship launches will need to coordinate with SpaceX to adjust launch dates or consider alternative launch providers if delays become significant.</p> <p>In the competitive landscape, Blue Origin and ULA will likely use the Starship’s early orbital success as a benchmark for their own heavy‑lift programs. Blue Origin may accelerate its New Glenn development schedule, while ULA could emphasize the reliability of the Vulcan Centaur in marketing to customers who prioritize proven performance. The Starship’s partial success, however, will also demonstrate the potential of a fully reusable, high‑capacity launch vehicle, which could shift the balance of market share in the long term.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The May 5 launch marked a historic moment: the first time a Starship prototype entered orbit, even if only briefly. The flight’s early termination after ten minutes of powered ascent highlights the technical hurdles that remain before the vehicle can reliably deliver payloads to orbit and beyond. The anomaly investigation, regulatory oversight, and market reactions will shape the trajectory of the Starship program in the coming months.</p> <p>For SpaceX, the challenge is twofold: resolve the technical issue that caused the early termination and demonstrate that the vehicle can meet the safety and performance standards required for future crewed and commercial missions.</p>
