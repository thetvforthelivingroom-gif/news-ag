SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
SpaceX’s Starship SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica on Thursday, but the vehicle was brought down after just 2 minutes and 12 seconds when the flight‑termination system was triggered. The abort, which occurred only 90 seconds into the ascent, left engineers with a data‑rich but incomplete test and highlighted the trade‑offs between rapid development and safety in the pursuit of reusable orbital launch vehicles.

What happened

At 11:00 p.m. EDT, Starship SN20 ignited its Super‑Heavy booster and accelerated toward orbit. The launch followed a 15‑minute countdown that included a 10‑minute hover at the pad, a 5‑minute “pad‑proximity” phase, and a 30‑second “boost‑phase” during which the Super‑Heavy’s 33 Merlin engines fired. Within 90 seconds of liftoff, the vehicle’s trajectory exceeded the planned velocity envelope. At 2 minutes and 12 seconds, the flight‑termination system was activated, severing the booster and upper stage and causing the vehicle to re‑enter the atmosphere over the Gulf of Mexico. The recovery team collected the intact vehicle and all onboard telemetry for analysis.

The abort was triggered by a software anomaly detected in the guidance computer that indicated a potential loss of control. Engineers later confirmed that the anomaly was not due to a structural failure but to a transient data error in the guidance system. The payload—a small, non‑human test module carrying sensors to record the vehicle’s performance—was recovered during the safe return.

Background and context

Starship is part of SpaceX’s long‑term vision to ferry humans and cargo to Mars and beyond. The spacecraft’s design incorporates a stainless‑steel hull, a heat‑shield that can survive re‑entry at 10,000 mph, and a fully reusable launch system that can be refueled on the ground in less than an hour. The first orbital flight marks a milestone in the company’s rapid‑iteration development approach, which has included dozens of suborbital test flights, several high‑profile failures, and a 2020 prototype explosion that destroyed the SN1 vehicle during a static‑fire test.

The 2020 explosion, which occurred during a static‑fire test of the Starship prototype SN1, was widely reported as a setback but also highlighted the risks inherent in pushing the boundaries of rocket technology. The incident prompted a redesign of the vehicle’s propellant feed system and a reassessment of safety protocols. Subsequent suborbital flights—SN8, SN10, and SN15—demonstrated progressive improvements in launch performance, but each test also revealed new challenges in propulsion, guidance, and structural integrity.

SpaceX’s approach to development—rapid prototyping, aggressive testing, and iterative redesign—has been praised for accelerating progress but also criticized for potential safety trade‑offs. The company’s public statements emphasize that each test is designed to gather data that will inform the next iteration, with the ultimate goal of achieving a fully reusable, cost‑effective launch system.

Regulatory oversight has played a significant role in the Starship program. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the U.S. Space Force have granted launch licenses and monitored the safety of the launch site. The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation issued a launch license for the Starship program after reviewing the company’s safety plan and environmental impact statement. The U.S. Space Force’s Space Launch Delta 45 has also provided regulatory guidance to ensure that the launch complies with national security and safety standards.

The early abort has prompted discussions about the balance between speed and safety. While SpaceX’s engineers view the abort as a valuable data point that will refine the guidance system, critics argue that the incident highlights the need for more rigorous pre‑flight checks and a longer testing window before attempting orbital flights. The FAA’s oversight role will likely intensify as the company seeks to demonstrate the reliability of its launch system for future commercial and government missions.

Investor confidence in SpaceX’s Mars agenda remains high, but the abort has introduced a degree of caution among some stakeholders. The company’s valuation, already in the tens of billions, is supported by its contracts with NASA and private customers, but the incident underscores the financial risk of rapid development. NASA’s Commercial Crew and Commercial Lunar Payload Services programs have awarded contracts to SpaceX, and the agency’s partnership will likely continue, but the agency will monitor the company’s safety record closely.

The incident has sparked a broader conversation about the role of private companies in national space policy and the need for transparent safety protocols.

Engineers will review telemetry to determine whether the anomaly was a software glitch, a sensor fault, or a design oversight. The company will likely adjust its flight‑termination logic and guidance software before attempting another orbital flight.

Regulatory agencies will examine the findings of the FAA and the U.S. Space Force to assess whether additional safety measures are required. The FAA may revise its launch licensing process to incorporate more stringent pre‑flight verification steps, while the Space Force may update its oversight protocols for commercial launch operators.

The impact on SpaceX’s Mars timeline is uncertain. While the abort does not invalidate the company’s long‑term plans, it may delay the first orbital flight that could carry humans to Mars or serve as a demonstrator for interplanetary missions. The company’s reusable launch economics, which rely on rapid turnaround between flights, may be affected by the need for more extensive testing and potential redesigns.

Investor sentiment will likely be influenced by the company’s ability to demonstrate a clear path to reliable orbital launches.
