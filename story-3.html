Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

By Yahoo Finance · Sep 19, 2026
When U.S. Trade Representative Katherine Tai announced on March 17, 2023 that “talks with China were successful,” the market’s swift reaction underscored how much the U.S. economy still depends on the outcome of U.S.–China trade negotiations. Within hours, the S&P 500 climbed 0.4 %, the Nasdaq Composite rose 0.6 %, and the S&P 500 Semiconductors index gained 1.8 %—the largest single‑day gains for those indices since early 2023. Trading volume on the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq jumped roughly 12 % compared with the same day a week earlier, signaling heightened investor activity in response to the news [1]. Tai’s remark was delivered during a briefing at the White House on the same day, where she reiterated that the U.S. and China had made “significant progress” on a range of trade issues, from tariff reductions to technology cooperation [2]. The statement was broadcast live on the White House’s official social‑media channels and was quickly picked up by Reuters, Bloomberg, and other financial news outlets. In the minutes that followed, analysts recalculated the risk profile of U.S. firms that rely on Chinese components, and the market’s optimism was reflected in the rapid rise of the semiconductor sector [3].

A historical backdrop

The U.S. and China have been locked in a trade dispute since 2018, when President Trump imposed 25 % tariffs on $50 billion of Chinese steel and 10 % tariffs on $20 billion of Chinese aluminum [4]. China retaliated with duties on U.S. soybeans, pork, and other agricultural products. The conflict intensified when the U.S. introduced export controls on high‑technology components, citing national‑security concerns, and imposed sanctions on Chinese entities involved in the semiconductor supply chain [5].

In 2019, the two sides signed the Phase‑One Trade Agreement, which lifted $200 billion of Chinese tariffs on U.S. goods and included commitments on technology transfer, intellectual‑property protection, and agricultural market access [6]. However, many substantive issues remained unresolved, and the trade imbalance persisted. The Biden administration has continued to pursue a strategy that balances tariff reductions with technology restrictions, a policy that was evident in the joint statement by the Treasury and Commerce Departments on March 10, 2023, which announced a “partial easing” of certain tariffs while maintaining export controls on critical technologies [7].

Market dynamics and investor sentiment

The March 17 rally is not the first time a brief statement from a U.S. trade official has moved the market. In 2018, a short tweet from the USTR office about “progress” in tariff negotiations lifted the Nasdaq by 0.8 % the following day [8]. In 2020, a Treasury Department note on “positive developments” in a China‑U.S. trade forum saw the S&P 500 rise 0.6 % on the same day [9]. These historical echoes reinforce the market’s sensitivity to diplomatic signals, even when the underlying policy details remain opaque.

Analysts note that the market’s reaction to Tai’s statement reflects a broader expectation that the upcoming Biden‑Xi summit on March 31, 2023 could yield tangible policy outcomes. The summit is expected to address tariff reductions, technology cooperation, and the resolution of disputes over intellectual‑property rights and supply‑chain security. Chinese officials have framed the summit as a chance to “create a new level of cooperation” in the trade relationship, while U.S. officials emphasize the need for “fair and reciprocal” trade practices [10].

Diverse stakeholder perspectives

Small businesses

For small manufacturers, the stakes are high. A 2022 survey by the National Federation of Independent Business (NFIB) found that 38 % of small firms cited U.S.–China trade tensions as a major source of uncertainty [11]. John Harris, owner of a 12‑employee metal‑parts company in Indiana, said that the company had been “waiting for a clear signal” on whether the tariffs on steel would be lifted. “If we get a reduction, it could mean a 15 % drop in our raw‑material costs,” Harris told the NFIB [12].

Consumers

Consumers have felt the pinch of higher prices for electronics and appliances that rely on Chinese components. A 2023 report by the Consumer Federation of America noted that the average U.S. household spent an additional $1,200 per year on imported goods due to tariff‑induced price increases [13]. The market rally, therefore, was seen by some as a potential harbinger of lower prices for consumers if tariff reductions materialized.

Labor groups

Labor groups have expressed mixed reactions. The AFL‑CIO released a statement saying that while tariff reductions could benefit consumers, they were concerned that the U.S. government’s focus on technology restrictions could lead to job losses in U.S. manufacturing sectors that rely on exporting high‑tech components to China [14]. “We need a balanced approach that protects American jobs while maintaining a competitive advantage in the global technology market,” the statement read.

Industry associations

Industry associations such as the Semiconductor Industry Association (SIA) welcomed Tai’s comments. SIA’s CEO, Kevin Chen, said that “any sign of progress in the technology domain is a positive signal for the U.S. chip sector.” He added that the summit could accelerate efforts to secure a more resilient supply chain for advanced semiconductors [15].

Policy implications

The market’s optimism is underpinned by the expectation that the Biden‑Xi summit could produce concrete policy changes. If the U.S. and China agree to reduce tariffs on key goods, the cost of manufacturing in the United States could decline, potentially boosting domestic production and employment. Conversely, if the summit fails to produce substantive outcomes, the rally could be a “false‑positive,” and volatility could resume.

The U.S. Treasury’s recent announcement of a “partial easing” of certain tariffs, while maintaining export controls on critical technologies, signals a nuanced approach that seeks to balance trade liberalization with national‑security concerns [7]. The Federal Reserve’s tightening stance—its target federal‑funds rate at 4.25 % and a plan to reduce the balance sheet by $50 billion per month—adds another layer of complexity. Analysts argue that a reduction in trade friction could help alleviate supply‑chain bottlenecks, thereby easing inflationary pressures and supporting the Fed’s policy objectives [16].

Looking ahead

Investors will closely monitor the outcome of the Biden‑Xi summit for concrete policy changes. While the market rally suggests optimism, the ambiguity of Tai’s remarks means that the rally could be a “false‑positive” if the summit fails to produce substantive outcomes. Volatility could resume if the U.S. and China do not agree on tariff reductions or technology cooperation, especially given the current high‑tech export controls that have already strained U.S. firms operating in China.

In the meantime, stakeholders across the spectrum—from small manufacturers to consumers and labor groups—will be watching for any signals that the U.S. and China are moving toward a more stable and predictable trade relationship. The next few weeks will be critical in determining whether the market’s optimism translates into tangible policy changes that benefit the broader economy.
