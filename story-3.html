<h1>Satellites Reveal a 12‑Trillion‑Ton Ice Loss: A Wake‑Up Call for Global Policy</h1>

<p>For the first time in human history, a fleet of orbiting eyes has quantified the exact amount of ice that has vanished from the world’s two largest glaciers. The numbers are staggering: more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice have been shed from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years, a loss that could raise global sea level by roughly two metres if all of it were to melt. Yet the international climate‑damage fund that was supposed to help vulnerable nations recover from such losses remains mired in bureaucracy, leaving communities—like the flood‑hit villages of Nepal’s Himalayas—waiting for money that never arrives.</p>

<p>Since the launch of the first Earth‑observation satellite, Geosat, in 1976, a succession of missions—Soviet‑Russian, ESA, NASA, and the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑2—has refined the art of measuring ice‑sheet height with centimetre precision. By comparing successive altimetry profiles, scientists convert changes in elevation into changes in mass, producing a time series of ice‑loss rates that is both precise and globally consistent.</p>

<p>Recent analyses that combine data from CryoSat‑2, ICESat‑2, and the latest Jason‑3 ocean‑altimetry mission show that Greenland has shed about 3.5 trillion tonnes of ice since 1973, while Antarctica has lost roughly 8.5 trillion tonnes in the same period. The split reflects the different dynamics of the two ice sheets: Greenland’s loss is dominated by surface melt, whereas Antarctica’s is driven largely by ice‑stream acceleration and the collapse of ice shelves that buttress the continental ice.</p>
