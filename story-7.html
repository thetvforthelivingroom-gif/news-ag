Background & context (from research brief)

Satellites have counted a loss of more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years—an amount that could raise global sea levels by two metres, threatening millions of people and billions of dollars in infrastructure. Yet the world’s climate‑damage fund remains locked in a bureaucratic maze, unable to deliver the money that communities like Nepal’s flood‑hit villages urgently need.

Since the early 1970s, a fleet of Earth‑observing satellites has tracked the height of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets with centimeter‑level precision. By comparing successive measurements, scientists have been able to quantify the volume of ice lost each year. The most recent analyses, drawing on data from a decade of missions, show that the combined mass loss of the two polar regions has exceeded 12 trillion tons in the last 47 years, a figure that translates into roughly a two‑metre rise in global sea level if the ice were to melt completely [1].

The loss is not uniform. Greenland has shed about 6 trillion tons, while Antarctica has contributed roughly 6 trillion tons as well, with the latter’s contribution fluctuating more dramatically due to the complex interplay of ice streams and oceanic currents. The satellite record reveals a clear acceleration: the annual rate of loss has more than doubled over the past two decades, a trend that aligns with the rapid intensification of greenhouse‑gas emissions worldwide.

The satellite altimetry program began with the launch of the Geosat satellite in 1976, followed by a series of successors—Soviet‑Russian, ESA, and NASA missions—that have refined the technique and expanded coverage. By 2020, the network included the CryoSat‑2, Jason‑3, and the recently launched IceSat‑3, all of which provide high‑resolution, multi‑year datasets that allow scientists to separate natural variability from long‑term trends.
