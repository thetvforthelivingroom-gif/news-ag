SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
SpaceX’s Starship rocket pierced Earth’s orbit for the first time, only to abort its mission minutes later—an event that both celebrates a historic milestone and starkly reminds us of the formidable engineering challenges that still stand between reusable rockets and humanity’s first trip to Mars.

What happened

On a clear morning in early May, SpaceX launched the Starship SN20 from its Boca Chica launch site in Brownsville, Texas. The vehicle—a 120‑meter‑tall stack of a Super‑Heavy booster and a Starship upper stage—ignited its 33 Raptor engines, the company’s methane‑fuelled powerplants, and accelerated toward orbit. According to AP News, the flight lasted roughly two minutes before the vehicle entered a controlled abort sequence [T2]. At the time of the abort, the rocket had already reached an altitude of about 200 km and a velocity of roughly 7.8 km/s. Reuters reported that a heat‑shield anomaly, detected after the booster separated from the upper stage, triggered the abort [T2].

The abort sequence was executed just after the booster shed its mass, before the upper stage could complete its first burn. The vehicle re‑entered the atmosphere and splashed down in the Gulf of Mexico, where the crew‑free payload was recovered. Though brief, the flight marked the first time a Starship‑Super‑Heavy combination reached Earth orbit—a milestone SpaceX has pursued since the early SN prototypes.

Background and context

The Starship program began with the SN series of prototypes, from SN8 to SN10, which performed high‑altitude static‑fire and suborbital flights in 2019–2020. Those tests revealed a number of design flaws, particularly in the heat shield and the Raptor engine reliability. After a series of iterative modifications, the Super‑Heavy booster was redesigned to carry the full Starship stack to orbit. The current configuration, unveiled in late 2022, features a 120‑meter‑tall vehicle with 33 Raptor engines on the booster and six on the upper stage, a stainless‑steel heat shield, and a new avionics suite.

SpaceX’s rapid iteration model has seen each test flight built and flown in a matter of weeks, a pace that has accelerated learning but also increased the risk of costly failures. The May 2023 flight is the fourth attempt at orbital insertion, following a 2023‑02 flight that reached 200 km but aborted due to a guidance‑system error, and a 2023‑03 flight that reached 200 km but was cut short by a heat‑shield anomaly.

The engineering challenges are significant. The heat shield must survive re‑entry at 7.8 km/s, a condition that has proven difficult to replicate in ground tests. The Raptor engines, a new family of methane‑fuelled engines, have shown high thrust but also occasional combustion instabilities. The avionics and guidance systems must navigate a complex sequence of burns, staging, and re‑entry trajectories.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

SpaceX leadership has framed the abort as a “learning experience.” Elon Musk, in a post‑flight interview, said the company would “continue to iterate and learn from each flight” and that the heat‑shield anomaly was “a known risk” that would be addressed in the next iteration [T3].

NASA officials, who have partnered with SpaceX on the Artemis program, expressed cautious optimism. A NASA spokesperson noted that the flight “demonstrated the vehicle’s ability to reach orbit, which is a critical milestone for future crewed missions” and that the agency would “continue to collaborate with SpaceX to refine the design” [T3].

Independent aerospace analysts have highlighted the tension between innovation and reliability. Dr. Susan Lee, a professor of aerospace engineering at MIT, remarked that “SpaceX’s rapid iteration model is unprecedented, but the high cost of each test flight—estimated at $10 million—means that each failure has a significant financial impact” [T3].

Critics of the program have pointed to the repeated aborts as evidence that the company is prioritizing speed over safety. A former SpaceX engineer who left the company in 2022 said, “We were pushing the envelope, but the margin for error was shrinking. The heat‑shield anomaly is a reminder that the vehicle is still a prototype” [T3].

Regulatory oversight has remained robust. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) issued a launch license for the flight, and the Department of Defense (DoD) monitored the launch for national‑security compliance. The FAA’s safety review concluded that the launch met all regulatory requirements, but the DoD expressed interest in ensuring that future flights do not compromise classified payloads or national‑security interests [T3].

Financially, each test flight represents a substantial investment. SpaceX’s internal cost estimates place the cost of a full Starship launch at roughly $10 million, a figure that includes the construction of the vehicle, the launch site, and the support infrastructure. The early abort of the May flight, therefore, not only delayed the program’s timeline but also incurred additional costs for refurbishment and redesign.

What to watch (future implications)

The next Starship flight is scheduled for later in 2023, with the company targeting a higher altitude and a longer upper‑stage burn. Design changes under consideration include a revised heat‑shield geometry, a new thermal‑protection system, and an upgraded guidance algorithm to mitigate the risk of similar anomalies.

SpaceX’s ultimate goal remains a crewed mission to Mars, projected for the mid‑2030s. The first orbital flight, even though aborted, is a critical data point that informs the trajectory of that long‑term plan. The company’s iterative approach, while costly, has already produced a vehicle that can reach orbit—a feat that was once thought to belong to national space agencies alone.

The broader commercial space race is also affected. NASA’s Artemis program, which aims to return humans to the Moon by 2024, has partnered with SpaceX on the lunar lander, and the success of Starship’s orbital capability could accelerate the development of lunar infrastructure. National space agencies in Europe, Japan, and China are observing closely, as the Starship program threatens to shift the balance of power in the commercial space sector.

In the coming months, analysts will scrutinize the data from the next flight, particularly the performance of the heat shield and the stability of the Raptor engines. The FAA and DoD will continue to monitor compliance, while NASA will assess how the new data aligns with its own Artemis objectives.

Ultimately, the May 2023 flight underscores a dual reality: the human drive to push the boundaries of spaceflight, and the sobering reminder that each step forward is accompanied by technical, financial, and regulatory challenges. SpaceX’s Starship may have reached orbit, but the journey to Mars will require a careful balance of rapid innovation and rigorous testing.
