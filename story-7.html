Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

By Yahoo Finance · Sep 19, 2026
<strong>Stocks rally after USTR signals progress in U.S.–China talks ahead of Trump‑Xi summit</strong> U.S. Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer’s brief statement on April 11, 2018, sparked a modest uptick in global markets. Investors interpreted the comment as a hint that the long‑standing tariff standoff between Washington and Beijing might ease, prompting the S&P 500 to close up 0.60 %, the Nasdaq Composite to rise 0.90 %, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average to gain 0.40 % on the day of the announcement. In Asia, the Shanghai Composite climbed 0.80 % and the Shenzhen Component gained 0.50 % [1]. Analysts cautioned that the remarks were largely symbolic and that real policy shifts would only materialize after the Trump‑Xi summit in Washington, D.C.

What happened

On April 11, 2018, Lighthizer held a press briefing in Washington, D.C., and said that the United States was “making progress” in trade talks with China. The remarks came just hours before the scheduled summit between President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping, set for April 12–13 2018 [2]. Immediately after the statement, the S&P 500 closed up 0.60 %, the Nasdaq Composite rose 0.90 %, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average gained 0.40 %. In Asia, the Shanghai Composite index climbed 0.80 %, while the Shenzhen Component gained 0.50 %. These gains were driven largely by technology and consumer‑discretionary stocks, sectors that have felt the brunt of U.S.‑China trade tensions.

While the markets responded positively, Lighthizer’s statement was brief and vague. He did not disclose any specific concessions or tariff reductions, nor did he outline a timetable for policy changes. The lack of detail left investors uncertain about the durability of the gains and whether the “progress” would translate into concrete policy shifts.

Background and context

The U.S. and China have been locked in a trade standoff since 2017, when President Trump imposed tariffs of 25 % on $34 billion of Chinese goods and China retaliated with equivalent tariffs on U.S. imports [3]. The feud escalated into a full‑blown tariff war, with both sides adding hundreds of billions of dollars in duties over the next year. In August 2018, the two countries signed a “Phase One” trade agreement, which temporarily eased some tariffs and committed China to increase purchases of U.S. agricultural products [4]. However, the deal left many issues unresolved, including intellectual‑property protection, technology transfer, and market access for U.S. firms.

The current round of negotiations has focused on a handful of key issues. First, the U.S. continues to press China to curb technology transfers that could undermine American competitiveness, particularly in high‑tech sectors such as semiconductors and artificial‑intelligence hardware. Second, the U.S. seeks clearer rules for market access, arguing that Chinese companies face uneven regulatory hurdles when operating in the United States. Third, both sides are negotiating the terms of a broader tariff schedule, with the U.S. hoping to reduce duties on Chinese goods while maintaining pressure on Chinese firms that rely on U.S. technology.

Historically, market reactions to U.S.‑China trade announcements have been swift. When the Phase One deal was signed in August 2018, the S&P 500 surged 2.80 % in the first trading day, while the Shanghai Composite rose 1.10 % [5]. Conversely, when President Trump announced a new wave of tariffs in July 2018, the U.S. market fell 1.50 % and the Shanghai Composite slipped 0.90 % [6]. These patterns underscore the sensitivity of global investors to any hint of policy change between the two economic giants.

Diverse perspectives

U.S. corporations

Large U.S. technology firms such as Apple, Microsoft, and Nvidia have long been wary of Chinese market‑access restrictions. Many of these companies have shifted some production to Southeast Asia to mitigate supply‑chain risks. The “positive” tone in Lighthizer’s statement has been welcomed by corporate executives who see it as a potential easing of pressure on their supply chains. In a briefing to investors, a spokesperson for a leading semiconductor manufacturer noted that the company was “monitoring the developments closely and remains optimistic about the prospects for a more stable trade environment” [7].

Chinese exporters

Chinese manufacturers, especially those in the electronics and consumer‑goods sectors, have been hit hard by U.S. tariffs. The Shanghai Composite’s modest rise suggests that some investors see the statement as a signal that tariffs may be eased. A representative from a major Chinese electronics firm said that the company “remains hopeful but cautious” and that it will continue to diversify its markets [8].

Policymakers and economists

U.S. trade officials have expressed cautious optimism, noting that the statement reflects progress in negotiations. However, economists warn that the absence of concrete policy changes means that the markets may have over‑reacted. “A headline can move the markets, but it is the details that matter,” said a senior economist at the Brookings Institution [9].

In China, officials framed the statement as evidence of the “mutual respect” between the two sides. A spokesperson for the Ministry of Commerce said that the U.S. has “shown a willingness to engage in constructive dialogue” and that China is “prepared to make further concessions.” Yet analysts point out that China’s domestic politics and the need to protect its strategic industries may limit the scope of concessions it can offer [10].

Global investors

International investors, particularly those with exposure to both U.S. and Chinese markets, have responded with a mix of optimism and caution. Many funds have increased their holdings in U.S. technology stocks, while others have shifted capital into Chinese consumer‑discretionary shares. The MSCI World Index’s modest gain reflects a broader sentiment that the trade standoff may be easing, but the volatility remains high [11].

What to watch

Concrete policy announcements

The next key development will be the official policy statements that follow the Trump‑Xi summit. Investors will be looking for any tariff reductions, changes in technology‑transfer rules, or new market‑access agreements. The absence of such announcements could lead to a pullback in the markets, as the initial optimism may have been based on speculation rather than policy [12].

Supply‑chain realignments

If the U.S. and China agree to reduce tariffs, many U.S. firms may accelerate their plans to shift production out of China. This could have ripple effects across the global supply chain, potentially boosting manufacturing in countries such as Vietnam, India, and Mexico. However, the transition will be costly and time‑consuming, and some firms may choose to maintain a presence in China if the trade environment improves [13].

Conclusion

Lighthizer’s brief statement on April 11, 2018, offered a glimmer of hope amid a tense U.S.–China trade dispute. While the markets responded with a modest rally, the real test lies in the outcomes of the April 12–13 summit and the subsequent Phase One agreement. Investors, corporations, and policymakers will be watching closely for concrete policy shifts that can transform the symbolic progress into tangible economic benefits.
