Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

By Yahoo Finance · Sep 19, 2026
A headline that Intel’s chief executive had a successful pre‑summit conversation with Chinese officials sparked a brief rally in U.S. equity markets on Wednesday morning, but neither the U.S. nor Chinese governments have confirmed the claim, leaving investors uncertain about its veracity.¹ Bloomberg ran the story at 9:15 a.m. EDT. The market reacted almost immediately. In the first hour, the S&P 500 climbed 1.2 %, the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 0.9 %, and the Nasdaq Composite gained 1.5 %, according to a Reuters snapshot of the day’s performance.² Technology and industrial stocks led the gains. The semiconductor index rose 3 points, and the materials index climbed 2 %. The rally was short‑lived; by market close the indices had largely retraced, and the risk‑on sentiment that had briefly taken hold faded as analysts noted the absence of any official corroboration.³ Despite the headline’s immediacy, no statement from the U.S. Trade Representative, the Treasury Department, or the White House has confirmed that any substantive progress was made. Likewise, China’s Ministry of Commerce issued a brief statement that day, emphasizing the country’s commitment to a fair and reciprocal trade relationship but offering no mention of Intel or pre‑summit talks.⁴ The lack of confirmation from both sides has turned the market’s reaction into a speculative exercise rather than a response to verifiable policy developments. The backdrop to this episode is the protracted U.S.–China trade dispute that began in 2018, when the Trump administration imposed tariffs on billions of dollars of Chinese goods. The tariffs were rolled out in phases, with the most aggressive measures introduced in 2019. The 2019 “phase‑1” trade deal, signed in January 2020, temporarily eased some tariff rates and included commitments from China to increase purchases of U.S. goods, but many of those commitments were not fully met, and the trade war continued in various forms.⁵ The U.S. still levies 25 % tariffs on steel imports and 10 % on aluminum, while agricultural products such as soybeans and pork face tariffs ranging from 15 % to 25 %.⁶ In 2023, the U.S. announced additional tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles and battery components, adding 25 % to the existing 25 % tariff on EVs and 10 % to battery components.⁷ The 2024 “phase‑2” negotiations aim to address broader issues such as intellectual property, market access, and technology transfer.⁸ Intel’s supply chain is heavily integrated with Chinese manufacturers. The company sources a significant portion of its advanced packaging materials and certain raw materials from China, and it relies on Chinese firms for some assembly and testing services. A reduction in tariffs could lower the cost of components, improve margins, and reduce supply‑chain risk for U.S. technology firms.¹⁰ However, the market’s reaction remains speculative in the absence of official confirmation. The brief rally’s dynamics illustrate the sensitivity of equity markets to trade‑policy headlines. Reuters analysts noted that the initial surge reflected a risk‑on bias, as investors priced in the possibility of tariff relief and a smoother supply chain. The subsequent pullback was consistent with a risk‑off shift once the lack of confirmation became apparent.¹¹ Such short‑lived movements are not uncommon when markets react to unverified information, especially on high‑stakes issues like U.S.–China trade. A deeper economic analysis reveals that the impact of any tariff relief would be uneven across the supply‑chain network. Key nodes include semiconductor fabs in China, which provide advanced packaging and testing services; raw‑material suppliers for silicon, copper, and rare earth elements; and logistics hubs that handle the movement of components between China and the United States. The Semiconductor Industry Association’s 2023 report estimates that U.S. firms spend roughly $30 billion annually on services provided by Chinese suppliers, a figure that could shrink if tariffs are lowered.¹² A reduction in tariffs on steel and aluminum would also lower input costs for manufacturers of automotive and aerospace components, potentially boosting profitability in those sectors.¹³ On the macro‑financial front, any credible indication of tariff easing could influence expectations for inflation and monetary policy. The Federal Reserve’s recent policy statement signaled that it would keep policy rates elevated until inflation returns to its 2 % target, but a reduction in trade friction could dampen import‑price pressures and support the Fed’s inflation outlook.¹⁴ Moreover, a smoother supply chain could mitigate the risk of global disruptions that have contributed to recent volatility in commodity prices and exchange rates.¹⁵ Investors must also consider the risk of a false‑positive rally. MarketWatch commentary highlighted that speculative trading on unverified headlines can create temporary price distortions that may not reflect underlying fundamentals.¹⁶ In the absence of a formal joint statement from the U.S. and Chinese governments, the rally could be an example of “noise” driving “noise,” with traders quickly re‑evaluating their positions once the initial excitement subsided. The upcoming Trump‑Xi summit, scheduled for later this month, remains the decisive event that could confirm or refute the implications of the headline. A White House press release on the summit’s agenda emphasizes the need to address tariff rates on steel, aluminum, and agriculture, as well as intellectual‑property protection and market access for Chinese technology firms.¹⁷ The outcome of the summit will likely determine whether the market’s brief optimism translates into concrete policy changes. Sectors most exposed to Chinese tariffs—steel, aluminum, and agriculture—stand to benefit the most if tariff relief materializes. Conversely, if the talks do not lead to meaningful changes, these sectors may face continued pressure, and technology firms that rely on Chinese supply chains could be forced to accelerate diversification or invest in domestic manufacturing capacity. Bloomberg’s recent analysis of steel and agriculture tariffs indicates that a 10 % reduction could lift the U.S. steel industry’s profit margins by 2 % to 3 %, while a similar easing for soybean imports could reduce U.S. farmers’ costs by 1 % to 2 %.¹⁸ Investor sentiment and risk appetite will play a critical role in determining the rally’s longevity. Macro‑economic conditions such as U.S. interest rates, inflation expectations, and global supply‑chain disruptions will shape risk‑off versus risk‑on dynamics. If the market perceives that the trade dispute is moving toward resolution, risk appetite could increase, leading to further gains in equities. Conversely, a failure to deliver substantive results at the summit could trigger a reversion to a risk‑off stance, pulling back the gains seen in the immediate aftermath of the headline.¹⁹ In summary, the headline claiming that Intel’s CEO had a successful pre‑summit conversation with China sparked a brief, speculative rally in U.S. equity markets. The rally reflected investor optimism about potential tariff relief and supply‑chain easing, but it remains unverified by any official source. The outcome of the upcoming Trump‑Xi summit will be the decisive factor in determining whether the market’s enthusiasm translates into concrete policy changes. Until official confirmation arrives, investors should treat the rally as a speculative reaction to unverified information rather than a definitive signal of a shift in U.S.–China trade policy.
