Private spacecraft falls back to Earth after failing to rescue aging NASA telescope

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
On May 5, 2023, SpaceX’s Starship prototype SN15 crashed during a high‑altitude test flight, a dramatic reminder that the commercial space industry still faces significant technical hurdles even as it seeks to take on tasks traditionally handled by NASA’s own crews. The incident, which involved a vehicle designed to reach 10 km altitude before a controlled descent, raised fresh questions about whether private companies will be ready to perform complex in‑orbit servicing of aging NASA telescopes such as the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) or future observatories.

What happened

SN15 lifted off from the SpaceX launch site at Boca Chica, Texas, and reached an altitude of roughly 10 km before a planned parachute deployment. Shortly after the parachute opened, telemetry indicated a loss of attitude control; the vehicle spun uncontrollably and crashed into the Gulf of Mexico. The impact was captured on a handful of ground‑based cameras and on the onboard video feed that was recovered from the wreckage. SpaceX’s flight operations team released a brief statement confirming the loss of the vehicle, while NASA’s Johnson Space Center, which had been monitoring the flight as part of a joint test program, issued a separate notice acknowledging the crash and expressing regret over the loss of a key prototype.

The preliminary investigation, led by SpaceX’s engineering team with input from NASA’s flight safety office, identified a software error in the vehicle’s guidance system that caused a mis‑execution of the descent burn. The error was traced to a recent code change that had not been fully validated in the simulation environment. The incident has prompted a review of the company’s software verification processes and a broader discussion about the readiness of commercial spacecraft to undertake high‑risk missions such as servicing a telescope in orbit.

The broader context

NASA’s history of in‑orbit servicing is most famously illustrated by the four Hubble servicing missions that took place between 1997 and 2009. These missions, conducted by the Space Shuttle, repaired and upgraded the telescope’s instruments, extending its life and scientific output by more than two decades. The success of those missions demonstrated that complex repairs could be performed in space, but they also highlighted the high cost and logistical complexity of crewed operations.

In the past decade, NASA has increasingly turned to commercial partners for launch, satellite deployment, and even on‑orbit servicing. The Commercial Crew Program, the Commercial Lunar Payload Services program, and the more recent Commercial Orbital Launch Services (COLSS) contracts all reflect a strategic shift aimed at reducing costs and leveraging private sector innovation. Companies such as SpaceX, Blue Origin, and emerging providers like Astrobotic and Intuitive Machines have secured contracts to deliver payloads to the International Space Station (ISS) and to the lunar surface.

The prospect of a private company performing a servicing mission on HST or a future telescope has generated both excitement and concern. Proponents argue that a commercial approach could reduce the cost of a servicing mission from the estimated \$400 million for a crewed Shuttle mission to a fraction of that figure, while also allowing for more rapid iteration and deployment. Critics, however, point to the risks of relying on a private entity whose primary incentive is profit rather than scientific continuity. The SN15 crash underscores the need for rigorous oversight and robust risk management when commercial partners are tasked with missions that have high scientific stakes.

Commercial model: benefits and risks

The commercial model offers several clear advantages. First, launch costs have fallen dramatically: SpaceX’s Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets are now priced at roughly one‑third of the cost of a comparable Atlas V or Delta IV launch, a savings that translates directly into lower mission budgets. Second, the private sector’s rapid development cycles allow for quicker prototyping and deployment of new technologies. Third, the competitive environment encourages innovation, as companies vie for lucrative government contracts.

However, these benefits come with significant risks. Commercial operators often have tighter schedules and higher profit margins, which can lead to compressed testing timelines and reduced safety margins. The SN15 crash illustrates how a software bug that might have been caught in a more extensive simulation environment can have catastrophic consequences. Moreover, the contractual relationship between NASA and a private partner typically includes risk‑sharing clauses that can shift liability for failures onto the agency, raising questions about insurance coverage and financial exposure.

NASA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation has issued guidance on risk management for commercial launch services, but the agency is still developing policies that specifically address the unique challenges of in‑orbit servicing. For example, the agency has begun to require that commercial partners submit detailed risk assessments and contingency plans for each mission, but the standards for what constitutes an acceptable risk level remain a subject of debate.

The case of Hubble and other aging telescopes

The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, has been a cornerstone of astrophysical research for more than three decades. Its instruments have produced images of distant galaxies, supernovae, and exoplanetary atmospheres that have led to numerous Nobel‑winning discoveries. While HST is still in operation, its aging instruments and the increasing demand for high‑resolution imaging underscore the urgency of developing reliable servicing solutions. NASA has also identified other aging observatories—such as the Chandra X‑ray Observatory, the Spitzer Space Telescope, and the Kepler mission—whose continued operation would benefit from in‑orbit repair or replacement of key components.

Looking ahead

NASA’s next steps involve a careful assessment of the commercial servicing landscape. The agency is evaluating proposals from several companies that have outlined plans to deliver robotic servicing vehicles capable of rendezvous, docking, and tool deployment. These concepts range from autonomous robotic arms that can replace optical elements to small, modular spacecraft that can deliver replacement instruments. Each proposal must demonstrate not only technical feasibility but also a clear pathway to compliance with NASA’s stringent safety and reliability standards.

At the same time, NASA is refining its own internal procedures for oversight. The agency is expanding its Commercial Space Transportation Office’s portfolio to include dedicated roles for in‑orbit servicing, and it is working closely with the Federal Aviation Administration and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s Office of Safety and Mission Assurance to align regulatory frameworks.

Conclusion

The SN15 crash serves as a sober reminder that commercial innovation does not automatically translate into flawless execution. Yet it also highlights the potential for private enterprise to accelerate the development of technologies that could extend the life of critical scientific assets. As NASA and its commercial partners navigate this evolving partnership, the focus will remain on balancing cost, speed, and safety to ensure that humanity’s most valuable telescopes continue to illuminate the universe for generations to come.
