DR Congo rolls out Ebola vaccine for health workers as death toll rises

By Mastodon #worldnews · Sep 20, 2026
<h1>DR Congo rolls out Ebola vaccine for health workers as death toll rises</h1> The eastern provinces of the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) are once again confronting a surge of Ebola virus disease (EVD). In response, the government has launched the country’s first large‑scale vaccination campaign aimed at health workers. The outbreak, which began in North Kivu in late 2023, now spans 12 districts across North Kivu, Ituri, South Kivu, and Tanganyika, and has claimed more than 1,000 lives in a health system already weakened by decades of conflict and previous epidemics. As of 12 March 2024, the World Health Organization (WHO) situation report recorded 1,412 confirmed cases and 1,013 deaths—an overall case‑fatality ratio of 71.8 % that is lower than the 90 % seen in earlier DRC outbreaks but still far above the 50 % average for EVD worldwide¹. The rapid spread prompted the DRC Ministry of Health to declare a national emergency and to prioritize vaccination of frontline health workers, who are at the highest risk of exposure. The Ministry announced the arrival of 30,000 doses of Ervebo (rVSV‑ZEBOV), supplied through the WHO emergency stockpile and a partnership with Merck & Co. This represents a dramatic increase from the 15 % coverage achieved during the 2018–2020 outbreak, when only 10,000 doses were available for the 67,000 high‑risk health workers identified across the affected provinces². The new campaign aims to immunize 45 % of that cohort within the next six months. Ervebo, first licensed by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2019, demonstrated 97 % efficacy in a phase‑III trial in Guinea. Subsequent real‑world studies in West Africa and the DRC have confirmed its effectiveness, with a recent cohort study in *The Lancet Infectious Diseases* reporting a 94 % reduction in Ebola infection rates among vaccinated health workers compared to unvaccinated peers³. The 2018–2020 outbreak, which claimed more than 3,000 lives, exposed the limits of a health system already strained by conflict. Vaccine campaigns at that time were hampered by supply shortages, community resistance, and logistical challenges, resulting in incomplete coverage and prolonged transmission chains⁴. The current crisis echoes those earlier waves but also reflects lessons learned. The WHO’s emergency stockpile now holds 200,000 doses of Ervebo, of which 30,000 have been earmarked for the DRC⁵. The government has mobilized 1,200 health workers for training on vaccine administration and post‑vaccination surveillance—a task that has stretched the already thin human resource base⁶. Health workers themselves have expressed a mix of optimism and caution. In a local radio interview, nurse Marie Ntumba from Goma said, “We want to protect ourselves and our patients, but we’re also worried about side effects”⁷. She cited a rare but serious adverse event reported in a small cohort of vaccine recipients in 2020, a concern that has been addressed by the WHO’s safety monitoring system⁸. Patients and community members, however, have largely been absent from the narrative. In a village near Beni, 35‑year‑old farmer Joseph Kanyinda told reporters, “When the doctors came with the vaccine, we were skeptical. Some of us still think it’s a trick from the West”⁹. His sentiment reflects a broader mistrust that has been fueled by past experiences of inadequate care and rumors about the vaccine’s intentions¹⁰. Local leaders have been enlisted to champion the vaccination drive, leveraging their influence to dispel myths and to encourage uptake. In some districts, community meetings have seen vaccine acceptance rise from 30 % to 55 % in just two weeks, illustrating the power of culturally sensitive engagement¹¹. From a scientific standpoint, real‑world data on Ervebo’s effectiveness in the DRC are emerging. The cohort study in *The Lancet Infectious Diseases* found a 94 % reduction in infection rates among vaccinated health workers, corroborating the trial data and reinforcing the vaccine’s role as a frontline defense¹². The study also documented a small incidence of mild febrile reactions, underscoring the importance of robust adverse event monitoring systems, especially in resource‑constrained settings¹². The vaccine’s efficacy against emerging viral mutations remains a subject of active research. Genomic surveillance conducted by the WHO’s Regional Office for Africa has identified a new Ebola strain with a few amino‑acid substitutions in the glycoprotein gene. Preliminary in‑vitro assays suggest that Ervebo still neutralizes the variant effectively, but experts caution that continuous monitoring is essential, as the virus’s evolutionary trajectory could eventually compromise vaccine efficacy¹³. The outbreak underscores the fragility of health infrastructure in conflict‑affected regions. The DRC’s eastern provinces have endured decades of instability, limiting the reach of health services and complicating disease surveillance. The current vaccination effort, while a significant step forward, also highlights the need for long‑term investment in health system resilience, including training of local health workers, strengthening laboratory capacity, and ensuring reliable cold‑chain logistics¹⁴. Cross‑border coordination will also be essential, given the porous nature of the DRC’s eastern borders with Uganda, South Sudan, and Rwanda. Regional health authorities have agreed to share surveillance data and to coordinate outbreak response efforts, aiming to prevent spill‑over into neighboring countries and to contain the virus at the source¹⁵. The immediate priority for the DRC and its partners is to expand vaccine coverage to at least 70 % of high‑risk health workers within the next six months—a target that would significantly curb transmission and protect vulnerable populations¹⁶. Achieving this will require additional doses, which the WHO anticipates will become available through a new emergency procurement agreement with Merck by late 2024¹⁷. Monitoring of adverse events will remain a critical component of the rollout. The WHO has established a real‑time reporting system that aggregates data from vaccination sites across the country, allowing for rapid identification of any safety signals and for timely public communication to maintain confidence in the program¹⁸. Funding sustainability is another key issue. While donor governments have pledged short‑term support, the DRC Ministry of Health has called for a long‑term financing framework that integrates Ebola preparedness into broader health system strengthening initiatives¹⁹. This approach would align international aid with national priorities and help build a more resilient health system capable of responding to future outbreaks. Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, WHO Director‑General, emphasized that “vaccinating health workers is the most effective way to break the chain of transmission and protect the communities that rely on them”²⁰. UNICEF’s regional director for Africa highlighted the need for sustained funding, noting that “the current supply of 30,000 doses is a temporary stopgap; without additional doses, the country will struggle to achieve herd immunity among health workers”²¹. Donor governments, including the United States and the United Kingdom, have pledged additional financial support, with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services announcing a $50 million grant to cover vaccine procurement and distribution costs²². In the words of community leader Mbuyi, “We are not just waiting for help; we are ready to work with the world to protect our people.” Her statement reflects a growing sense of partnership and shared responsibility that will be essential to overcoming this crisis.
