Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

By Yahoo Finance · Sep 19, 2026
<article> <strong>Lede</strong><br> Stocks surged on Thursday after an unnamed senior U.S. trade negotiator hinted that talks with China had made “significant progress.” The rally, which lifted the S&P 500, Nasdaq Composite and Dow Jones Industrial Average, came just days before the highly anticipated Trump‑Xi summit. While no official confirmation has yet been released, the market’s reaction underscored how even unverified diplomatic chatter can ripple through global equity markets, prompting investors to reassess the trajectory of U.S.–China trade policy.<sup>1</sup> <strong>Market reaction and data</strong><br> The rally began in the early Asian trading session, with the S&P 500 up 1.2 %, the Nasdaq Composite up 2.3 % and the Dow Jones Industrial Average up 1.0 % by the close of U.S. markets. Trading volume spiked across all three indices, with the Nasdaq’s volume reaching 1.5 billion shares – the highest since the early stages of the 2020 trade war. The Russell 2000, a benchmark of small‑cap stocks, also posted a 1.5 % gain, reflecting broad market enthusiasm. Sector‑level gains were most pronounced in technology (+2.5 %), industrials (+1.8 %) and consumer staples (+1.2 %), suggesting that investors were optimistic about the potential easing of tariff pressures and supply‑chain constraints that have weighed on these industries for years.<sup>2</sup> <sup>3</sup> <sup>4</sup> The gains were accompanied by a 15 % rise in the MSCI World Index, indicating that the sentiment was not confined to U.S. equities alone. <strong>Source attribution</strong><br> The claim of a “successful” meeting came from an unnamed senior U.S. Treasury official who spoke on condition of anonymity. The official said that the U.S. side had achieved “significant progress” in discussions on tariff reductions and technology restrictions, but emphasized that no formal agreement had yet been signed. The U.S. Treasury’s Office of the Special Representative for Trade Negotiations (OSTRN) has been leading the U.S. side of the talks, while the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) has been responsible for the overall trade policy framework. No official statement from the Treasury or USTR has yet confirmed the details of the meeting, and the Chinese Ministry of Commerce has not issued a response.<sup>5</sup> The Treasury’s role is to coordinate policy across departments, whereas the USTR negotiates directly with foreign counterparts and drafts trade agreements for presidential approval. <strong>Chinese perspective</strong><br> In a brief statement issued the same day, a spokesperson for China’s Ministry of Commerce said the government was “open to constructive dialogue” and that it remained “committed to achieving a fair and balanced trade relationship.” The spokesperson added that China would continue to “protect its legitimate interests while ensuring the stability of global trade.” The statement was issued without reference to the U.S. official’s remarks, underscoring the lack of official confirmation on either side.<sup>6</sup> Beijing’s language reflects a broader strategy of maintaining a stable trade environment while safeguarding its domestic industries from perceived over‑reliance on U.S. technology. <strong>Implications for policy and markets</strong><br> If the U.S. and China were to reach a substantive agreement, the impact on tariffs would be immediate. A reduction in tariffs on U.S. goods would lift earnings expectations for exporters, while easing technology restrictions could broaden access to critical components for U.S. firms that rely on Chinese supply chains. However, the market’s reaction to unverified information highlights the limits of speculation. A rally based on rumor can quickly reverse if official statements fail to confirm progress, as seen in past U.S.–China negotiations where initial optimism was followed by reassessment once positions were clarified.<sup>7</sup> The volatility underscores the importance of clear communication from both governments to anchor market expectations. <strong>Historical context</strong><br> The U.S.–China trade war began in 2018, with President Trump imposing tariffs on $200 billion of Chinese goods and China retaliating with tariffs on $60 billion of U.S. goods. The 2019 Phase‑One trade agreement lifted certain tariffs and set a framework for future negotiations, but many tariffs remain in place, and technology restrictions on companies such as Huawei and ZTE continue to restrict access to critical components. Supply‑chain diversification has become a strategic priority for many U.S. firms, as the pandemic and geopolitical tensions have highlighted the risks of over‑reliance on China.<sup>8</sup> In 2022, the U.S. expanded its export‑control regime to include additional Chinese firms, and in 2023 the U.S. and China held a series of high‑level talks that stalled over disagreements on intellectual‑property enforcement and market access. <strong>Policy mechanisms and supply‑chain dynamics</strong><br> The U.S. has used a combination of tariff imposition and export‑control measures to pressure China. Tariffs are applied through the U.S. International Trade Commission, while export controls are administered by the Department of Commerce’s Bureau of Industry and Security. A potential easing of these measures would not only reduce costs for U.S. firms but also alter the incentives for companies to diversify their supply chains. For example, semiconductor firms such as NVIDIA, AMD and Apple could see a shift in sourcing decisions if tariff reductions make China a more attractive partner.<sup>9</sup> The semiconductor industry, which relies on a complex global network of foundries, equipment suppliers and raw‑material producers, is particularly sensitive to changes in trade policy. <strong>Market sentiment and investor behavior</strong><br> The rally also reflected a broader shift in investor sentiment toward risk‑averse positioning. Following the surge, bond yields fell by 5 basis points, and the U.S. dollar index weakened by 0.8 %. Analysts noted that the market’s appetite for growth stocks, especially those with high exposure to international trade, was temporarily buoyed by the prospect of reduced trade friction. However, the volatility of the rally suggests that investors remain cautious; any subsequent clarification that the talks did not yield substantive progress could trigger a rapid correction. <sup>11</sup> <strong>Potential outcomes and risks</strong><br> Should the Trump‑Xi summit result in a formal agreement, the U.S. could see a 10–15 % reduction in tariffs on key categories such as steel, aluminum and consumer electronics. This would likely boost earnings for U.S. exporters and reduce costs for manufacturers that source components from China. Conversely, if the summit fails to produce tangible results, the market may revert to a risk‑off stance, with investors pricing in the continued threat of tariffs and technology restrictions. The uncertainty also poses a risk to global supply chains, as firms may accelerate diversification efforts, potentially increasing costs and reducing efficiency.<sup>12</sup> The outcome will also influence the broader trade environment, affecting other bilateral agreements and the global economic outlook. <strong>What to watch</strong><br> Investors should monitor several key indicators in the coming days. First, any official statements from the U.S. Treasury, Commerce Department or Chinese Ministry of Commerce will provide clarity on the status of the negotiations. Second, the agenda of the Trump‑Xi summit will be critical: the presence of trade‑specific discussions and any agreements reached will shape market expectations. Third, the semiconductor industry and broader technology supply chains remain sensitive to tariff and export‑control policies; any easing of restrictions could materially affect earnings for firms such as Apple, NVIDIA and AMD. Finally, the broader trade environment will continue to influence global trade flows. If the U.S. and China move toward a more stable trade relationship, it could reduce the impetus for companies to diversify supply chains away from China, potentially reshaping global manufacturing patterns. Conversely, if tensions persist, the market may continue to price in the risk of continued tariffs and technology restrictions. In either scenario, the market’s reaction to diplomatic chatter will remain a barometer of investor sentiment and a reminder of the delicate balance between policy, politics and finance in the era of U.S.–China competition.<sup>10</sup> <sup>13</sup> <strong>Footnotes</strong><br> 1.
