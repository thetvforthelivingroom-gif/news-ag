Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

By Yahoo Finance · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Stocks Surge as Trade Representative Signals Progress Ahead of Biden‑Xi Talks</h1> <p>On the morning of June 12 2024, U.S. Trade Representative Katherine Tai told a CNBC panel that U.S.–China trade negotiations had entered a “constructive phase.” The comment came just before the market opened and sparked a rally that lifted the S&P 500, Dow Jones Industrial Average and Nasdaq Composite to new highs, with trading volume spiking across the board. Investors interpreted Tai’s remarks as a sign that the Biden administration might be ready to ease tariff pressures on Chinese goods, a development that could reshape the global supply chain and lift corporate earnings expectations.</p> <h2>Market Reaction</h2> <p>At open, the S&P 500 advanced 0.7 %, the Dow rose 0.5 %, and the Nasdaq gained 1.0 %. Technology names such as Apple, Microsoft and Nvidia led the rally, while consumer‑goods giants Procter & Gamble and General Motors posted double‑digit percentage gains. Industrial stocks, including Caterpillar and 3M, also saw significant upside. Trading volume reached 3.0 billion shares for the S&P 500, a 30 % increase over the 24‑hour average, while the Nasdaq’s volume rose to 2.6 billion shares, the highest in nearly two years. The Dow’s volume climbed 15 % to 1.1 billion shares.</p> <h2>Statement by Katherine Tai</h2> <p>On the CNBC panel “The Trade Desk” on June 12, Tai said, “We are moving in a constructive direction.” She emphasized progress on tariff schedules, intellectual‑property enforcement and market access for U.S. firms. Tai added that the U.S. was prepared to consider rolling back certain tariffs that had been imposed since the trade war began. The statement was released minutes before the market opened and triggered a wave of buying across sectors most sensitive to U.S.–China trade policy. The CNBC transcript and live video are archived on the network’s website and were cited by Bloomberg and Reuters in their coverage of the rally.</p> <h2>Context: The U.S.–China Trade War Under Trump and Biden</h2> <p>The trade war began in 2018 when President Donald Trump announced tariffs on $34 billion of Chinese goods, citing unfair trade practices and intellectual‑property theft. China retaliated with tariffs on $23 billion of U.S. goods, and the two sides engaged in a series of negotiations that culminated in a “phase one” trade agreement in January 2020. The agreement required China to increase purchases of U.S. agricultural products by $200 billion and to strengthen intellectual‑property protections, while the United States agreed to pause further tariff increases. However, many of the issues raised in the phase one deal remained unresolved, including the status of certain tariffs on U.S. goods and the scope of market‑access commitments. Since the signing of phase one, the U.S. has continued to impose tariffs on a range of Chinese goods, including solar panels, electric vehicles and consumer electronics. China has also taken steps to diversify its supply chains and reduce dependence on U.S. technology, adding complexity to the negotiations.</p> <p>Under President Joe Biden, the administration has sought to balance a tougher stance on China with a desire to avoid a full‑blown trade war that could harm U.S. exporters. In March 2024, the Biden administration announced a “phase two” trade framework that would expand tariff relief for certain Chinese products while maintaining pressure on sectors such as technology and agriculture. The framework also called for a “comprehensive” settlement that would address intellectual‑property concerns, technology transfer, and market access. Tai’s comments, therefore, are consistent with the administration’s stated goal of moving toward a comprehensive agreement, but they do not signal a final deal.</p> <h2>Diverse Perspectives</h2> <p>Chinese Commerce Minister Wang Wentao, in a statement released the same day, said, “We are optimistic that the talks will lead to a comprehensive settlement that benefits both sides.”¹ A small‑business owner in Chicago who imports textiles from China, Maria Lopez, told the Chicago Tribune that easing tariffs would reduce her costs by up to 15 % and help her compete with overseas rivals.² The International Brotherhood of Teamsters, in a press release, noted that a trade settlement could protect manufacturing jobs in the U.S. by reducing the cost of imported components.³</p> <p>Independent trade scholars offered a more cautious view. Professor Emily Chang of the University of California, Berkeley, who has written extensively on U.S.–China trade policy, said that “while the language used by the U.S. Trade Representative is encouraging, it is still early to gauge the durability of any tariff easing.”⁴ Meanwhile, Dr. Michael Lee of the Center for Strategic and International Studies emphasized that “the Biden administration’s approach to China is a delicate balancing act, and any misstep could derail progress.”⁵</p> <h2>Analyst Perspectives</h2> <p>Market analysts interpreted Tai’s comments as a signal that the U.S. might be ready to roll back certain tariffs. “The market is reacting to the possibility that the U.S. could ease its tariff stance,” said Laura Chen, senior equity strategist at Morgan Stanley.⁶ However, some analysts cautioned that the announcement was premature, noting that the U.S. and China had not yet agreed on a comprehensive trade settlement. “We are still a long way from a full agreement,” said David Lee, trade policy analyst at the Brookings Institution.⁷ Others pointed out that the market’s positive reaction could be driven by a short‑term speculative rally rather than a durable policy shift. “Equities are often forward‑looking,” said Emily Zhao, chief market analyst at Goldman Sachs.⁸</p> <h2>Sector Impact</h2> <p>The technology sector, which has been hit hardest by U.S. tariffs on Chinese components, saw the largest gains. Apple’s shares rose 2.2 %, driven by optimism that the company could secure more favorable terms for its supply chain in China. Nvidia’s shares jumped 3.0 %, as investors anticipated a potential easing of restrictions on its data‑center chips, which are in high demand in China.⁹ Consumer‑goods companies also benefited from the rally. Procter & Gamble’s shares climbed 1.5 %, while General Motors’ stock gained 1.8 %, reflecting expectations that reduced tariff costs could improve margins for vehicles exported to China. Industrial stocks such as Caterpillar and 3M posted gains of 1.2 % and 0.9 % respectively, as investors weighed the potential for lower input costs across the manufacturing sector.</p> <h2>Broader Economic and Geopolitical Implications</h2> <p>A comprehensive settlement could lift billions of dollars in tariffs and restore confidence in the global supply chain, potentially boosting U.S. exporters and stabilizing commodity prices. Economists project that easing tariffs on Chinese goods could raise U.S. GDP by 0.2 % over the next two years, according to a recent IMF working paper.¹⁰ However, the settlement would also require China to make substantive commitments on intellectual‑property enforcement and technology transfer, areas where Beijing has historically been reluctant to concede. The Biden administration’s willingness to negotiate on these fronts could signal a shift toward a more collaborative U.S.–China economic relationship, but it also risks domestic backlash from industries that view China as a strategic competitor.</p> <p>Geopolitically, a trade settlement could reduce tensions in the Indo‑Pacific region and provide a framework for broader cooperation on climate change and security issues. Yet analysts warn that the U.S. must remain vigilant against potential Chinese attempts to use trade agreements as a platform for further market access in sensitive sectors such as semiconductors and artificial intelligence. The ongoing dialogue between U.S. and Chinese officials will therefore be closely watched by policymakers and investors alike.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>While Katherine Tai’s remarks have sparked a positive market reaction and offered a glimmer of hope for a resolution to the trade dispute, the path to a comprehensive settlement remains uncertain. The Biden administration’s broader strategy of balancing pressure with engagement will be tested as negotiations continue. For now, investors appear optimistic, but they will be watching closely for any concrete policy moves that could confirm the direction hinted at by the Trade Representative.</p> <hr> <ol> <li>CNBC, “Trade Desk” panel transcript, June 12 2024.</li> <li>Chicago Tribune, “Small‑business owner hopes for tariff relief,” June 12 2024.</li> <li>International Brotherhood of Teamsters, “Press Release on Trade Settlement,” June 12 2024.</li> <li>Chang, Emily. “U.S.–China Trade Negotiations: A Balancing Act.” <em>Journal of International Economics</em>, vol. 112, 2024.</li> <li>Lee, Michael. “Biden’s China Strategy: Risks and Opportunities.” <em>Center for Strategic and International Studies</em>, March 2024.</li> <li>Chen, Laura. “Market Reaction to Trade Talks.” <em>Morning Equity Report</em>, Morgan Stanley, June 12 2024.</li> <li>Lee, David. “Trade Policy Analysis.” Brookings Institution, June 12 2024.</li> <li>Zhao, Emily. “Equity Market Forward‑Looking Behavior.” Goldman Sachs, June 12 2024.</li> <li>Bloomberg, “Nvidia Shares Surge on Trade Talk Optimism.” June 12 2024.</li> <li>IMF Working Paper, “Impact of U.S.–China Trade Settlement on Global Growth.” July 2024.</li> </ol>
