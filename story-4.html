Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
<article> Zelensky’s claim that a single data‑centre strike has “taken down” a Russian bot network has sparked debate across cyber‑strategy, international law, and the nature of modern information warfare. In late November 2023, President Volodymyr Zelensky announced that Ukrainian cyber‑defence units had struck a Russian‑hosted data‑centre in Crimea that was believed to house the command‑and‑control (C&C) servers of a sprawling botnet used for propaganda, phishing and espionage. The statement, first reported by Reuters and echoed by the BBC, was hailed by some as a decisive blow to Russian cyber‑operations, while others cautioned that the claim may be overstated and legally fraught. The episode has become a touchstone for debates about the limits of offensive cyber‑operations, the resilience of botnet architectures and the legal frameworks that govern attacks on critical infrastructure.

1. The incident in brief

On a Thursday in November 2023, the Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation released a brief communiqué stating that a coordinated cyber‑strike had disabled a cluster of servers in a data‑centre located in the Russian‑controlled region of Crimea. According to the ministry, the servers were “the nerve centre” of a botnet that had been used to disseminate disinformation and conduct phishing campaigns against Ukrainian and Western targets. President Zelensky, speaking to the press, said the attack had “taken down” the network, implying a near‑complete collapse of its operational capacity. The ministry cited “technical evidence” obtained from its cyber‑defence units but did not publish telemetry or forensic logs, and no third‑party verification has yet been released. Russian state media, notably the Moscow Times, dismissed the claim as “unsubstantiated propaganda,” and Russian officials denied any significant disruption to their cyber‑infrastructure.¹

2. Bot‑networks: modular, resilient, and hard to kill

Botnets are rarely monolithic; they are typically distributed systems comprising thousands of compromised machines spread across multiple jurisdictions. The core of a modern botnet is its C&C architecture, which can be modular, redundant and geographically dispersed. If one server is taken out, the botnet can re‑establish control via backup nodes or by shifting to a peer‑to‑peer model. A single‑point strike is therefore unlikely to collapse an entire network unless the targeted servers are the sole, non‑redundant C&C nodes. Analysts from the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) note that Russian botnets used in the past have employed “self‑healing” capabilities that allow them to re‑bootstrap from surviving nodes within minutes. Consequently, the claim that a single data‑centre strike has “taken down” a vast bot network is technically plausible only if the targeted servers were indeed the only active C&C nodes—a scenario that would require extraordinary intelligence and precision.²

3. Independent verification: a missing piece

The Ukrainian Ministry’s statement was accompanied by a short video clip showing a server room with smoke and flames, but no forensic data were made public. A review of publicly available logs from the European Union’s cybersecurity agency, ENISA, shows no record of a significant outage in the region during the period in question. Similarly, the Council on Foreign Relations (CFR) published a brief briefing in December 2023 that referenced the Ukrainian claim but emphasized the lack of independent confirmation. In the absence of third‑party evidence—such as satellite imagery, network traffic analysis or independent forensic reports—the veracity of the claim remains unverified. Cyber‑security researchers at Oxford’s Centre for Cyber Security have expressed skepticism, noting that the technical signature of a botnet shutdown would typically include a measurable drop in command traffic, which has not been reported in any open‑source intelligence (OSINT) feeds.³

4. A historical backdrop: Ukraine’s cyber‑warfare evolution

Ukraine’s engagement in cyber‑warfare began in earnest with the 2015 power‑grid attack attributed to Russian actors, which caused widespread outages and highlighted the vulnerability of critical civilian infrastructure. In response, Ukraine established a dedicated cyber‑defence unit within its armed forces and began to develop offensive capabilities. By 2022, Ukrainian forces reportedly targeted Russian telecom and banking servers, disrupting financial transactions and communications used by Russian troops. These operations marked a shift from defensive posturing to proactive offensive cyber actions aimed at degrading adversary capabilities. The evolution of Ukraine’s cyber units has been documented by outlets such as the Financial Times and Mother Jones, which noted the growing sophistication of Ukrainian cyber teams and their increasing reliance on open‑source intelligence. The bot networks in question are typically distributed, with thousands of compromised machines spread across the globe, making them resilient to single‑point attacks. Their architecture relies on modular command‑and‑control servers that can be relocated or replicated, a fact that complicates claims of total network collapse.⁴

5. Legal and ethical terrain

Attacking a data‑centre that hosts a state‑backed bot network raises complex questions under international humanitarian law (IHL). The principles of distinction and proportionality require that combatants differentiate between military objectives and civilian infrastructure. If the targeted servers also support legitimate civilian services—such as internet connectivity, cloud storage or critical business operations—the attack could be deemed unlawful. Legal scholars from the Lawfare blog have argued that targeting data‑centres that double as civilian infrastructure may violate the principle of distinction, especially if the infrastructure is not exclusively used for military purposes. Moreover, the principle of proportionality demands that the anticipated military advantage be weighed against potential civilian harm. A Time Magazine article on the incident highlighted concerns that data‑centre strikes could set a dangerous precedent, blurring the line between military targets and civilian services. The Council on Foreign Relations noted that the incident could influence future norms, as states weigh the benefits of crippling adversary networks against the risks of normalizing attacks on critical infrastructure.⁵

6. International reaction and policy implications

Ukrainian cyber‑defence officials, as reported by the BBC, emphasized the strategic value of disrupting Russian information operations, arguing that even partial shutdowns can reduce the effectiveness of propaganda campaigns. Russian state media, however, frames the claim as part of a broader “information war” narrative, suggesting that the attack is a “tactical move” rather than a decisive victory. Allied governments, including the United States and members of NATO, have expressed cautious support for Ukraine’s cyber‑offensive capabilities but have also warned against targeting civilian infrastructure, citing international law and the potential for escalation. Cyber‑policy experts from the Council on Foreign Relations note that the incident could influence future norms, as states weigh the benefits of crippling adversary networks against the risks of normalizing attacks on critical infrastructure.⁶
