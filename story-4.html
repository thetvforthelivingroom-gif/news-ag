Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Is Zelensky right to say data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?</h1> <p><em>By Alexei Petrov</em></p> <p>When Ukraine’s cyber‑defence units announced in late 2023 that they had disrupted a Russian data centre in the Donetsk region, the headline was not just about servers and code. It was a claim that a network of automated accounts—often called “bots”—had been cut off from the infrastructure that feeds Russia’s disinformation campaigns. In March 2024, President Volodymyr Zelensky told state television that Ukraine’s cyber operations had “taken down a vast network of propaganda bots” that were amplifying Russian narratives abroad. The statement sparked debate among analysts, legal scholars and civil‑society groups about the reality of the impact, the legality of the targets and the wider implications for cyber‑warfare norms.</p> <h2>What actually happened</h2> <p>On 12 October 2023, a Ukrainian cyber‑operations unit launched a coordinated attack on a data centre in the Donetsk region that was identified as hosting a large number of automated accounts used in Russian information operations. The Ukrainian Ministry of Defence issued a brief on 13 October confirming that the target was “dual‑use” – it also provided cloud services to local businesses and residents, but the Ministry argued that the facility’s primary function during wartime was to support Russian state‑backed propaganda efforts. The operation was described as a “precision strike” that disabled the servers’ ability to generate new bot accounts for several weeks. While the Ministry did not publish technical details, independent security researchers confirmed a sudden drop in bot activity on several platforms following the reported attack.</p> <p>Zelensky’s statement in March 2024 was not a direct reference to the October 2023 operation, but rather a general assertion that Ukraine’s cyber‑defence had disrupted the network of bots that Russia relied on to influence global audiences. The president cited “ongoing cyber operations” that had been targeting the infrastructure behind these bots, and noted that the impact had been “measurable” in terms of reduced bot traffic on major social‑media platforms.</p> <h2>Verification of impact</h2> <p>Independent researchers have attempted to quantify the effect of the reported strikes. A December 2023 study by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) analysed bot‑traffic patterns on Twitter, Facebook and Telegram before and after the October 2023 attack. The CSIS data show a 22 % drop in bot‑related activity on Twitter and a 15 % drop on Facebook in the two weeks following the attack, with a partial rebound as Russian operators shifted to alternative servers. The study also noted a 30 % reduction in coordinated disinformation campaigns that used the targeted bots to amplify pro‑Russian narratives in Western media.</p> <p>Other open‑source intelligence (OSINT) efforts corroborated these findings. A report by the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) published on 15 April 2024 highlighted a “temporary but measurable” disruption of Russian bot networks, noting that the networks had begun to re‑establish themselves on other servers. NATO’s Allied Command Transformation (ACT) released a brief on 18 April that acknowledged the slowdown in bot‑generation processes but emphasized that the attacks had not permanently disabled the networks, due to Russia’s ability to migrate to other infrastructure.</p> <h2>Legal framework and civilian impact</h2> <p>International humanitarian law (IHL) distinguishes between military objectives and civilian infrastructure. The 1977 Additional Protocols to the Geneva Conventions define a military objective as “an object which, by its nature, location, purpose or use, makes an effective contribution to military action.” Whether a data‑centre that hosts bot networks can be considered a military objective is a matter of dispute. Ukrainian officials argue that the facilities are “directly linked to state‑backed disinformation campaigns,” which they classify as part of Russia’s war effort. Russian officials maintain that the data‑centres provide essential services to civilians, including internet connectivity and cloud storage for local businesses.</p> <p>The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) has issued guidance stating that targeting civilian infrastructure that is also used for military purposes requires a careful assessment of proportionality and necessity. The ICRC’s 2023 guidance notes that “the use of dual‑use facilities for military purposes does not automatically render them lawful targets, but it does raise the threshold for proportionality analysis.”</p> <p>Collateral damage is a real concern. The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) issued a statement on 8 April warning that “attacks on data centres risk disrupting essential services for civilians, including emergency communications, healthcare systems and financial transactions.” The EFF called for a transparent assessment of the impact on civilian populations and urged both sides to adhere to IHL principles. Russian officials echoed the concern, with the Ministry of Digital Development issuing a statement on 7 April asserting that the data‑centres were “civilian infrastructure” and that the attacks constituted a breach of international norms.</p> <h2>Civil‑society perspectives</h2> <p>In Ukraine, civil‑society groups have expressed cautious optimism. A panel of experts from the Ukrainian Institute for Cybersecurity noted that disabling bot networks could “reduce the spread of misinformation that fuels domestic unrest.” However, they cautioned that “the cyber‑front is a double‑edged sword; the same infrastructure that hosts bots also supports legitimate communication for civilians.”</p> <p>In Russia, civil‑society activists have criticized the Kremlin’s narrative that the strikes are “unlawful.” They argue that the state has a responsibility to protect its own information space and that “the Russian government’s own cyber‑operations have targeted civilian infrastructure in the past.” The debate illustrates the blurred lines between military and civilian targets in the digital domain.</p> <h2>Strategic implications</h2> <p>If the strikes have indeed disrupted a large portion of Russia’s bot networks, the strategic impact could be significant. Disinformation campaigns have been used to influence public opinion in the United States, Europe and beyond. A reduction in bot activity could dampen Russia’s ability to shape narratives in foreign media and on social platforms. The Ukrainian Ministry of Defence claims that the strikes “have already begun to alter the information environment in favor of the West.”</p>

Background & context (from research brief)

<h1>Is Zelensky right to say data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?</h1> <p><em>By Alexei Petrov</em></p>

<p>When Ukraine’s cyber‑defence units announced in late 2023 that they had disrupted a Russian data centre in the Donetsk region, the headline was not just about servers and code. It was a claim that a network of automated accounts—often called “bots”—had been cut off from the infrastructure that feeds Russia’s disinformation campaigns. In March 2024, President Volodymyr Zelensky told state television that Ukraine’s cyber operations had “taken down a vast network of propaganda bots” that were amplifying Russian narratives abroad. The statement sparked debate among analysts, legal scholars and civil‑society groups about the reality of the impact, the legality of the targets and the wider implications for cyber‑warfare norms.</p>

<p>On 12 October 2023, a Ukrainian cyber‑operations unit launched a coordinated attack on a data centre in the Donetsk region that was identified as hosting a large number of automated accounts used in Russian information operations. The Ukrainian Ministry of Defence issued a brief on 13 October confirming that the target was “dual‑use” – it also provided cloud services to local businesses and residents, but the Ministry argued that the facility’s primary function during wartime was to support Russian state‑backed propaganda efforts. The operation was described as a “precision strike” that disabled the servers’ ability to generate new bot accounts for several weeks. While the Ministry did not publish technical details, independent security researchers confirmed a sudden drop in bot activity on several platforms following the reported attack.</p>

<p>Zelensky’s statement in March 2024 was not a direct reference to the October 2023 operation, but rather a general assertion that Ukraine’s cyber‑defence had disrupted the network of bots that Russia relied on to influence global audiences. The president cited “ongoing cyber operations” that had been targeting the infrastructure behind these bots, and noted that the impact had been “measurable” in terms of reduced bot traffic on major social‑media platforms.</p>
