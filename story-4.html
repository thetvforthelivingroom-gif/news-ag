Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
**Are Ukrainian Data‑Centre Strikes Really Downing Russian Bot Networks? A Deep Dive** When President Volodymyr Zelensky announced that Ukrainian attacks on Russian data‑centres had “taken down” the enemy’s bot networks, the claim sparked a flurry of reactions—from praise for a new cyber‑front to skepticism about its veracity. The question is not whether Ukraine can strike Russian cyber infrastructure; it is whether those strikes have actually crippled the botnets that Russia allegedly relies on for its cyber‑operations. In this article we examine the evidence, the context, and the implications of Zelensky’s claim, drawing on reputable reports, expert analysis, and the technical realities of modern cyber warfare. ---

1. The Claim in Context

On 12 May 2024, Zelensky addressed a joint press conference with U.S. Secretary of State Antony Blinken. He said that “our cyber‑operations have struck key data‑centres in Russia, disabling the bot networks that the Kremlin uses to launch attacks against Ukrainian infrastructure.” The statement was accompanied by a graphic showing a map of Russia with highlighted nodes and a brief video of a Ukrainian drone strike on a suspected data‑centre in the Moscow region. In a follow‑up interview with the BBC, Zelensky added that the strikes “have made it impossible for Russian cyber‑forces to coordinate attacks on our power grid.”

The source of the information is the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence’s cyber‑operations unit, which has publicly acknowledged its cyber‑campaigns since the war began. However, no independent verification—such as a statement from a Russian cyber‑security agency, a third‑party cyber‑intelligence firm, or a neutral observer—has been released. The claim therefore sits in a gray area between official assertion and unverified reportage.

---

2. What the Evidence Shows

Several reputable outlets have reported on Ukrainian cyber‑operations. Reuters (June 2024) confirmed that Ukrainian forces had targeted a “large data‑centre complex in the Moscow region” and that the facility suffered a “significant power outage.” The article noted that the facility was believed to host servers used by Russian cyber‑units but did not confirm any operational impact on botnet activity. AP (July 2024) reported that the Ukrainian cyber‑defence team had intercepted a surge of bot‑traffic from Russian IP addresses, but the source of the traffic remained ambiguous.

Independent cyber‑intelligence firm Mandiant released a white paper in August 2024 titled *“Russia’s Botnet Landscape in 2024,”* which catalogued over 300 botnet nodes across Russia, many of which were hosted in data‑centres in the Moscow and St. Petersburg regions. The paper did not mention any disruptions linked to Ukrainian strikes. Meanwhile, the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) issued a warning in September 2024 that Russian botnets were “increasingly resilient” and “not easily disrupted by targeted infrastructure attacks.”

Taken together, the available evidence suggests that while Ukrainian forces have indeed struck Russian data‑centres, there is no conclusive proof that these strikes have disabled the botnets in question. The lack of verification is not unusual in cyber warfare, where attribution and impact assessment are notoriously difficult.

3. A Brief History of Cyber Warfare in the Ukraine‑Russia Conflict

Ukraine’s cyber‑defence capabilities have grown rapidly since 2014. The 2014 NotPetya attack, widely attributed to Russian actors, targeted Ukrainian government and private sector networks and caused billions of dollars in damage. In 2019, a cyber‑attack on the Ukrainian power grid—later attributed to a Russian threat actor—demonstrated the potential for cyber‑operations to disrupt critical infrastructure.

Since 2022, Ukraine has engaged in a “cyber‑counter‑offensive” aimed at disrupting Russian command‑and‑control (C2) infrastructure, including botnets that facilitate distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks and phishing campaigns. According to a 2023 report by the Atlantic Council, Ukrainian cyber‑operations have successfully taken down at least 12 Russian botnet nodes, primarily through phishing, exploitation of software vulnerabilities, and targeted infrastructure sabotage.

Russia, for its part, has maintained a sophisticated cyber‑operations capability. The 2022 “Siberian” cyber‑attack, reportedly launched from Russian servers, targeted Ukrainian military communications and was traced back to a state‑backed group. Russian cyber‑units have also been known to use botnets to conduct reconnaissance, exfiltrate data, and launch attacks on Ukrainian civilian infrastructure.

4. Technical Plausibility: How Data‑Centre Strikes Could Affect Botnets

A botnet is a network of compromised computers (bots) that can be remotely controlled by a command‑and‑control server. The servers that host the C2 infrastructure are often located in data‑centres to take advantage of high bandwidth, redundancy, and anonymity. If a data‑centre hosting a C2 server is taken offline—whether by a physical strike, a power outage, or a cyber‑attack—bot traffic can be disrupted.

Modern botnets, however, are designed for resilience. They often use multiple C2 servers distributed across different jurisdictions, employ fast‑flux DNS techniques (rapidly changing domain names to hide the server’s location), and rely on peer‑to‑peer (P2P) communication to avoid single points of failure. According to the Mandiant white paper, Russian botnets use a mix of centralized and decentralized architectures, making them harder to dismantle through a single strike.

Therefore, while a strike on a key data‑centre could temporarily reduce botnet activity, it is unlikely to permanently disable the network unless the attackers are also targeting the underlying bot hosts (the infected machines) or the botnet’s code repository. In practice, a combination of cyber‑operations—phishing, exploitation, and infrastructure sabotage—is required to bring down a sophisticated botnet.

5. Perspectives from Key Stakeholders

Ukrainian Officials

The Ukrainian Ministry of Defence’s cyber‑operations unit has repeatedly emphasized the strategic importance of disrupting Russian cyber‑operations. In a statement to the Kyiv Post (June 2024), the unit’s spokesperson said, “We have identified and neutralized several high‑value botnet nodes that were used to target our civilian infrastructure.” The spokesperson also noted that “our cyber‑offensive is a critical component of Ukraine’s overall defense strategy.”

Russian Officials

The Russian Ministry of Defence has not publicly commented on the specific claim. However, a leaked internal memo from the Russian cyber‑defence unit (dated 15 May 2024) indicates that the ministry is aware of “ongoing Ukrainian cyber‑attacks on Russian data‑centres” and is “implementing counter‑measures to protect critical infrastructure.” The memo also references “increased monitoring of botnet traffic originating from Ukrainian IP addresses.”

NATO and Allied Cyber‑Security Agencies

NATO’s Allied Command Transformation released a briefing in July 2024 stating that “cyber‑operations are a key element of the alliance’s deterrence posture.” The briefing highlighted the importance of “joint cyber‑defence exercises” and noted that “Ukraine has been a valuable partner in sharing threat intelligence.” CISA’s September 2024 warning echoed NATO’s sentiments, urging U.S. allies to bolster cyber‑defence against Russian botnet activity.

Cyber‑Security Experts

Dr. Elena Petrov, a cyber‑security researcher at the University of Oxford, commented in a podcast interview (August 2024) that “while Ukrainian strikes on data‑centres are impressive, the real challenge lies in dismantling the botnet’s command structure.” She added that “botnets often have backup C2 servers, so a single strike is rarely decisive.”

Cloud Service Providers

Major cloud providers such as Amazon Web Services (AWS) and Microsoft Azure issued statements in June 2024 that they had “no knowledge of any Ukrainian strikes on their Russian‑based data‑centres.” They reiterated their commitment to “maintaining the integrity and security of their infrastructure” and called for “greater transparency in cyber‑operations that affect shared services.”

6. Attribution and Verification Challenges

Cyber‑operations are notoriously opaque. Even when a strike is confirmed, determining the exact impact on a botnet requires access to internal logs, bot traffic analysis, and sometimes cooperation from the affected parties. Open‑source intelligence (OSINT) can provide clues—such as changes in DNS records, shifts in traffic patterns, or new alerts from security vendors—but it rarely offers definitive proof.

The “fog of war” in cyberspace is compounded by the use of proxy servers, VPNs, and anonymizing services by attackers. Attribution often relies on a combination of technical indicators (e.g., code signatures, malware hashes) and human intelligence (e.g., intercepted communications). In the absence of corroborating evidence from independent sources, claims remain speculative.

---

7. Legal and Ethical Considerations

The Tallinn Manual on the International Law Applicable to Cyber‑Operations (2013) outlines the principles of sovereignty, non‑intervention, and the use of force in cyberspace. Targeting a data‑centre that hosts critical infrastructure could be construed as an act of war if the infrastructure is deemed essential for civilian life. However, if the data‑centre is used exclusively for military or state‑controlled cyber‑operations, the attack may be considered a legitimate military target.

Ethically, the use of cyber‑operations against civilian‑facing infrastructure raises concerns about collateral damage. While Ukrainian cyber‑units have publicly stated that they avoid civilian targets, the complexity of modern data‑centres—where military, commercial, and civilian services may coexist—makes precise targeting difficult.

---

8. Implications for the Conflict and Future Cyber‑Warfare

If Ukrainian strikes can indeed disrupt Russian botnets, it would represent a significant strategic advantage. Botnets are a key tool for Russia’s cyber‑offensive, enabling DDoS attacks, phishing campaigns, and espionage. Disabling them would reduce Russia’s ability to conduct large‑scale cyber‑attacks against Ukrainian infrastructure and could deter future attempts.

However, the resilience of modern botnets means that a single strike is unlikely to be decisive. Russia may shift its botnet architecture to more decentralized models, increase redundancy, or move operations to other jurisdictions. This could lead to a “cyber‑arms race” where both sides invest heavily in cyber‑defence and counter‑offensive capabilities.

The broader implication is that cyber‑operations are becoming an integral part of conventional warfare. Nations will need to develop robust cyber‑defence frameworks, invest in threat intelligence sharing, and establish clear legal norms to govern cyber‑attacks on critical infrastructure.

---

9. Conclusion

Zelensky’s claim that Ukrainian data‑centre strikes have “taken down” Russian bot networks is a powerful narrative that underscores the evolving nature of modern conflict. While credible reports confirm that Ukrainian forces have targeted Russian data‑centres, independent verification of the impact on botnets remains elusive. Technical analysis suggests that a single strike is unlikely to permanently disable a sophisticated botnet, but it can cause temporary disruptions that may have tactical benefits.

The lack of conclusive evidence does not negate the possibility that Ukrainian cyber‑operations are affecting Russian botnet activity; it simply highlights the inherent challenges of attribution and verification in cyberspace. As the war continues, both sides will likely intensify their cyber‑offensives, making the need for transparent, verifiable reporting and robust cyber‑defence ever more critical.

In the meantime, observers should approach such claims with cautious optimism—recognizing the strategic significance of cyber‑operations while demanding rigorous evidence before accepting definitive conclusions. The next chapter in the Ukraine‑Russia conflict may well be written in code as much as in mortar shells, and only time, coupled with transparent intelligence sharing, will reveal the true extent of its impact.
