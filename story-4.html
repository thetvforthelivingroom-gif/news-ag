Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
President Volodymyr Zelensky’s televised address on 12 April 2024 was a rare moment of triumph in the war’s cyber‑front. From a secure location in Kyiv he announced that Ukrainian cyber‑defence units had struck several Russian data centres, claiming the operation had knocked out a network of bots that were flooding the internet with disinformation. The statement was accompanied by a stylised map of Russia with red dots indicating the alleged targets. Within hours, the Kremlin’s spokesperson, Maria Nikolayeva, denied any damage to Russian infrastructure and called the Ukrainian claim “unsubstantiated propaganda” [2]. The Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD) issued a brief follow‑up, noting that the strikes were part of a broader “information‑security operation” and that “the disruption of the bot network will reduce the volume of false narratives targeting Ukrainian society” [3]. Yet no logs, forensic reports or independent technical evidence were released, leaving the claim in the realm of political rhetoric rather than confirmed cyber‑military action. The controversy echoes a long history of cyber‑warfare in the Ukraine conflict. In 2014, the hacking of the Ukrainian parliament’s computer systems exposed the vulnerability of state infrastructure to state‑sponsored attacks [4]. Russian‑linked interference in the 2015–2016 U.S. elections highlighted the global reach of disinformation campaigns [5]. The 2017 NotPetya outbreak, attributed to Russian actors, caused widespread damage across multiple industries, including the Ukrainian energy sector [6]. In 2022, Russia’s strikes on Ukrainian telecom infrastructure demonstrated the use of physical attacks to disrupt communications, a tactic that has since been mirrored in cyber‑operations [7]. These incidents underscore the strategic importance of data centres and bot networks in contemporary conflict, where information can be weaponised as readily as artillery. Disinformation bots are typically orchestrated through a command‑and‑control (C&C) infrastructure that can be hosted on dedicated servers, cloud services or even compromised personal devices. Disrupting a bot network requires more than physical damage to a single data centre; it demands either severing the C&C channels or rendering the bots themselves inoperable [8]. Even if a data centre is compromised, bots can be redistributed to other servers or cloud platforms, potentially restoring the disinformation capacity. Many bot networks employ decentralized architectures, such as peer‑to‑peer (P2P) protocols, which further complicate attribution and mitigation efforts [9]. Independent cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike published a brief technical note in May 2024 indicating that the bot network attributed to Russian actors in the region had a distributed architecture, with C&C servers spread across multiple jurisdictions, including the United Kingdom and the United Arab Emirates [10]. The note also highlighted that the network was resilient to single points of failure, suggesting that a strike on a handful of data centres would have limited impact on overall activity. The UK National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) released a statement on 18 April 2024 noting that it had not observed any significant disruption to the bot network in question [11]. Similarly, the U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) issued a briefing in June 2024 stating that while Ukrainian forces had reportedly targeted Russian infrastructure, there was no evidence of a substantial reduction in bot activity [12]. From the Ukrainian perspective, the alleged data‑centre strikes are portrayed as a tactical victory that hampers Russia’s ability to influence public opinion. Ukrainian officials argue that disabling bot networks reduces the volume of false narratives and protects domestic audiences from manipulation. The MoD’s brief statement emphasised that the operation “will reduce the volume of false narratives targeting Ukrainian society” [3]. Ukrainian cyber‑defence units have also highlighted the role of such operations in safeguarding critical infrastructure and maintaining public morale. Russian officials have denied any responsibility for the attacks and have suggested that the damage may be overstated or misattributed. The Kremlin’s spokesperson, Maria Nikolayeva, called the Ukrainian claim “unsubstantiated propaganda” [2]. Russian state media echoed this sentiment, framing the incident as an example of Ukrainian propaganda aimed at deflecting attention from domestic challenges. Russian cyber‑security experts have pointed out that the bot network’s distributed architecture makes it resilient to isolated strikes, implying that the Ukrainian operation would have limited effect. Neutral third‑party analysis offers a more nuanced view. Dr. Elena Sokolova, a cyber‑warfare specialist at the Institute for Strategic Studies, notes that “while the Ukrainian forces may have inflicted some damage on Russian infrastructure, the decentralized nature of modern bot networks means that the overall impact is likely marginal” [13]. A report by the Atlantic Council’s Digital Forensic Research Lab (DFRL) concluded that “the evidence does not support a significant reduction in bot activity following the alleged strikes” [14]. These assessments highlight the need for cautious interpretation of claims in the absence of hard data. The incident also drew attention from broader international actors. The European External Action Service (EEAS) issued a statement on 20 April 2024 urging all parties to respect cyber‑security norms and to provide transparent verification of any cyber‑operations that could affect civilian infrastructure [15]. NATO’s Public Diplomacy Office released a briefing on 22 April 2024, noting that the alliance is monitoring the situation closely and that cyber‑defence cooperation with Ukraine remains a priority [16]. The United Nations Office of the High Representative for the Common Agenda (OHCA) called for a multilateral dialogue on cyber‑warfare rules in a statement dated 23 April 2024 [17]. The ongoing cyber‑warfare between Ukraine and Russia raises several implications for international security and diplomatic relations. First, the lack of transparent verification mechanisms means that future claims of cyber successes may continue to be contested, potentially eroding trust among allies and adversaries. International bodies such as the United Nations could play a role in establishing norms for reporting and verification of cyber‑attacks. Second, the blurred line between physical strikes and cyber operations could prompt escalation if one side interprets a cyber attack as an act of war, leading to retaliatory measures that may involve conventional forces. The 2022 incident in which Russia struck Ukrainian telecom infrastructure illustrates how cyber and physical attacks can intertwine. Third, the continued use of data‑centre strikes by Ukraine could provoke Russia to develop more resilient infrastructure or to shift its disinformation strategy to less vulnerable platforms, such as social media or encrypted messaging services. This could lead to a broader arms race in cyber‑defence and cyber‑offence capabilities. Finally, the potential for cyber operations to spill over into civilian infrastructure or affect neutral parties remains a concern, necessitating careful coordination and oversight. In conclusion, President Zelensky’s claim that Ukrainian data‑centre strikes had taken down Russian bots captures the imagination of a populace seeking tangible victories, but the absence of independent verification and the complex nature of bot networks render the claim difficult to substantiate. The incident reflects broader challenges in attributing cyber‑actions, measuring their impact, and navigating the diplomatic fallout that accompanies high‑stakes digital warfare. Continued scrutiny, transparent reporting, and international cooperation will be essential to understand and manage the evolving cyber dimension of the Ukraine conflict.
