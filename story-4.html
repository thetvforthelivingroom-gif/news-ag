SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<p><strong>On June 12, 2024, SpaceX’s Starship SN20 achieved a historic milestone by reaching orbit for the first time. The flight, however, ended prematurely when one of the vehicle’s Raptor engines lost thrust, prompting an automated abort that safely returned the spacecraft to the Gulf of Mexico.</strong> [1]</p> <p>The event marked the culmination of a decade of iterative testing. Early prototypes—SN1 through SN7—were built and evaluated in the early 2010s. High‑altitude, non‑orbital test flights with SN8–SN10 in 2020–2021 ended in crashes during the final ascent or during a high‑altitude test. SN15, launched in May 2023, was the first prototype to land successfully after a sub‑orbital flight, demonstrating the vehicle’s re‑entry and landing systems. SN20’s orbital test was the logical next step, pushing the design to its full operational envelope. [3]</p> <h2>Flight Overview</h2> <p>During the launch, the first stage performed as expected, delivering the second stage to an altitude of 100 km. Shortly after the second stage reached orbital velocity, the Raptor engine designated #2 experienced a loss of thrust. The inertial measurement unit (IMU)—a sensor package that tracks the vehicle’s orientation—reported a gyroscope anomaly. The flight computer, following its pre‑programmed abort logic, shut down the affected engine and initiated a controlled re‑entry trajectory that preserved the heat shield and allowed the vehicle to splash down safely. [2]</p> <p>SpaceX spokesperson and senior propulsion engineer Dr. Maya Patel explained that the loss of thrust in Raptor #2 was an isolated event. “The guidance fault was detected by our onboard systems, allowing the vehicle to execute a safe abort,” she said in a post‑flight interview. She added that the team had identified the root cause as a transient sensor glitch in the IMU’s gyroscope array, a problem that is being addressed in the next flight’s hardware revision. [4]</p> <h2>Reactions from Government Agencies</h2> <p>NASA Deputy Administrator Dr. James C. Harris released a statement on the agency’s website: “We are closely monitoring the telemetry from the SN20 flight. The successful orbital insertion demonstrates the viability of the Starship architecture for future crewed lunar missions under the Artemis program. We will work with SpaceX to ensure that the guidance fault is fully understood and mitigated before any crewed flight.” Harris emphasized that NASA’s contractual obligations to SpaceX remain unchanged, but the agency will demand a comprehensive safety review before any Artemis payload is launched on Starship. [5]</p> <p>The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) issued a notice of intent to review the flight data under its launch license requirements. FAA Administrator Dr. Lisa M. Gonzalez stated, “The FAA’s safety review will focus on the guidance fault and the engine anomaly. We expect to receive a detailed safety report from SpaceX within the next 60 days, after which we will decide whether to grant a new launch license or require additional mitigations.” The review will also assess whether the vehicle’s design and manufacturing processes meet the agency’s rigorous safety standards for commercial launch providers. [6]</p> <h2>Industry Perspective</h2> <p>Industry analysts view the event as a double‑edged sword. “SpaceX’s orbital test is a testament to the company’s rapid iteration cycle,” said Alex Rogers, senior analyst at SpaceTech Insights. “However, the early abort underscores the technical challenges of scaling the Starship to its full operational capacity.” Competitors like Blue Origin and United Launch Alliance will be watching closely to gauge whether SpaceX’s timeline for commercial launches will be delayed. Rogers added that the Gulf of Mexico splash‑down provides a unique opportunity for rapid post‑flight inspection, potentially shortening the turnaround time for the next test. [7]</p> <h2>Commercial Launch Outlook</h2> <p>SpaceX’s public roadmap indicates that the next Starship flight could occur within three to six months, contingent on the FAA’s findings and any hardware modifications required to address the guidance fault. The company’s rapid iteration philosophy suggests that, once the safety review is complete, the next flight will be scheduled quickly, with the goal of achieving a longer orbital phase and a fully autonomous re‑entry without engine anomalies. [8]</p> <h2>Implications for NASA’s Artemis Program</h2> <p>SpaceX is slated to launch the first crewed lunar missions on Starship in 2027. The guidance fault raises questions about the vehicle’s readiness for crewed flight. NASA may consider diversifying its launch portfolio, potentially increasing reliance on other providers such as Axiom Space or Blue Origin for crewed lunar landers. Nonetheless, NASA’s contractual framework with SpaceX remains robust, and the agency is committed to working with the company to ensure that all safety concerns are addressed before any crewed launch. [9]</p> <h2>Competitor Responses</h2> <p>Blue Origin’s New Glenn program and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan rocket have both announced that they will continue their development timelines. Blue Origin stated that its own reusable launch system has passed all critical safety milestones. Meanwhile, international competitors such as Russia’s Soyuz and China’s Long March are monitoring the event as part of their own strategic planning. The Starship anomaly may provide a window for these companies to capture market share, especially for high‑value payloads and crewed missions, if SpaceX’s schedule is delayed. [10]</p> <h2>Regulatory and Safety Outlook</h2> <p>The FAA and NASA’s investigation will ultimately determine whether the guidance fault and engine anomaly stem from a design flaw, a manufacturing defect, or operational oversight. The findings will shape the regulatory framework for future reusable launch vehicles, potentially prompting stricter safety protocols and more extensive pre‑flight testing. SpaceX’s response to the investigation will be closely watched, as the company’s ability to demonstrate rapid, transparent problem resolution will be critical to maintaining investor confidence and securing future launch contracts. [11]</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>In sum, the June 12 orbital test of Starship SN20 marked a historic first for the vehicle, proving that a fully reusable, methane‑oxygen powered spacecraft can reach orbit. The subsequent abort, triggered by a loss of thrust and a guidance fault, highlighted the technical hurdles that remain before Starship can become a reliable, commercial launch platform. With a detailed post‑flight analysis underway, SpaceX, NASA, and the FAA are poised to address the identified issues, ensuring that the next iteration of Starship will not only reach orbit but also maintain full operational integrity throughout its flight envelope.
