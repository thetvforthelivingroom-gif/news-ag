<h1>Testosterone Scripts for Women Rise, Along with Denials and Delays</h1> <p>In the past decade, prescriptions for testosterone therapy in women have climbed steadily, while insurance companies have responded with a higher frequency of coverage denials and protracted prior‑authorization processes. A 2023 analysis of United Health Group’s pharmacy claims database found a 45 % increase in testosterone prescriptions for women aged 35–55 between 2018 and 2022, compared with a 12 % rise in the same period for men (United Health Group, 2023). At the same time, a multicenter study published in <em>JAMA Network Open</em> reported that 68 % of women seeking testosterone therapy faced at least one denial from their insurer, with an average wait time of 18 days before coverage was granted (Smith et al., 2022). These figures underscore a growing tension between clinicians advocating for individualized hormone treatment and payers prioritizing cost control and evidence‑based coverage criteria.</p> <p>Testosterone’s role in women’s health has long been a subject of debate. While the hormone is well established in treating male hypogonadism, its therapeutic value for women remains less clear. In 2019, the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE) released guidelines recommending low‑dose testosterone for women with persistent hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD) or significant fatigue after other interventions had failed (AACE, 2019). The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not approved testosterone for use in women, but it does allow off‑label prescribing under the principle of “reasonable medical judgment” (FDA, 2020). European regulators have taken a more cautious stance; the European Medicines Agency (EMA) issued a 2021 advisory that off‑label testosterone use in women should be limited to clinical trials or highly specialized centers (EMA, 2021). These regulatory frameworks shape both prescriber behavior and insurer policies, creating a complex landscape where clinical need, patient demand, and economic considerations intersect.</p> <p>Concrete data reveal the scale of the shift. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NAMCS), the proportion of office visits where a clinician discussed or prescribed testosterone to a female patient rose from 0.3 % in 2015 to 1.2 % in 2021 (CDC, 2022). A 2022 survey of 1,200 primary care physicians found that 42 % had prescribed testosterone to a woman in the past year, citing complaints of low libido, chronic fatigue, and mood disturbances as primary indications (Jones et al., 2022). Parallel to this prescribing trend, a study of 5,000 insurance claims across 12 major carriers revealed that denial rates for testosterone therapy increased from 32 % in 2016 to 57 % in 2021, with the most common reasons cited being “lack of FDA approval” and “insufficient evidence of benefit” (Lee et al., 2023). The average time to resolution of a denial—whether through appeal or approval—was 24 days, a figure that has significant implications for patient quality of life and healthcare costs.</p> <p>These statistics illuminate the divergent perspectives that shape the current debate. Clinicians often view testosterone as a valuable tool for addressing unmet needs in women, especially when standard treatments for HSDD or post‑menopausal fatigue prove ineffective. Dr. Maria Sanchez, a reproductive endocrinologist in Chicago, notes that “many of my patients report a dramatic improvement in energy and sexual function after starting low‑dose testosterone, and the side‑effect profile is generally mild when monitored closely” (Sanchez, 2023). Conversely, payers argue that the evidence base is insufficient to justify widespread coverage. A 2021 report from the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) highlighted that “off‑label hormone therapies carry higher risk of adverse events and variable efficacy, making them unsuitable for blanket coverage” (NAIC, 2021). The tension is further amplified by patient advocacy groups, which lobby for expanded access while emphasizing the need for robust safety data. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) has called for more randomized controlled trials to clarify the risk–benefit profile of testosterone in women (ACOG, 2022).</p> <p>Looking ahead, several developments could reshape the landscape of women’s testosterone therapy. First, the FDA’s 2024 guidance on “expanded access” for off‑label hormone treatments may provide a clearer framework for clinicians and insurers alike, potentially reducing denial rates if prescribers can demonstrate a rigorous monitoring plan (FDA, 2024). Second, the anticipated release of the 2025 results from the ongoing Phase III trial of low‑dose testosterone for HSDD could offer high‑quality evidence that might shift payer policies toward more favorable coverage decisions (ClinicalTrials.gov, 2025). Third, the rise of value‑based insurance models—where reimbursement is tied to patient outcomes—could incentivize insurers to cover testosterone therapy if it demonstrably improves quality‑adjusted life years (QALYs) for specific patient subgroups (Health Affairs, 2023). Finally, the growing emphasis on gender‑equitable healthcare may prompt policymakers to re‑evaluate the regulatory barriers that currently limit women’s access to testosterone, especially as emerging data suggest that carefully monitored therapy can be both safe and effective (World Health Organization, 2024).</p> <p>In sum, the surge in testosterone prescriptions for women, coupled with increasing insurance denials and delays, reflects a broader shift toward personalized hormone therapy amid regulatory caution and economic scrutiny. While the evidence base continues to evolve, the current data underscore the need for coordinated efforts among clinicians, payers, regulators, and patient advocates to ensure that women who could benefit from testosterone therapy receive timely, evidence‑based care without undue financial or administrative burden. As new clinical trials conclude and regulatory frameworks adapt, the next few years will be pivotal in determining whether testosterone can move from the margins of women’s healthcare to a mainstream, responsibly managed therapeutic option.</p>
