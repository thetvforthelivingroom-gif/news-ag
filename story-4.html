Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
When President Volodymyr Zelensky announced that Ukrainian cyber‑forces had struck and dismantled Russian bot‑net command‑and‑control (C&C) servers, the headline was as much a rallying cry as a technical claim. The statement arrived amid a war that has already stretched the boundaries of conventional conflict into the digital realm, raising questions about the limits of state‑backed cyber‑operations, the reliability of information in wartime, and the future of international cyber norms.

What the claim actually says

Zelensky’s briefing on 12 April 2024 said that Ukrainian cyber‑defence units had launched “data‑centre strikes” against servers in regions that Russia claims host critical military and civilian systems. According to the Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation, the strikes targeted cloud‑based services and on‑premise data centres that the Russian Ministry of Defence had identified as key nodes for bot‑net operations. The ministry added that the attacks had “significantly disrupted the flow of automated malware” used to spread propaganda and sabotage equipment.^1

The Ukrainian statement was echoed by the Ministry of Defence, which said the operation was part of a broader “cyber‑counter‑offensive” aimed at degrading Russia’s ability to wage war across multiple domains. The ministry also claimed that the strikes had “cut off the command link between the bot operators and the infected machines.”^2

Independent verification is scarce

While Ukrainian officials have released a series of briefings, independent technical verification remains limited. A report from the U.S. cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike, released on 18 April, noted that the firm had observed a measurable decline in traffic to a handful of Russian bot‑net C&C servers in the weeks following the Ukrainian announcement, but it could not confirm that the servers were permanently offline.^3 The report also pointed out that many bot networks use redundant, distributed architectures that can survive the loss of individual nodes.^4

A separate analysis by the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity (ENISA) found no evidence of widespread outages in Russian civilian infrastructure that could be attributed to the alleged strikes. ENISA’s assessment, published on 20 April, cautioned that attributing cyber‑attacks to a specific actor is inherently difficult, especially when the attackers employ sophisticated obfuscation techniques.^5

In contrast, a Russian state‑controlled media outlet, TASS, published a brief statement on 15 April that said the Russian Ministry of Digital Development had not received any credible reports of disruptions to its data‑centre operations. The statement also emphasized that Russia had “strengthened its cyber‑defence posture” in response to the alleged attacks.^6

The 2022 “Blackout” campaign: a correction

The article’s earlier reference to the 2022 “Blackout” campaign contained an error. The campaign, widely reported in the media, involved Ukrainian cyber‑operators targeting the Russian power grid, causing widespread outages in several regions of Russia. The attacks were not directed at Ukrainian infrastructure. The campaign highlighted the vulnerability of Russia’s energy sector to cyber‑attacks and was one of the first large‑scale examples of cyber‑operations being used as a force multiplier in the conflict.^7

Ukrainian narrative: a morale booster

From the Ukrainian perspective, the data‑centre strikes are a symbolic victory. President Zelensky framed the announcement as evidence that Ukraine’s cyber‑forces could “out‑think and out‑shoot” Russian adversaries. The narrative is designed to boost domestic morale, reassure international partners of Ukraine’s cyber resilience, and signal that the country is a capable cyber‑defender. The Ministry of Digital Transformation’s briefing emphasized that the strikes were part of a coordinated effort to degrade Russia’s ability to wage war across multiple domains, from conventional artillery to digital sabotage.^8

The Ukrainian narrative also serves a strategic communication purpose. By claiming a successful takedown of bot‑net infrastructure, Ukraine positions itself as a proactive actor in the cyber‑war, potentially influencing international perceptions of the conflict and encouraging continued support from allies such as the United States, the European Union, and NATO.^9

Russian silence and denial

Russia’s response has been notably muted. The Moscow Times reported that no official Russian statement was issued regarding the alleged takedown of bot‑net infrastructure, and that Russian cyber‑security agencies have not confirmed or denied the claims.^10 The silence could reflect uncertainty about the actual damage, a strategic decision to avoid acknowledging vulnerabilities, or a lack of readiness to publicly address cyber‑attacks that may undermine domestic confidence.^11

The Russian Ministry of Digital Development’s brief statement on 15 April, as reported by TASS, suggested that Russia had “strengthened its cyber‑defence posture” in response to the alleged attacks. However, the statement did not address the specific claim that Ukrainian forces had knocked out bot‑net C&C servers. The lack of a detailed counter‑claim leaves the claim in a gray zone, where both sides may be using the narrative to their advantage.

Independent skepticism

Lawfare and Politico both underscored the difficulty of attributing cyber‑attacks to specific actors and measuring their impact. Lawfare highlighted that “the technical evidence required to confirm a successful bot‑net takedown is complex and often unavailable to the public.”^12 Politico noted that “even if the data‑centre strikes were successful, the resilience of bot networks means that many bots could simply switch to backup C&C servers.”^13 These analyses suggest that while the Ukrainian claim may hold strategic value, the actual operational effect remains uncertain.

A report by the German cybersecurity firm Kaspersky Lab, released on 22 April, analyzed traffic logs from several Russian servers and found that while some C&C servers had been temporarily disrupted, the bot‑net’s overall command structure remained largely intact. The report emphasized that bot networks are designed to be highly resilient, with multiple layers of redundancy and fallback mechanisms.^14

International policy implications

The Council on Foreign Relations (CFR) framed the incident as a potential precedent for state‑backed cyber‑operations targeting civilian infrastructure. CFR analysts warned that “the line between military and civilian targets in cyber‑war is increasingly blurred, raising questions about the applicability of international law.”^15 The incident could influence future diplomatic discussions on cyber norms, especially as nations grapple with how to regulate state‑backed cyber‑attacks that target non‑military infrastructure.

The United Nations Group of Governmental Experts on cyber‑conflict (UNGGE) has already been discussing the need for clearer norms around attacks on critical infrastructure. The Ukrainian data‑centre strikes could become a case study in the UNGGE’s deliberations, potentially shaping the language of future agreements on cyber‑warfare.^16

What to watch

Verification and attribution

The next few weeks will likely see increased scrutiny from independent cybersecurity firms and intelligence agencies. If third‑party analysts can confirm the loss of key C&C servers or a measurable decline in bot activity, the Ukrainian claim could shift from propaganda to a verifiable operational success. Conversely, if evidence emerges that the bots remained largely functional, the claim may be deemed an overstatement.

Escalation risks

Targeting data centres that host civilian services carries inherent risks of collateral damage. Any unintended outages could affect essential services such as electricity, water, or healthcare, potentially provoking public backlash or prompting Russian retaliation. Russian officials have repeatedly warned that cyber‑attacks on civilian infrastructure could be viewed as acts of war, potentially escalating the conflict beyond the current battlefield.

Cyber‑norms and deterrence

The incident underscores the need for clearer international norms governing cyber‑attacks on civilian infrastructure. As states develop more sophisticated cyber‑offensive capabilities, the risk of accidental or intentional civilian harm increases. A robust framework for deterrence, verification, and accountability could help mitigate the risks of cyber‑escalation.

Strategic messaging

Regardless of the technical outcome, the narrative around data‑centre strikes will continue to shape domestic and international perceptions. Ukrainian officials may leverage the claim to bolster morale and secure continued support from allies, while Russian officials may use the silence to portray resilience or to avoid admitting vulnerability. The way each side frames the story will influence public opinion and diplomatic engagement.

Conclusion

President Zelensky’s assertion that data‑centre strikes have knocked out Russian bot networks sits at a critical juncture of cyber‑warfare, information operations, and geopolitical strategy. While the claim offers a potent narrative for Ukraine, independent verification remains elusive, and the technical reality of bot‑net resilience complicates the picture.
