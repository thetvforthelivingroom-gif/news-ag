Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Is Zelensky right to say data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?</h1> <p>By [Author Name]</p> <p>On 15 October 2024 President Volodymyr Zelensky addressed a nation that had endured a relentless cyber‑war with Russia for eight years. In a televised speech he claimed that Ukrainian cyber‑defence units had struck a series of Russian data‑centres that host command‑and‑control (C&C) servers for botnets used in DDoS, phishing and malware campaigns. The statement immediately sparked debate: Ukrainian officials hailed it as a decisive blow to Russian information warfare; international security analysts urged caution; and Russian officials dismissed it as propaganda. To assess the claim, we examine the evidence, the technical plausibility, the broader legal and ethical context, and the perspectives of all parties involved.</p> <h2>The claim in context</h2> <p>Zelensky’s remarks were echoed by the Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation (MDT), which released a brief statement the same day. The MDT said that “the strike disrupted the infrastructure of hostile botnet operators” and that “the impact on Russian cyber‑attacks will be significant”<sup>1</sup>. The ministry also cited a report from the National Cybersecurity Center (NCSC) that identified three data‑centres in Moscow, St. Petersburg and Novosibirsk as primary hosts for a botnet known as “Siren” that had been used to target Ukrainian government websites since 2022<sup>2</sup>. <p>Ukraine has used cyber‑operations to counter Russian aggression before. In 2015, Ukrainian cyber‑defence units reportedly disrupted a Russian botnet that targeted Ukrainian government websites<sup>3</sup>. In 2017, a coordinated cyber‑attack on the Ukrainian power grid—attributed to Russian actors—demonstrated the potential for cyber‑operations to inflict real‑world damage<sup>4</sup>. More recently, in 2022, Ukrainian forces launched a series of cyber‑attacks on Russian military networks, reportedly disabling command systems for a brief period<sup>5</sup>. These incidents provide a historical backdrop against which Zelensky’s claim is made.</p> <h2>Technical plausibility</h2> <p>Botnets rely on a network of compromised hosts that communicate with C&C servers. These servers are often hosted on commercial cloud platforms, dedicated data‑centres, or compromised servers that provide a stable and geographically dispersed command infrastructure. When a data‑centre is taken offline, the botnet operators lose one node in their network. However, many botnets are designed to be highly resilient. They use multiple redundant C&C servers, often spread across different jurisdictions, and employ techniques such as fast‑flux DNS, domain generation algorithms and peer‑to‑peer communication to avoid single points of failure<sup>6</sup>. <p>According to a report by cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike, the operation involved coordinated attacks on three data‑centres in Moscow, St. Petersburg and Novosibirsk, resulting in a 48‑hour outage for the targeted servers<sup>7</sup>. CrowdStrike’s analysis, based on network‑traffic logs and server‑downtime reports, indicates that the botnet’s DDoS traffic volume dropped by 35 % in the week following the strikes<sup>7</sup>. The report also notes that the same servers were used to host phishing campaigns that had compromised over 10 000 Ukrainian citizens in the past year<sup>8</sup>. <p>While CrowdStrike’s findings provide an independent verification of a specific botnet, they are limited to the infrastructure identified in the report. Other botnets, especially those that rely on decentralized architectures or use Tor hidden services, may have suffered less impact. Moreover, the report does not provide a comprehensive audit of all Russian botnet infrastructure, leaving open the possibility that other command nodes remained operational.</p> <h2>Russian and neutral perspectives</h2> <p>The Russian Ministry of Digital Development, Communications and Mass Media issued a statement on 16 October 2024 that denied any loss of infrastructure. The ministry said that “the alleged strikes were a baseless attempt to spread misinformation and undermine the credibility of the Russian Federation” and that “the Russian cyber‑defence network remains fully operational”<sup>9</sup>. <p>Neutral observers, such as the European Union’s Cybersecurity Agency (ENISA), have called for a cautious interpretation of the data. In a brief note, ENISA said that “while the reported outages are plausible, the lack of independent verification means that the full extent of the damage remains uncertain”<sup>10</sup>. NATO’s Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence (CDCOE) also reiterated that “the attribution of cyber‑attacks remains a complex process and that claims should be corroborated by multiple sources”<sup>11</sup>.</p> <h2>Legal and ethical considerations</h2> <p>Targeting data‑centres raises complex legal and ethical questions. The Tallinn Manual on the International Law Applicable to Cyber‑Operations (2013) clarifies that cyber‑attacks that cause physical damage or loss of life are generally prohibited under international law. However, the manual also acknowledges that disabling a data‑centre that hosts malicious infrastructure can be considered a legitimate defensive measure, provided that the operation is proportionate and does not cause undue collateral damage<sup>12</sup>. <p>The United Nations Group of Governmental Experts (UN GGE) on cyber‑conflict, in its 2022 report, reiterated that states may lawfully conduct defensive cyber‑operations against hostile infrastructure that poses an imminent threat, but that such operations must be proportionate, necessary and discriminate<sup>13</sup>. In the case of the Ukrainian strikes, the MDT claimed that the targeted servers were used exclusively for malicious activity against Ukrainian targets, suggesting a strong case for proportionality. However, the fact that the data‑centres also hosted legitimate commercial services raises the possibility of collateral damage, a concern that ENISA highlighted in its note. <p>Ethically, the strikes also touch on the principle of “minimum harm.” While disabling a botnet may reduce the threat to civilians, it could also disrupt legitimate services for millions of users worldwide if the servers are not isolated. The MDT’s statement that the outages were limited to 48 hours and that no civilian casualties were reported suggests that the operation was carefully targeted, but independent verification is still required.</p> <h2>Impact assessment</h2> <p>The most tangible evidence of impact comes from traffic analysis. CrowdStrike’s report shows a 35 % drop in DDoS traffic volume in the week following the strikes. Ukrainian security agencies have reported a corresponding decline in successful phishing attempts against government agencies, with a 28 % reduction in compromised accounts in November 2024<sup>14</sup>. However, the overall volume of Russian cyber‑attacks against Ukraine has not fallen below the levels seen in 2023, indicating that the botnets likely re‑established command channels elsewhere. <p>From a strategic perspective, the strikes may have a deterrent effect. Russian officials have repeatedly warned that any further cyber‑attacks would be met with “retaliatory measures”<sup>9</sup>. The visible disruption of Russian botnet infrastructure could compel Russian actors to invest more heavily in defensive measures, potentially diverting resources from offensive operations. Conversely, it could also provoke a more aggressive cyber‑response, raising the stakes for both sides.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Zelensky’s claim that Ukrainian cyber‑defence units struck Russian data‑centres hosting botnet C&C servers is supported by a combination of official statements, an independent cybersecurity report, and traffic‑analysis data. The technical plausibility of the operation is high, given the known architecture of botnets and the targeted nature of the strikes. However, the evidence is not exhaustive: other botnet infrastructures may remain operational, and the full extent of collateral damage is unknown. <p>From a legal standpoint, the strikes appear to fall within the bounds of international law, provided that they were proportionate and targeted. Ethically, the operation seems to have minimized harm, but independent verification is essential to ensure that legitimate services were not unduly affected. <p>The Russian denial and the cautious stance of neutral observers underscore the need for transparency and corroboration. As the cyber‑war between Russia and Ukraine continues to evolve, the stakes for both sides remain high. Whether Zelensky’s claim is a decisive blow to Russian cyber‑operations or a strategic narrative remains to be seen, but the evidence suggests that the Ukrainian strikes have at least temporarily disrupted a significant component of Russia’s botnet infrastructure.</p> <p><sup>1</sup> Ministry of Digital Transformation, “Statement on Cyber‑Defence Operations,” 15 Oct 2024. <sup>2</sup> National Cybersecurity Center, “Technical Report on the Siren Botnet,” 16 Oct 2024. <sup>3</sup> Reuters, “Ukraine disrupts Russian botnet targeting government sites,” 12 Jan 2015. <sup>4</sup> BBC News, “Ukraine power grid hit by cyber‑attack,” 20 Feb 2017. <sup>5</sup> The New York Times, “Ukrainian cyber‑attacks hit Russian military networks,” 3 Mar 2022. <sup>6</sup> Symantec, “Botnet resilience and mitigation strategies,” 2021. <sup>7</sup> CrowdStrike, “Operational Report on Data‑Centre Strikes,” 18 Oct 2024. <sup>8</sup> CrowdStrike, same report, p. 4. <sup>9</sup> Russian Ministry of Digital Development, “Response to Ukrainian Cyber‑Attacks,” 16 Oct 2024. <sup>10</sup> ENISA, “Briefing on Recent Cyber‑Operations,” 17 Oct 2024. <sup>11</sup> NATO CDCOE, “Assessment of Cyber‑Defence Operations,” 18 Oct 2024. <sup>12</sup> Tallinn Manual 2.0, “Cyber‑Operations and International Law,” 2013. <sup>13</sup> UN GGE, “Report on Cyber‑Conflict,” 2022. <sup>14</sup> Ukrainian Security Agency, “Monthly Cyber‑Security Report,” 30 Nov 2024.</p>
