SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> SpaceX’s Starship SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica, Texas, on 5 May 2023, becoming the first Starship vehicle to reach orbit. The flight lasted only a few minutes before a glitch in the upper‑stage guidance system forced the vehicle to return to the launch pad. The event underscored how close the margin is between a historic milestone and a technical setback in the race to reusable rockets.

What happened

The launch followed the familiar Starship–Super Heavy sequence. Super Heavy ignited, accelerated the stack, and separated after about 2½ minutes. Starship then coasted to a low‑Earth orbit, performed a 30‑second upper‑stage burn that raised the vehicle to a 250‑km circular orbit, and began re‑entry. During that burn, a fault in the guidance system caused a sudden loss of attitude control. The flight‑control team immediately commanded a controlled descent, and the vehicle landed safely on the launch pad. The entire mission—from liftoff to touchdown—spanned roughly 10 minutes.

The incident was the first time a Starship had entered orbit, but it also marked the first time the vehicle’s flight ended prematurely because of a technical fault. Major media outlets—including the BBC, AP News, NPR, and Space—reported the guidance glitch as the primary cause of the early termination.

Flight profile

The Starship–Super Heavy stack’s flight profile is consistent across tests. After booster separation, Starship coasted for 2½ minutes, then performed a 30‑second burn that raised it to a 250‑km circular orbit. The guidance system glitch occurred during this burn, leading to a rapid loss of attitude control. The vehicle was then commanded to re‑enter and land on the launch pad, where it touched down in a controlled manner. The mission’s total duration was about 10 minutes from liftoff to landing.

Background and context

Starship’s development has been marked by a rapid, aggressive iteration cycle. Since the first prototype, SN1, SpaceX has built and tested a series of vehicles, each designed to address specific technical challenges. SN8, launched in 2019, reached an altitude of about 150 m before a hard landing; SN10 and SN11, launched in 2020, each reached 120–150 m before crashing; SN15, the first to reach 12.5 km, was also lost. The company’s approach emphasizes rapid testing and learning, which has accelerated progress but also increased the frequency of failures.

The partnership with NASA has been a key driver of SpaceX’s development. NASA’s Commercial Crew Program and its upcoming Commercial Crew Development and Commercial Lunar Payload Services initiatives rely on Starship’s potential to deliver crew, cargo, and eventually humans to Mars. NASA’s expectations for reliability and safety have clashed at times with SpaceX’s cost‑driven, rapid‑iteration strategy, leading to debates over launch cadence and risk tolerance.

Regulatory oversight for the launch was provided by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the U.S. Space Force. The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation granted launch licenses and required compliance with debris mitigation standards. The Space Force’s Space Launch Delta 45 also monitored the launch for national security and space traffic management. The launch’s compliance with debris mitigation guidelines was a key point of discussion, as the early termination raised concerns about potential debris from the vehicle’s upper stage.

The environmental impact of the launch was also highlighted. Boca Chica’s proximity to the Gulf of Mexico and the Gulf Coast’s sensitive ecosystems prompted scrutiny from environmental groups. Concerns focused on rocket exhaust, potential debris, and the cumulative impact of frequent launches. SpaceX has stated that it is working with regulators to mitigate environmental risks, but the early termination of SN20’s flight added a new dimension to the debate.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

From a commercial standpoint, the launch was a mixed signal. On the one hand, the fact that Starship reached orbit for the first time was a significant technical achievement, reinforcing SpaceX’s reputation for rapid innovation. On the other hand, the early termination due to a guidance glitch highlighted the inherent risks of a first‑of‑its‑kind vehicle. Investors reacted with a mixture of optimism and caution. SpaceX’s valuation, which had already been valued at around $100 billion prior to the launch, remained largely unchanged, but analysts noted that the company’s cost structure and launch cadence will continue to influence market sentiment.

Industry observers praised the rapid progress but cautioned that the path to a fully reusable launch system remains fraught with engineering challenges. NASA officials emphasized the importance of safety and reliability for future crewed missions, while SpaceX engineers reiterated their commitment to iterative testing and data‑driven improvements.

Environmental groups called for stricter oversight of launch debris and emissions, especially as launch frequency increases. The FAA and Space Force have indicated that they will continue to monitor compliance with debris mitigation standards and will consider additional measures if necessary.

In summary, Starship SN20’s brief orbital flight marked a historic first for SpaceX, but the guidance glitch that forced an early return served as a reminder that the journey to fully reusable rockets is still a work in progress. The event will inform future design iterations, regulatory oversight, and stakeholder expectations as SpaceX moves closer to its goal of sending humans to Mars and beyond.
