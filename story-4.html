Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
<h2>Ukraine claims cyber‑strikes crippled Russian bot networks – what’s the evidence?</h2> <p>On 12 April, President Volodymyr Zelensky addressed the nation in a televised speech, announcing that Ukrainian cyber‑defence forces had struck several data‑centres that allegedly hosted command‑and‑control (C&C) servers for Russian bot networks, rendering those bots inoperable. “We have taken decisive action against the digital infrastructure that Russia uses to wage war against us,” he declared, echoing earlier statements that have been used to rally domestic support and justify international aid.1</p> <h3>From reactive to proactive: Ukraine’s cyber‑defence evolution</h3> <p>Ukraine’s experience with cyber warfare began in 2015 with the NotPetya attack, attributed to Russian actors. The malware crippled government systems, caused an estimated $10 billion in damage, and exposed the fragility of the country’s digital infrastructure.2 In 2022, a coordinated series of cyber‑operations targeted the Ukrainian power grid, temporarily knocking out electricity in several regions and proving that critical infrastructure can be disrupted from afar.3 These events prompted the creation of the Ukrainian Cyber‑Defence Forces in 2021 and the deployment of advanced threat‑detection systems nationwide.</p> <h3>What the claims say about the alleged data‑centre strikes</h3> <p>According to the BBC, the strikes combined distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks and exploit‑based intrusions that seized control of target servers.4 The Guardian echoed this, noting that the operations were part of a broader “cyber‑offensive” aimed at undermining Russian military communications.5 France 24 described the attacks as “high‑profile operations” targeting servers in regions heavily used by Russian cyber actors.6 Mother Jones added that these operations are part of a long‑standing Ukrainian cyber‑strategy that dates back to NotPetya and the 2022 power‑grid attacks.7</p> <p>Ukrainian cyber‑defence chief Oleksandr Klymenko told reporters that the forces had “identified and neutralised the servers that host the command and control of the Russian botnet.” “The servers were located in data‑centres that were heavily used by Russian actors,” he said.8 No Ukrainian official has released technical details, citing operational security concerns.9 Kyiv Post reported that Ukrainian cyber‑defence units have remained tight‑lipped about the specifics of the strikes.10</p> <p>Russian state media and cyber‑security agencies have likewise been silent; Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty noted that no Russian statement has confirmed or denied the claim.11 However, TASS reported that the Russian Ministry of Digital Development, Communications and Mass Media denied any disruption to its bot networks, saying, “We have no evidence that Ukrainian attacks have impacted our infrastructure.”12 Roskomnadzor spokesperson Yulia Sokolova added, “Our systems remain fully operational, and we continue to monitor the situation.”13</p> <h3>Can a handful of data‑centres bring a bot network down?</h3> <p>Bot networks used by state actors are typically distributed across multiple servers and cloud platforms to increase resilience. Modern botnets often employ a hierarchical architecture: a central C&C server issues commands to a fleet of infected hosts, but redundant C&C servers are deployed across different geographic regions and cloud providers to avoid single points of failure.14 The Conversation notes that such architectures are designed to survive localized attacks, with redundant pathways and automated fail‑over mechanisms that can maintain bot operations even if a single node is taken offline.15</p> <p>Disabling a distributed network through a single or a few data‑centre attacks is unlikely unless those centres host critical, non‑redundant components. The University of Oxford’s Centre for Cyber Security has analysed similar botnet structures and found that targeted strikes can only cripple a botnet if they hit the core C&C servers that are not mirrored elsewhere.16 Mandiant’s recent report confirms that the resilience of modern botnets makes them difficult to dismantle with targeted strikes alone, especially when the attackers use cloud‑based infrastructure that can be quickly re‑provisioned.17</p> <p>Snopes reviewed the claim and found no conclusive evidence that the bot networks were effectively disrupted.18 The lack of corroborating evidence from independent analysts or from Ukrainian cyber‑defence units leaves the claim unverified.19</p> <h3>Russian silence – deliberate strategy or lack of evidence?</h3> <p>In the absence of an official response, analysts have looked for indirect indicators of Russian cyber activity. A study by the Atlantic Council’s Digital Forensics and Cyber Intelligence Centre (DFICC) noted a temporary dip in Russian botnet traffic following the alleged strikes, but the data is inconclusive and could be attributed to other factors such as routine maintenance or unrelated cyber‑operations.20 Russian cyber‑security agencies have not issued any statements acknowledging or refuting the claim, a silence that some interpret as a deliberate strategy to avoid legitimizing the Ukrainian narrative or to conceal ongoing counter‑cyber operations.21</p> <h3>International reactions – donors, media, and public perception</h3> <p>From a Ukrainian perspective, the claim serves to rally domestic support and to signal to international donors that the country is actively countering Russian cyber threats. Kyiv Post reported that Ukrainian officials framed the strikes as evidence of “digital resilience” and a key component of the nation’s defence strategy.22</p> <p>Donor governments, particularly in the United States and the European Union, have been quick to endorse the narrative, citing it as justification for continued cyber‑defence aid and training programs. The Times of India highlighted that such claims can influence funding decisions, noting that “public perception of cyber‑success can sway political will to allocate resources.”23</p> <p>International media coverage has varied. The Financial Times discussed how such claims could influence funding decisions, while Mashable provided a technical overview of bot networks, explaining how DDoS attacks can overload servers but may not necessarily dismantle the underlying bot infrastructure if redundancy is in place.24 The London Economic added that the narrative of cyber‑victory can be used to maintain morale among the Ukrainian populace and to keep the war narrative focused on resilience rather than loss.25</p> <h3>Legal and ethical considerations</h3> <p>Targeting data‑centres that may host civilian services raises questions about proportionality and legality under international law. The Tallinn Manual 2.0, a scholarly work on the application of international law to cyber warfare, states that attacks on civilian infrastructure are prohibited unless the target is a legitimate military objective.26 Mother Jones highlighted that data‑centres often provide critical infrastructure for both government and private sectors, and that indiscriminate strikes could disrupt essential services such as healthcare, finance, and communications. This raises questions about the proportionality and legality of such attacks under international humanitarian law.27</p> <h3>What to watch in the coming weeks</h3> <p><strong>Independent technical assessments.</strong> The veracity of Zelensky’s claim remains unverified. Neutral cybersecurity researchers or international bodies could provide clarity on whether the bot networks were indeed disrupted.</p> <p><strong>Potential escalation.</strong> If Russia perceives the strikes as a direct threat to its cyber‑infrastructure, it may respond with its own targeted attacks against Ukrainian data‑centres or other critical assets, potentially spilling over into civilian infrastructure.</p> <p><strong>Political ramifications for donor governments.</strong> The narrative of cyber‑victory can influence public opinion and policy decisions regarding funding for Ukraine’s cyber‑defence. Monitoring how donor agencies adjust their budgets and training programs in response to such claims will shed light on the interplay between information warfare and real‑world support.</p> <p><strong>Legal and ethical frameworks.</strong> The international community will need to grapple with questions about the legitimacy of targeting data‑centres that may host civilian services, the proportionality of cyber‑attacks, and the accountability mechanisms for state‑backed cyber‑operations.</p> <h3>Conclusion</h3> <p>Zelensky’s assertion that Ukrainian data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bot networks has resonated with many, but the lack of independent verification and the technical challenges inherent in dismantling distributed bot architectures mean that the claim should be treated with cautious scrutiny. The unfolding dynamics of cyber‑warfare in the Ukraine conflict will continue to test the limits of digital resilience, strategic communication, and international law. As the world watches, the next few weeks will be crucial in determining whether the Ukrainian narrative holds up under technical scrutiny and whether it shapes the future of cyber‑defence support for the country.</p>

Background & context (from research brief)

<h2>Ukraine claims cyber‑strikes crippled Russian bot networks – what’s the evidence?</h2>

<p>On 12 April, President Volodymyr Zelensky addressed the nation in a televised speech, announcing that Ukrainian cyber‑defence forces had struck several data‑centres that allegedly hosted command‑and‑control (C&C) servers for Russian bot networks, rendering those bots inoperable. “We have taken decisive action against the digital infrastructure that Russia uses to wage war against us,” he declared, echoing earlier statements that have been used to rally domestic support and justify international aid.1</p>

<p>Ukraine’s experience with cyber warfare began in 2015 with the NotPetya attack, attributed to Russian actors. The malware crippled government systems, caused an estimated $10 billion in damage, and exposed the fragility of the country’s digital infrastructure.2 In 2022, a coordinated series of cyber‑operations targeted the Ukrainian power grid, temporarily knocking out electricity in several regions and proving that critical infrastructure can be disrupted from afar.3 These events prompted the creation of the Ukrainian Cyber‑Defence Forces in 2021 and the deployment of advanced threat‑detection systems nationwide.</p>

<p>According to the BBC, the strikes combined distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks and exploit‑based intrusions that seized control of target servers.4 The Guardian echoed this, noting that the operations were part of a broader “cyber‑offensive” aimed at undermining Russian military communications.5 France 24 described the attacks as “high‑profile operations” targeting servers in regions heavily used by Russian cyber actors.6 Mother Jones added that these operations are part of a long‑standing Ukrainian cyber‑strategy that dates back to NotPetya and the 2022 power‑grid attacks.7</p>
