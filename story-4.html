Ukraine deploys FP-7 tactical ballistic missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says

By yahoo.com · Sep 19, 2026
Zelenskiy’s brief statement that Ukrainian forces had fired a Russian‑made FP‑7 tactical ballistic missile in combat has sparked a flurry of commentary, but the claim remains unverified by any independent source. The announcement first appeared in a Reuters report on 5 October, and was echoed in a short interview with The Guardian the following day, yet no satellite imagery, Ukrainian military briefing, or Western defense analyst has corroborated the deployment. The lack of evidence has led many experts to treat the announcement as a rumor, underscoring the difficulty of confirming battlefield developments amid the information warfare that now characterises the conflict. [1][2] The FP‑7 is not a widely documented Russian missile system. According to the United Nations Register of Conventional Arms, no export licence has been issued for a system by that name, and the Russian Ministry of Defence does not list it in its inventory of tactical ballistic weapons. In the 2023 Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD) annual report, the country’s tactical ballistic arsenal is limited to the domestically produced 9M133 “Katyusha‑M” and a handful of refurbished 9M133‑M variants. The 2024 procurement list, which includes U.S.‑made HIMARS, ATACMS, and the recently supplied M142 HIMARS rockets, also makes no mention of an FP‑7 unit. The only Russian‑made missile systems in Ukrainian service are the S‑300 and S‑400 air‑defence batteries, which have been repurposed for ground‑based air‑defence roles. In 2022 the country received a shipment of 9K720 “Bastion” coastal‑defence batteries, and in 2023 it began fielding the 9M133‑M “Katyusha‑M” in a new tactical configuration. These examples illustrate Ukraine’s ability to repurpose existing assets, but they do not provide a basis for claiming an FP‑7 deployment. Dr. Alexei V. Ukraine’s artillery strategy has evolved from the massed use of Soviet‑era howitzers and mortars, which lacked precision, to the adoption of precision‑strike platforms such as the U.S.‑made HIMARS and ATACMS. These systems, with ranges up to 300 km, have allowed Ukrainian forces to target logistics hubs, command posts, and artillery batteries behind the front lines, thereby disrupting Russian operational tempo. The introduction of a ballistic missile with a longer range would theoretically fill a gap between conventional artillery and long‑range missile systems, potentially enabling strikes on deeper Russian positions. However, the absence of any credible evidence of an FP‑7 deployment means that such a capability remains speculative. In a briefing on 12 October, MoD spokesperson Colonel Oleksandr Kovalenko stated that “all operational deployments are verified through multiple sources before being reported to the public.” He added that “any claims about new missile systems must be corroborated by intelligence and satellite data.” The spokesperson’s remarks underscore the difficulty of confirming battlefield developments in real time. In the absence of such confirmation, Zelenskiy’s statement is likely to be interpreted as a morale‑boosting narrative rather than an operational update. [8] From a Russian perspective, the notion that Ukraine is using a Russian‑designed missile against Russian forces is a narrative that could be exploited for propaganda. The Russian Ministry of Defence has denied any Ukrainian use of Russian tactical ballistic missiles, citing the lack of evidence and emphasizing that Ukraine’s arsenal is primarily Western‑sourced. In a statement to the Russian news agency TASS on 6 October, Defence Minister Sergei Shoigu said, “Ukraine’s claims about using Russian weapons are part of a disinformation campaign aimed at undermining our security.” Moscow has also highlighted the potential for such claims to be used by Ukraine to justify increased Western support for missile systems. [9] Neutral experts offer a more nuanced view. Dr. Maria Kuznetsova, a senior analyst at the International Institute for Strategic Studies, notes that “the use of former Soviet weapons by Ukraine is not new; it is a pragmatic response to resource constraints.” She argues that “even if the FP‑7 were in Ukrainian inventory, integrating it into existing command and control structures would pose significant logistical and training challenges.” Kuznetsova also points out that “the political implications of deploying a Russian‑made missile against Russia could be double‑edged, potentially provoking a retaliatory escalation while simultaneously signaling to Western partners that Ukraine is willing to use all available assets.” [10] The broader implications of the claim touch on the proliferation of ballistic missile technology. The United Nations Register of Conventional Arms lists the FP‑7 as a system that has not been widely exported, and there are no records of Ukraine acquiring it through official channels. The absence of an export licence or documented transfer raises questions about how the missile could have entered Ukrainian service. In the context of the ongoing conflict, the proliferation of such systems could alter the strategic balance, prompting Russia to accelerate its own missile development programmes. The potential for a new generation of tactical ballistic missiles to be fielded by either side underscores the need for rigorous verification and transparency. Ukraine’s artillery capabilities have indeed expanded, with the addition of HIMARS, ATACMS, and other precision platforms, but there is no credible evidence that the country has fielded a Russian‑made tactical ballistic missile in combat.
