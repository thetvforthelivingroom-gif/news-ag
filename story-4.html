Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
On 12 April, President Volodymyr Zelensky announced that Ukrainian forces had struck data‑centre infrastructure believed to host Russian botnet command‑and‑control (C&C) nodes. The statement, made from Kyiv’s presidential palace, was quickly reported by international outlets and sparked debate about the scale and effectiveness of the strikes. While the headline suggests a decisive blow to Russia’s cyber‑operations, the reality on the ground is far more nuanced. In the weeks that followed, analysts, government agencies, and academic researchers have struggled to separate fact from propaganda, underscoring the challenges of verification in cyber‑warfare. Zelensky’s address was brief but emphatic. “We have targeted and disabled several data‑centres that were part of the Russian botnet infrastructure, effectively taking down thousands of malicious machines,” he said on television. The Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation released a recording of the speech on its official channel, and a spokesperson later confirmed that the strikes were carried out by Ukrainian cyber‑defence units in coordination with allied partners. The announcement was echoed by BBC News, France 24, and Mother Jones, all of which highlighted the timing of the claim in the context of a series of Russian cyber‑attacks on Ukrainian infrastructure earlier that month. Independent verification, however, has been limited. The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) issued a brief statement on 13 April saying it was “monitoring the situation and will provide updates if credible evidence emerges.”¹ The cybersecurity firm Mandiant released a technical note on 15 April describing traffic anomalies originating from servers in the Kharkiv region that matched signatures associated with the “BlackEnergy” botnet, but the note stopped short of confirming a direct link to the Ukrainian strikes.² Russian state media, including TASS and RT, quickly responded with denials, describing the Ukrainian claim as a propaganda ploy and asserting that no significant disruption had been observed in Russian cyber‑operations.³ The technical plausibility of a single‑point strike disrupting a botnet depends on the network’s architecture. Botnets typically rely on a hierarchy of C&C servers, but many modern botnets employ distributed, redundant nodes that can re‑route traffic if one server is taken offline. A 2021 RAND Corporation report noted that “botnets often employ distributed, redundant C&C nodes, making them resistant to single‑point failures.”⁴ Ukrainian cyber‑defence units have publicly documented the use of distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) mitigation and network segmentation to isolate compromised nodes, but no independent audit has confirmed that the specific servers targeted in the 12 April strikes were critical hubs for Russian botnet activity.⁵ From the Ukrainian perspective, the strikes are framed as a defensive necessity. Defence Minister Oleksandr Syrskyi said in a press briefing on 14 April that “cyber‑attacks are the new battlefield. We cannot afford to let our adversary use the internet as a weapon against our people.” He added that “the Ukrainian cyber‑defence strategy is built on collaboration with NATO partners, which has provided us with threat intelligence and technical support.”⁶ Russian officials, on the other hand, have emphasized resilience and denial. A Kremlin spokesperson stated that “we have robust cyber‑defence mechanisms in place, and we have not experienced any significant disruption to our operations.”⁷ International observers have called for greater transparency and verification mechanisms. A joint statement by the United Nations Office for Disarmament Affairs (UNODA) on 16 April urged both sides to adhere to the 2001 Tallinn Manual’s principles on the use of force in cyberspace and called for “greater transparency in cyber‑operations.”⁸ The European Union’s Cybersecurity Agency (ENISA) released a brief on 18 April noting that “the incident underscores the need for shared threat intelligence and coordinated response mechanisms among EU member states.”⁹ The United States reiterated its support for Ukraine’s cyber‑defence, offering “technical assistance and intelligence sharing” in a State Department statement on 20 April.¹⁰ The war in Ukraine has become a crucible for cyber‑warfare, with both sides deploying sophisticated tools. A 2022 study by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) found that “cyber‑operations now account for approximately 30 % of Russia’s overall offensive strategy against Ukraine.”¹¹ The Ukrainian cyber‑defence programme, funded by NATO and the European Union, has reportedly increased its budget by 40 % since 2020, enabling the deployment of advanced intrusion‑detection systems and threat‑intelligence platforms. The data‑centre strikes fit into this broader context as a tactical measure aimed at disrupting command and control. However, the technical reality is that botnets are notoriously resilient. Even if a single C&C node is taken offline, the botnet can reorganise and re‑establish control through alternate nodes. The real measure of success, therefore, is not the number of servers disabled but the reduction in the botnet’s operational capacity. What to watch next 1. **Independent verification** – Cyber‑security firms and academic researchers are expected to release detailed analyses of traffic logs and server status reports in the coming weeks. The presence of malware signatures, server downtime logs, and changes in botnet activity will be key indicators. 2. **Russian cyber‑responses** – Monitoring Russian state media and cyber‑defence channels will reveal whether any counter‑measures have been deployed, and whether they target Ukrainian infrastructure or broader internet services. 3. **International legal developments** – The UN and EU are likely to convene discussions on cyber‑warfare norms, potentially leading to new guidelines that could impact the legality and proportionality of data‑centre strikes. 4. **Operational impact** – Observing the effect on Russian cyber‑operations, such as changes in phishing campaigns or DDoS activity against Ukrainian targets, will help gauge the practical outcome of the strikes. In conclusion, Zelensky’s assertion that Ukrainian data‑centre raids have knocked down Russian botnets is a headline that carries both symbolic weight and technical uncertainty. The Ukrainian narrative underscores a growing confidence in cyber‑defence capabilities, while Russian denials highlight the challenges of attribution and verification in the cyber domain. Independent technical assessments, such as those from Mandiant and RAND, suggest that while the strikes may have disrupted certain nodes, the overall resilience of botnets means that a single‑point failure is unlikely to cripple the entire network. The international community’s response—ranging from NATO support to UN calls for transparency—reflects the broader debate over the legality and ethics of cyber‑operations in armed conflict. As the war continues to evolve, the line between defensive and offensive cyber‑actions will blur further, demanding clearer norms and more robust verification mechanisms. Whether Zelensky’s claim holds up under scrutiny remains to be seen, but it already serves as a reminder of the complex interplay between technology, strategy, and information in modern warfare.
