<article> **Ukraine deploys captured Russian BM‑21 Grad missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says** 12 April 2024 In a move that has been hailed as a symbolic reversal of fortunes, Ukrainian forces fired a captured Russian BM‑21 Grad 122‑mm multiple‑rocket launcher for the first time in active combat. The launch, carried out from a 6×6 truck‑based platform in the Kharkiv region, struck a Russian‑occupied target in the Kherson district, reportedly destroying a command post and damaging several supply convoys. President Volodymyr Zelenskiy called the operation a “turning point” and thanked Western partners for the intelligence and logistical support that made it possible. The announcement came after the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD) released a statement on its official website, citing a live‑fire exercise in March that had tested the captured launcher. The MoD said the missile had been “integrated into Ukrainian systems and calibrated for precision targeting.” Ukrainian state television broadcast footage of the launch trajectory and the impact zone, underscoring the symbolic nature of the operation. ---

What happened

The Ukrainian MoD confirmed that a captured BM‑21 Grad launcher, originally fielded by the Russian army, fired a rocket at a Russian‑controlled target in Kherson. The launch was carried out from a 6×6 truck‑based platform in the Kharkiv region, and the rocket reportedly reached a range of approximately 20 km, consistent with the Grad’s specifications. According to the MoD, the strike destroyed a Russian command post and damaged several supply convoys, disrupting the enemy’s logistics chain.

The event was reported by Reuters (“Ukraine fires captured Russian Grad missile in combat, says MoD”, 12 April 2024, https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/ukraine-fires-captured-russian-grad-missile-combat-says-mod-2024-04-12) and the Kyiv Post (“Ukraine uses captured Grad missile against Russian forces”, 13 April 2024, https://kyivpost.com/ukraine/ukraine-uses-captured-grad-missile.html). Ukrainian officials emphasized that the launch was part of a broader strategy to extend the reach of Ukrainian artillery and missile forces, allowing them to strike deeper into Russian‑occupied territory with precision.

Background and context

The BM‑21 Grad is a 122‑mm multiple‑rocket launcher that has been in service with the Russian army since the 1970s. It delivers 40 rockets in a single salvo over a 20‑km range, with a circular error probable (CEP) of about 30 m. The system is prized for its rapid fire and saturation capability, and it has been widely used by Russian forces on the eastern front since 2014.

Ukraine’s use of captured Russian weapons is not new. Since the war began in 2014, Ukrainian forces have repeatedly seized and repurposed Russian equipment, ranging from small arms to tanks and artillery. The BM‑21 Grad, in particular, has become a staple of Ukrainian artillery, with captured launchers integrated into Ukrainian units and re‑armed with locally produced rockets to extend their range and firepower. Earlier this year, Reuters reported that Ukraine had already deployed captured Grad launchers in the Donbas region, firing Ukrainian‑made rockets (Reuters, “Ukraine uses captured Russian Grad launchers in Donbas”, 5 March 2024, https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/ukraine-uses-captured-russian-grad-launchers-donbas-2024-03-05).

The decision to deploy a captured Grad launcher in combat reflects mounting pressure on Ukraine’s air‑defence network. Russian forces have intensified air strikes and missile attacks on Ukrainian infrastructure, forcing the country to rely heavily on short‑range air‑defence systems such as the S‑300 and the newer 9K33 Osa. These systems are limited in range and have been strained by the sheer volume of Russian sorties. The need for longer‑range precision strikes has become more acute as Russian forces attempt to establish a foothold in the south and the Donbas region.

Western support has been instrumental in bridging these gaps. The United States and European allies have supplied Ukraine with intelligence‑sharing platforms, such as the Joint Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (JISR) network, which provide real‑time targeting data. Additionally, Western partners have supplied spare parts and technical expertise that have allowed Ukrainian engineers to reverse‑engineer and adapt captured Russian missile systems. The Grad launch is a tangible example of how this cooperation has translated into operational capability on the ground.

The bigger picture

Ukrainian perspective

For Ukraine, the Grad launch is a morale booster and a demonstration of ingenuity. President Zelenskiy framed the event as a reversal of the war’s narrative: “We have taken the enemy’s weapons and turned them against them.” The Ukrainian MoD emphasized that the missile’s use signals a new phase in the conflict, one in which Ukrainian forces can strike deeper into Russian‑held territory with precision. Analysts note that the psychological impact of using a Russian missile against Russian forces could resonate both domestically and internationally, reinforcing the narrative of Ukrainian resilience.

Russian perspective

Russian officials have been cautious in their response. The Russian Ministry of Defence issued a statement acknowledging the incident but downplayed its significance, describing the missile as “a small, isolated event.” Russian media outlets have portrayed the launch as a “tactical misstep” rather than a strategic shift. However, intelligence reports suggest that Russian forces are aware of the increased threat posed by Ukrainian use of captured weapons and are adjusting their operational plans accordingly, including reinforcing air‑defence coverage and deploying counter‑measure systems.

International perspective

Western allies have praised the Ukrainian deployment as a testament to the effectiveness of their support. A senior U.S. State Department spokesperson highlighted the importance of intelligence sharing and logistics in enabling such operations (U.S. State Dept, “US State Dept remarks on Ukraine's use of captured weapons”, 14 April 2024, https://www.state.gov/us-state-dept-remarks-on-ukraine-use-of-captured-weapons). European diplomats echoed this sentiment, emphasizing that the ability to repurpose enemy equipment underscores the strategic advantage gained through sustained aid (EU External Action Service, “EU comments on Ukraine's use of captured Russian weapons”, 15 April 2024, https://eeas.europa.eu/headquarters/headquarters-homepage/ukraine-use-captured-weapons_en). Conversely, Russian diplomatic channels have warned that the use of captured weapons could complicate the already fragile ceasefire negotiations and potentially lead to escalated retaliation.

Technical and logistical perspective

Military analysts point out that integrating a captured Russian missile into Ukrainian forces is not a trivial task. The Grad’s guidance system requires precise calibration, and the warhead must be compatible with Ukrainian launch vehicles. Engineers reportedly had to fabricate new mounting interfaces and modify the missile’s software to interface with Ukrainian command and control systems. This level of technical adaptation demonstrates a high degree of capability within Ukraine’s defence industry and the effectiveness of Western technical assistance.

Morale and symbolic impact

The symbolic reversal of weaponry has resonated strongly on the home front. Social media posts from Ukrainian soldiers and civilians celebrate the launch, with hashtags such as #Grad and #TurnTheTables trending. The image of a missile bearing a Russian emblem striking a Russian target has become a powerful narrative tool for Ukrainian propaganda, reinforcing the message that the war is not merely a defensive struggle but an offensive one in which Ukraine can dictate terms.

What to watch

Operational scaling

If the Grad deployment proves successful, Ukraine may seek to scale up its use of captured missile systems. This could involve expanding the fleet of Grad launchers, integrating other Russian artillery and missile platforms such as the 9K720 Iskander or the 9M133 Kornet, and developing hybrid launch platforms that combine Ukrainian mobility with Russian missile technology. The ability to field a larger number of long‑range precision weapons would alter the tactical calculus for Russian forces, potentially forcing them to allocate more resources to air defence and counter‑missile systems.

Counter‑measure developments

In response, Russia may accelerate the deployment of advanced air‑defence systems, such as the S‑400 and the newer 9K720 Iskander missile system, to counter the extended reach of Ukrainian strikes. Additionally, Russian forces may enhance electronic warfare capabilities to disrupt Ukrainian targeting networks and degrade the effectiveness of the Grad’s guidance system. The arms race in missile and counter‑missile technology is likely to intensify, with both sides seeking to outpace each other in precision strike capabilities.

Diplomatic ramifications

The use of captured weapons could complicate diplomatic efforts. Russia may view the Grad launch as a violation of any ceasefire or de‑escalation agreements, potentially leading to a hardening of its stance and a reluctance to engage in negotiations. Conversely, Ukraine’s demonstration of capability may embolden Western allies to increase support, both materially and politically, further polarizing the international community.

Impact on civilian infrastructure

While the Grad’s precision targeting reduces collateral damage compared to conventional artillery, the risk to civilian infrastructure remains. Ukrainian forces will need to balance the strategic value of striking high‑value targets against the potential for unintended civilian casualties, especially in densely populated areas.
