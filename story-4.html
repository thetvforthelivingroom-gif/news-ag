Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Ukraine claims it has disabled Russian botnet infrastructure by striking data centres</h1> <p>On 14 April 2024, President Volodymyr Zelensky announced that Ukrainian cyber‑forces had carried out a series of “precision strikes” against Russian data‑centres that host botnets used for information‑operations and cyber‑espionage. He said the attacks had “cut the digital arteries feeding the enemy’s propaganda machine” and that the botnet command‑and‑control servers were now offline. The statement was echoed by the Ministry of Defence, which released a brief video montage of screenshots showing Russian servers going down.</p> <p>Reuters reported the same day that the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence had confirmed the operations but declined to disclose technical details, citing ongoing investigations. The outlet noted that the attacks were part of a broader strategy to degrade Russia’s cyber‑infrastructure, a strategy that has evolved since the annexation of Crimea in 2014 [1].</p> <p>Russian officials dismissed the claim. A spokesperson for the Russian Ministry of Defence issued a statement on 15 April 2024 saying that the country’s cyber‑defence systems remained fully operational and that the alleged attacks were “unsubstantiated rumours” designed to sow confusion among the Ukrainian population. The Russian press agency TASS echoed this sentiment, noting that “no evidence has been presented to prove that any critical infrastructure was affected” [2].</p> <p>Independent fact‑checking outlets added a layer of scepticism. Snopes released a brief analysis on 16 April 2024, noting that while there were reports of increased traffic disruptions in certain Russian regions, no definitive proof linked those outages to the Ukrainian strikes [3].</p> <h2>Technical details of the operation</h2> <p>According to a statement from the Ukrainian cyber‑defence unit “Cyber Army of Ukraine,” the attacks involved coordinated distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) floods combined with malware injections designed to corrupt server configurations. The operation reportedly targeted three major cloud‑hosting providers that host Russian government and military servers: Yandex.Cloud, Mail.ru, and a Russian data‑centre in Moscow operated by the Ministry of Defence’s cyber‑operations unit, the 8th Directorate of the General Staff. However, no independent verification of these targets has been published, and the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence has not released a detailed after‑action report [4].</p> <p>Because no post‑incident analysis has been made public by a third‑party firm such as Mandiant, the technical specifics of the malware and the extent of the disruption remain unverified. Ukrainian officials have described the malware as a variant of the “Spearphishing Dropper” that had been used in previous operations, but this claim has not been corroborated by external security researchers [5].</p> <h2>Historical context</h2> <p>Ukraine’s cyber‑offensive capabilities have grown steadily since 2014. Early operations focused on disrupting Russian military communications and intelligence gathering. By 2019, Ukrainian cyber‑units were conducting coordinated attacks against Russian state media, financial institutions, and government websites, often using sophisticated phishing campaigns and zero‑day exploits. The 2022 escalation saw a surge in cyber‑attacks, with Ukrainian forces reportedly using botnets to flood Russian websites and interfere with election monitoring systems [6].</p> <p>Botnets—networks of compromised computers controlled by a single operator—have been a staple of Russian information warfare. They enable large‑scale dissemination of disinformation, automated trolling, and the execution of distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks. A 2023 report by the Ukrainian intelligence agency NBU identified several botnet clusters that were linked to the Russian Ministry of Defence’s cyber‑operations unit [7].</p> <h2>Perspectives from the parties involved</h2> <h3>Ukrainian narrative</h3> <p>From the Ukrainian perspective, the data‑centre strikes represent a “strategic win” in the cyber domain. The Ministry of Defence released a short video montage on 18 April 2024 showing screenshots of Russian servers going offline, accompanied by a voice‑over that praised the cyber‑forces’ “precision and effectiveness.” Zelensky’s own statement framed the attacks as a necessary countermeasure to “protect Ukrainian sovereignty” and to “neutralise the threat of Russian cyber‑propaganda” [8].</p> <p>The Ukrainian government also emphasised the legal framework underpinning the operations. A statement from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs cited the 2015 National Cyber‑Security Strategy, which outlines Ukraine’s defensive posture and the need to protect critical information infrastructure. The strategy does not authorise offensive cyber‑operations; rather, it calls for the development of defensive capabilities and international cooperation to counter cyber threats [9].</p> <h3>Russian counter‑claims</h3> <p>Russian officials have framed the situation as an example of “unlawful aggression” aimed at destabilising the region. The Russian Ministry of Defence’s spokesperson highlighted that the attacks, if real, would violate international law by targeting infrastructure that may serve civilian purposes. The Russian side also pointed to the lack of independent verification, arguing that the Ukrainian claims were part of a “propaganda campaign” designed to boost morale on the home front [10].</p> <p>In a televised interview on 20 April 2024, a senior Russian cyber‑defence officer from the Federal Security Service (FSB) warned that the attacks could trigger a “cyber‑retaliation” that might affect Russian citizens’ access to essential services. He also emphasised that the Russian government had already begun to reinforce its data‑centre security posture, citing increased investment in redundant systems and advanced intrusion detection [11].</p> <h3>International legal and ethical considerations</h3> <p>Lawfare’s analysis of the incident highlighted the grey area in international law regarding cyber‑attacks on data‑centres that may host both military and civilian services. The principle of distinction requires attackers to avoid civilian harm, but the dual‑use nature of many data‑centres complicates the assessment of proportionality. The United Nations Group of Governmental Experts on Cyber‑Conflict has not yet produced a consensus on the legality of such strikes [12].</p> <p>The European Union’s Cyber‑Security Agency (ENISA) issued a brief advisory urging member states to review their own data‑centre security protocols in light of the incident. The advisory stressed that “the line between military and civilian infrastructure is increasingly blurred, and that cyber‑attacks can have far‑reaching consequences for civilian populations.” While the advisory does not specifically mention the Ukrainian strikes, it reflects broader concerns about the resilience of critical infrastructure [13].</p> <h3>Media coverage and public perception</h3> <p>Reuters and Associated Press covered the incident with a focus on the political implications and the lack of independent verification. BBC News reported that Ukrainian officials had not yet provided technical evidence, and that the Russian Ministry of Defence had denied any damage. The coverage emphasised the difficulty of attributing cyber‑attacks in a hybrid warfare environment [14].</p> <p>Radio Free Europe’s coverage focused on the potential collateral damage, interviewing Ukrainian civilians in regions where internet service was reportedly disrupted. The report highlighted that while the attacks were aimed at Russian infrastructure, some Ukrainian users experienced temporary outages due to shared network routes. The piece concluded that “the risk of unintended civilian impact is a real concern in any cyber‑offensive operation” [15].</p> <h2>Future implications</h2> <h3>Attribution challenges</h3> <p>The incident underscores the inherent difficulty of attributing cyber‑attacks in a hybrid warfare environment. Attackers can route traffic through compromised servers in third‑country jurisdictions, use anonymising services, and employ malware that mimics legitimate software. These tactics blur the line between state‑backed operations and non‑state actors, complicating both legal accountability and strategic deterrence [16].</p> <h3>Collateral damage and civilian infrastructure</h3> <p>While Ukrainian officials claim that the strikes were precise, independent reports suggest that disruptions may have extended beyond the intended targets. The dual‑use nature of cloud infrastructure means that civilian services—such as banking, healthcare, and emergency communications—could be inadvertently affected. This risk may influence future policy decisions regarding the use of cyber‑operations in conflict zones [17].</p> <h3>Legal and normative developments</h3> <p>The incident may prompt a reevaluation of international cyber‑law. If the attacks are deemed to violate the principles of distinction and proportionality, they could set a precedent for future cyber‑offensives. Conversely, if Ukraine’s claims are substantiated, it could reinforce the legitimacy of state‑backed cyber‑attacks against hostile infrastructure. The United Nations and the Organization for Security and Co‑Operation in Europe (OSCE) may take up the issue, potentially leading to new guidelines or treaties addressing cyber‑conflict [18].</p> <h3>Impact on global cloud infrastructure</h3> <p>The targeting of major cloud‑hosting providers raises questions about the resilience of global data‑centres. The incident may spur increased investment in redundancy, geographic diversification, and advanced threat‑intelligence capabilities. Cloud providers may also reassess their terms of service regarding military use of their platforms, potentially leading to stricter controls or new compliance requirements for government clients [19].</p> <h3>Retaliation and escalation</h3> <p>Both sides have warned that a cyber‑attack could trigger a cycle of retaliation. Ukrainian officials have called for a coordinated international response to prevent escalation, while Russian officials have pledged to enhance their cyber‑defence capabilities. The risk of a broader cyber‑conflict underscores the need for clear norms and rapid communication channels between states to de‑escalate incidents [20].</p> <p>In the months that follow, analysts will monitor whether the alleged strikes have had a lasting impact on Russian cyber‑operations and whether the Ukrainian cyber‑forces can sustain such offensive campaigns without provoking a larger cyber‑retaliation. The outcome will shape the future of cyber‑warfare in the region and inform global policy debates on the legitimacy and proportionality of state‑level cyber‑attacks.</p>
