Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

By BBC · Sep 19, 2026
<article> President Volodymyr Zelensky’s March 12 tweet that a Ukrainian strike on a data‑centre had “silenced a swarm of Russian bots” instantly became headline news, sparking debate over the reality of cyber‑operations and the limits of verification. The post was accompanied by a short clip of a Ukrainian drone hovering over a darkened facility in the Kharkiv region, with a voice‑over claiming the strike had destroyed the infrastructure powering the enemy’s botnet. While the image was striking, the claim raises questions about the nature of cyber‑warfare, the legal status of dual‑use targets, and the role of propaganda in modern conflict. The Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation (MDT) issued a press briefing the day after the tweet. It stated that the target was a data‑centre operated by an entity linked to Russian cyber‑operators. According to the MDT, the facility housed command‑and‑control (C&C) servers that coordinated distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks against Ukrainian critical infrastructure. The ministry cited “intelligence reports” indicating that the data‑centre was “critical to the enemy’s cyber‑offensive capability” and that the strike had “disabled the botnet’s ability to coordinate attacks,” citing a drop in DDoS traffic observed by Ukrainian network operators in the hours that followed. The ministry’s brief was supported by a technical note released by the National Cyber Security Center (NCSC) on March 13, describing the attack as a “high‑precision kinetic strike” that destroyed the data‑centre’s main server room. The note claimed that the destruction of the servers caused the C&C infrastructure to go offline, effectively “breaking the chain of control” for the bots. It also mentioned that Ukrainian forces had “confirmed the loss of the target’s operational capacity” through post‑attack reconnaissance. Independent verification of the claim remains elusive. Major cyber‑security firms such as Mandiant, FireEye, and CrowdStrike have not published any analysis confirming the loss of the botnet or the destruction of the data‑centre. Likewise, Russian cyber‑defence analysts and the Russian Ministry of Defence (MoD) issued statements denying any significant loss of botnet infrastructure. On March 14, the Russian MoD posted a brief on X claiming that “the alleged attack had no measurable impact on our cyber‑operations” and that the data‑centre in question was a civilian facility with no strategic value. The absence of third‑party verification is not unusual in cyber‑warfare, where clandestine operations and the difficulty of accessing damaged infrastructure often leave analysts with only the claims of the parties involved. Historical context helps frame the current debate. The first widely publicised state‑backed cyber‑attack, Stuxnet, struck Iranian nuclear facilities in 2007, revealing the potential of cyber‑operations to inflict physical damage. In 2007, the United States also carried out Operation Orchard, a missile strike on a Syrian air‑defence site, which was later confirmed by satellite imagery from private companies such as Orbital Insight. In the cyber domain, the 2016 U.S. election saw Russian bots amplify political messaging and sow discord, while a 2018 Russian cyber‑attack on the Ukrainian power grid was later confirmed by a U.S. Department of Energy report that detailed the disruption of the attack’s C&C servers. These precedents illustrate both the strategic value of targeting cyber‑infrastructure and the challenges of verifying such strikes. Botnets—networks of compromised computers controlled by a single C&C server—have been a staple of Russian influence operations for years. A typical botnet architecture relies on a hierarchical structure: a central server issues commands to a tier of “middle‑men” servers, which then distribute instructions to the individual bots. The C&C servers often use custom protocols or standard HTTP/HTTPS traffic to blend in with legitimate traffic, making detection difficult. Counter‑measures include sinkholing (redirecting traffic to a benign server), distributed denial‑of‑service mitigation, and the use of decentralized peer‑to‑peer C&C architectures that reduce single points of failure. The Ukrainian claim that a single strike could disrupt an entire botnet hinges on the assumption that the targeted data‑centre housed a central C&C hub, a scenario that is plausible but not guaranteed. International law offers limited guidance on cyber‑operations that target dual‑use infrastructure. The principles of distinction and proportionality under the Geneva Conventions require that combatants distinguish between military and civilian targets and that the anticipated military advantage outweighs potential civilian harm. The United Nations Group of Governmental Experts (UN GGE) on cyber‑war, in its 2015 report, emphasized that attacks on civilian infrastructure are prohibited unless the target is a legitimate military objective. In the Kharkiv case, the Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation argued that the data‑centre’s role in hosting a botnet made it a legitimate target, while Russian officials countered that the facility also provided services to local businesses and that the strike could have collateral damage. The lack of a clear international legal framework for cyber‑attacks on dual‑use infrastructure complicates the assessment of the strike’s legality. Propaganda and information warfare play a significant role in shaping public perception of cyber‑operations. Zelensky’s statement, delivered in a tone of triumph, was likely intended to boost morale at home and signal to international partners that Ukraine can strike back in cyberspace. Russian officials framed the claim as a propaganda ploy. In a televised interview on March 15, the Russian Deputy Minister of Digital Development said, “We have no evidence that our botnet has been compromised. The Ukrainian claim is a classic example of information warfare designed to undermine our cyber‑defence.” Independent analysts have pointed out that the Ukrainian brief lacked technical details such as the type of missile used, the exact coordinates of the target, or evidence of the servers’ destruction. Without such details, it is difficult for external observers to assess the likelihood that the strike achieved its stated objective. Verification challenges in cyber‑war stem from the invisible nature of damage, the rapid relocation of infrastructure, and the saturation of misinformation. Satellite imagery, open‑source intelligence (OSINT), and technical analysis of network traffic have historically provided independent confirmation. For example, after the 2018 Russian cyber‑attack on the Ukrainian power grid, a U.S. Department of Energy report confirmed the disruption of the attack’s C&C servers. In the Kharkiv case, no such report has been released, and the Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation has not provided satellite imagery or other independent evidence. A 2023 report by the Institute for Security Studies noted that “the absence of third‑party verification is a common feature of cyber‑attacks, making it difficult to distinguish fact from propaganda.” Collateral damage remains a concern. The Kharkiv data‑centre is located in a region that hosts both commercial and residential buildings. Ukrainian officials have stated that the strike was designed to minimize collateral damage, but the lack of independent assessment leaves the question open. If the data‑centre housed civilian servers, the destruction could have disrupted local internet services, affecting businesses and residents. Russian officials have suggested that the strike may have caused unintended damage, though they have not provided evidence to support this claim. The potential for collateral damage raises questions about the proportionality of the strike. If the facility’s civilian services were disrupted, the Ukrainian claim of a “clean” cyber‑strike would be weakened. Conversely, if the facility was indeed a purely military asset, the strike would be more defensible under international law. The incident has several implications for the broader conflict and for the future of cyber‑warfare. First, the lack of independent verification underscores the need for greater transparency in cyber‑operations. International bodies, such as the UN GGE, could develop mechanisms for third‑party verification of cyber‑attacks, similar to the verification regimes used for conventional weapons. Such mechanisms could involve satellite imagery, technical forensic analysis, or third‑party reporting of network traffic changes. Second, Russia’s denial of any significant loss of botnet infrastructure suggests that the country may have already shifted its operations elsewhere or is preparing counter‑measures. Analysts predict that Russia could respond by deploying more resilient botnet architectures, such as decentralized peer‑to‑peer C&C servers, making future attacks more difficult to neutralise. Third, the incident highlights the gaps in international law regarding cyber‑attacks on dual‑use infrastructure. States may push for clearer definitions of what constitutes a legitimate cyber‑target, especially when civilian services are involved. The UN GGE could play a pivotal role in shaping such norms, ensuring that future cyber‑operations are conducted within a predictable legal framework that balances military necessity with civilian protection.
