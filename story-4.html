Private spacecraft falls back to Earth after failing to rescue aging NASA telescope

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
A private spacecraft launched from Cape Canaveral on Tuesday failed to reach orbit, igniting a fresh debate over the safety and oversight of commercial partners tasked with supporting NASA’s aging scientific assets. The vehicle, a Dragon 2 capsule aboard a Falcon 9, was on a test flight intended to validate autonomous rendezvous and docking procedures that could, in the future, enable commercial servicing of the Hubble Space Telescope (HST). The launch abort, confirmed by telemetry from NASA’s Mission Operations Center, left the company scrambling to investigate a loss of attitude control that occurred just 90 seconds after liftoff. The failure has prompted NASA officials to re‑examine the agency’s reliance on commercial contractors for critical missions. “We’re proud of the progress we’ve made with commercial partners, but this incident reminds us that the stakes are high when we’re dealing with assets that have been in orbit for decades and have delivered thousands of peer‑reviewed papers,” said Dr. Maria R. Lopez, NASA’s Deputy Administrator, in a statement released later that day.^1

What Went Wrong?

Telemetry data released by NASA shows that the Falcon 9’s second‑stage engine shut down prematurely, causing a rapid loss of velocity. The Dragon 2’s attitude control system failed to correct the trajectory, and the capsule re‑entered the atmosphere over the Atlantic Ocean, burning up before any debris could reach the surface. The anomaly was captured by multiple ground‑based radar systems and confirmed by the SpaceX flight controller team, who reported that the anomaly occurred during the second‑stage burn.^2

SpaceX spokesperson Alex Kim confirmed that the company had obtained the necessary launch license from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and had completed the required certification process with NASA for the test flight. “We’re committed to learning from this event and ensuring that our systems meet the highest safety standards,” Kim said.³ The company has pledged to conduct a full investigation, including a review of the propulsion system’s design and the flight control software.

The Hubble Connection

The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, has been serviced by the Space Shuttle on five occasions—STS‑61 in 1993, STS‑82 in 1997, STS‑103 in 1999, STS‑109 in 2002, and STS‑125 in 2009. NASA’s own records show that no private spacecraft has ever attempted to rescue or replace Hubble’s propulsion system.⁴ The current test flight was intended as a proof‑of‑concept for a commercial rescue mission that would, in theory, extend Hubble’s operational life by delivering a new propulsion module. However, the agency has not yet authorized a private rescue mission for Hubble, and the test was conducted solely as a technology demonstration.⁵

The Hubble’s scientific output has been monumental: it has contributed to the discovery of exoplanets, the measurement of the universe’s expansion rate, and the study of dark energy. A 2020 review in *Nature Astronomy* noted that Hubble has produced over 1,200 peer‑reviewed papers and has been cited more than 50,000 times.⁶ The loss of Hubble would have a profound impact on the global scientific community, underscoring the urgency of reliable servicing options.

Oversight and Regulation

The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation issued a launch license for the Dragon 2 test flight in 2023, following a rigorous review of the company’s safety plan and environmental impact assessment. The Department of Defense (DoD) also provided a certification of compliance with national security requirements, as the launch site is located near a military installation.^7

Despite these approvals, the incident has spurred calls for tighter debris mitigation guidelines. FAA Administrator John D. Smith emphasized that “the re‑entry of a failed spacecraft over a populated ocean raises legitimate concerns about debris risk.”⁸ He cited the FAA’s 2022 guidance on debris mitigation, which requires commercial operators to design vehicles that minimize the risk of surviving debris reaching the surface.^9

NASA’s own contingency plan for aging spacecraft, detailed in a 2021 internal memorandum, outlines several scenarios for extending the life of critical assets. The plan includes options for in‑house rescue missions, the use of commercial partners, and the development of autonomous servicing technologies.¹⁰ The current failure has prompted the agency to revisit the risk assessments associated with each option.

Stakeholder Perspectives

NASA officials have expressed both confidence in commercial partnerships and concern over the adequacy of contractual safeguards. “We need clear performance guarantees and liability clauses that protect both the agency and the public,” said Dr. Lopez.¹

SpaceX’s leadership has framed the incident as an “unforeseen technical issue” and has pledged to share the findings of its investigation with NASA and the FAA. “We’re committed to transparency and to working closely with our partners to improve safety,” Kim said.³

Scientists who rely on Hubble data have voiced frustration over the uncertainty surrounding the observatory’s future. Dr. Elena Garcia, an astrophysicist at the University of California, Berkeley, noted that “any delay in servicing could mean a loss of critical data for ongoing research projects.”¹¹

Independent safety experts have called for a more robust regulatory framework. Dr. Raj Patel, a professor of aerospace engineering at MIT, warned that “commercial operators must demonstrate a higher level of reliability before they are entrusted with servicing missions that could jeopardize national scientific assets.”¹² Environmental groups also raised concerns about the potential for debris to contaminate marine ecosystems. The Oceanic Conservation Alliance issued a statement urging the FAA to enforce stricter re‑entry protocols.¹³

Broader Implications for Commercial Spaceflight

The incident highlights the growing tension between the rapid expansion of commercial spaceflight and the need for stringent oversight. While the commercial sector has delivered cost savings and innovation, the Dragon 2 failure underscores that the margin for error shrinks as missions become more complex and critical.

Debris mitigation has emerged as a central issue. The FAA’s 2022 guidance requires operators to design vehicles that minimize the risk of surviving debris reaching the surface, but the guidance is voluntary. Critics argue that a mandatory debris‑mitigation standard is necessary to protect both people and the environment. The DoD’s compliance certification, meanwhile, focuses on national security rather than debris risk, leaving a regulatory gap that could be filled by a joint FAA‑DoD policy.

The future of in‑orbit servicing also hinges on the success of autonomous docking and rendezvous technologies. SpaceX’s Dragon 2 is one of several commercial platforms—such as Northrop Grumman’s Cygnus and Boeing’s Starliner—that are developing autonomous servicing capabilities. NASA’s 2021 memorandum indicates that autonomous servicing could reduce mission costs by up to 30 percent, but the technology remains in the testing phase. The Dragon 2 failure may delay the deployment of autonomous servicing for Hubble, but it also provides valuable data that could accelerate the maturation of the technology.

Looking Ahead

NASA’s contingency plan now includes a “risk‑adjusted” approach to selecting commercial partners for future servicing missions. The agency will require detailed risk assessments, independent audits, and a clear liability framework before approving any private rescue mission. SpaceX, for its part, is revising its flight‑control software and is exploring hardware redundancy to mitigate the risk of a second‑stage engine anomaly.

The Hubble Space Telescope remains in orbit, but its future is uncertain. NASA’s 2021 memorandum acknowledges that the telescope’s propulsion system will likely need replacement by the early 2030s. The agency is actively exploring both in‑house and commercial options, but the Dragon 2 failure has made it clear that any commercial solution must meet rigorous safety and reliability standards.

In the meantime, the scientific community is rallying to maximize the remaining operational life of Hubble. Researchers are prioritizing observations that can be completed before the telescope’s propulsion system fails, and data‑analysis teams are working to extract maximum value from existing datasets.

The Dragon 2 incident serves as a sobering reminder that the promise of commercial spaceflight must be matched by equally robust oversight. As the commercial sector pushes the boundaries of what is possible in space, regulators, scientists, and the public must demand that safety, environmental stewardship, and scientific integrity remain at the forefront of every mission.
