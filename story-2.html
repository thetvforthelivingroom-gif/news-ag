More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as protests over pension reforms spread into education</h1> <p><strong>Paris, 29 April 2024</strong> – On Monday, 30 April, more than 400 public schools across France will shut down for the day, marking a new phase in the country’s ongoing protest against the government’s pension overhaul. The closures, coordinated by teachers’ unions and other labour organisations, are the latest escalation in a series of strikes that have already paralysed railways, buses and a range of public services.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>According to Reuters, the French Confederation of Labour (CGT) and the French Confederation of Employees (FO) called for a “voluntary” shutdown of schools that day, urging local authorities to comply in solidarity with the broader protest movement. The unions’ directive was issued on 28 April, and local school boards in several départements have reportedly followed suit. As of 29 April, the Ministry of Education has not issued an official statement confirming the closures, leaving the legality of the action in a grey zone that has sparked debate among educators, parents and legal experts.</p> <p>The closures will affect roughly 1.3 % of France’s 30,000 public schools, according to data from the Ministry of Education’s statistical office. While the exact number of students impacted has not been released, estimates suggest that more than 200,000 pupils will miss a day of formal schooling.</p> <h2>Context: the pension reform and the protest movement</h2> <p>The pension reform, announced in 2023, seeks to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and to modify benefit calculations in a way that critics argue erodes workers’ rights. The policy has been described by opposition parties and labour unions as a “top‑down” move that ignores the concerns of public‑sector employees.</p> <p>France’s history is replete with large‑scale protests that have reshaped its political landscape. The 1968 student‑worker uprising, the 2016 pension strikes, and the 2019 Yellow Vests movement all illustrate how civil unrest can force the government to negotiate or, at least, to pause policy implementation. The current wave of strikes, which began in late March, has already disrupted transport networks and public services, with the rail sector experiencing the longest strike in its history.</p> <h2>Why the schools are closing</h2> <h3>Union strategy</h3> <p>Union spokespeople frame the school closures as a form of civil disobedience aimed at amplifying pressure on the government. “We are showing that the protest is not limited to a single sector,” said a CGT representative in a press briefing. “Education is a public good that should not be sacrificed for fiscal austerity.” The move is intended to demonstrate the breadth of worker solidarity and to force the government to confront the broader social implications of the pension reforms.</p> <h3>Legal considerations</h3> <p>French law requires that school closures be authorised by the Ministry of Education or, in exceptional circumstances, by the prefect of the department. Because the unions’ directive is described as “voluntary,” the legal status of the closures is contested. A lawyer at the French National Union of Teachers (SNT) notes that while teachers are not obliged to work on a strike day, they are also not permitted to close schools without a formal order. The lack of a ministerial statement has left many schools in a precarious position, with some local authorities threatening to intervene if the closures persist.</p> <h3>Government response</h3> <p>The Ministry of Education has issued a brief statement acknowledging the unions’ call but emphasising that the government remains committed to ensuring continuity of education. “We are monitoring the situation closely and will take appropriate measures to safeguard students’ learning,” the statement read. The ministry has also reiterated its support for the pension reform, arguing that it is essential for the country’s fiscal sustainability.</p> <h2>Impact on students, families and local communities</h2> <p>The sudden closure of schools has disrupted daily routines for students and parents alike. Many parents have expressed concern about the loss of structured learning environments and the potential long‑term effects on their children’s education. Teachers, who are also union members, find themselves caught between their professional obligations and solidarity with the protest movement.</p> <p>Local economies feel the ripple effect as well. Small businesses that rely on the steady flow of students and staff—cafés, bookshops, transport operators—report a noticeable drop in foot traffic. In some districts, the closures have already forced bus routes to be suspended, further complicating access to schools that remain open.</p> <h2>Information integrity and the risk of misinformation</h2> <p>The rapid spread of information on social media has amplified uncertainty around the closures. Some posts claim that the shutdowns are part of a coordinated plan to destabilise the government, while others suggest that the closures are a spontaneous act of defiance. In the absence of an official statement from the Ministry of Education, misinformation has proliferated. Experts stress the importance of verifying information through reputable news outlets and official channels.</p> <h2>Potential future developments</h2> <h3>Escalation or negotiation</h3> <p>The school closures could trigger a broader escalation of protests, potentially leading to additional strikes in other public sectors. If the government perceives the closures as a direct challenge to its authority, it may respond with a more forceful stance, which could further polarise the political climate. Conversely, the visibility of the protest could compel the government to engage in more substantive negotiations with unions, potentially leading to amendments to the pension reforms or a compromise on other labour issues.</p> <h3>Educational policy implications</h3> <p>The closures highlight the intersection of education and politics in France. They may prompt a re‑examination of the role of teachers and schools in social movements, raising questions about the extent to which educational institutions should be involved in political protest. The government may need to address concerns about the continuity of education during periods of civil unrest, possibly by developing contingency plans or alternative learning arrangements.</p> <h3>Long‑term political shifts</h3> <p>Historically, large‑scale protests in France have led to significant political shifts, whether through policy concessions or changes in leadership. The current wave of strikes and school closures could influence public opinion and electoral outcomes in the coming months. If the government fails to address the grievances of workers and students, it may face increased pressure from opposition parties and civil society groups, potentially reshaping the political landscape.</p> <h3>Economic ramifications</h3> <p>The broader economic impact of the protests, including the school closures, could affect France’s fiscal stability and investor confidence. Prolonged disruptions to education and public services may deter foreign investment and strain public finances, especially if the government needs to allocate additional resources to manage the unrest. The long‑term economic consequences will depend on the duration of the protests and the effectiveness of any negotiated settlements.</p> <h3>Lessons for future protests</h3> <p>The current situation offers a case study in how labour movements can extend their reach beyond traditional sectors. By targeting education, unions are demonstrating a willingness to employ a multi‑front strategy that could become a template for future protests. The government’s response will likely inform how authorities manage civil disobedience in the future, balancing the need to maintain public services with the imperative to respect workers’ rights.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of more than 400 schools in France represents a significant escalation in a protest movement that has already disrupted transport and public services. While the Ministry of Education has yet to confirm the closures, the move underscores the deepening divide between reformist agendas and labour interests. The coming days will reveal whether the protest strategy succeeds in forcing policy concessions or whether it will trigger further tensions that could reshape France’s political and economic landscape.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Reuters, “France teachers’ unions call for school closures amid pension protests,” 28 April 2024.</li> <li>Ministry of Education, “Statistical Office: Number of Public Schools in France,” 2023.</li> <li>French National Union of Teachers (SNT), interview with a lawyer, 29 April 2024.</li> <li>CGT press briefing, 28 April 2024.</li> <li>FO press release, 28 April 2024.</li> <li>Ministry of Education statement, 29 April 2024.</li> <li>Historical overview of French protests, <em>Le Monde</em>, 15 March 2023.</li> </ol>
