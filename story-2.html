More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France shut their doors, turning classrooms into silent witnesses of a nation’s growing frustration over a pension reform that many say threatens the fabric of public life. The closures came amid a wave of protests that has rattled the country for months, leaving students, teachers, parents, and local businesses scrambling to adjust to an abrupt halt in routine education.

What happened

The decision to close schools was announced in the early hours of Monday. Reports indicated that the shutdown would affect institutions ranging from primary schools to secondary schools in both urban and rural areas. While the Ministry of Education has not yet issued an official confirmation, the widespread nature of the closures suggests a coordinated response to the ongoing protests. According to several news outlets, the disruptions were triggered by the mobilization of teachers and other public‑sector workers who joined the larger demonstration against the pension reform. The protests, which have escalated in recent weeks, have led to a significant number of schools being closed across the country, with more than 400 institutions reportedly affected [T2].

The closures have left a sizable number of students without a place to learn. Many schools have had to cancel or postpone classes and exams. The lack of a clear timetable for when schools will reopen has added to the uncertainty, prompting parents to seek alternative childcare arrangements and teachers to grapple with the implications for their professional responsibilities [T2].

Background and context

The pension reform at the heart of the protests seeks to raise the retirement age and modify the calculation of pension benefits. Critics argue that the changes undermine workers’ rights and could disproportionately affect lower‑paid public‑sector employees. The government, meanwhile, contends that the reforms are necessary to ensure the long‑term sustainability of France’s pension system. This clash of perspectives has fueled a broader wave of dissent that has seen strikes in various public services, including transportation, healthcare, and education.

France’s recent history is replete with large‑scale protests that have targeted public policy. The 2018‑2019 pension protests and the Yellow Vests movement both saw widespread participation and significant disruptions to daily life. In those earlier episodes, schools were also closed, setting a precedent for how the state may respond to mass mobilizations. The current wave of demonstrations echoes those past moments, with a similar pattern of public‑sector workers taking collective action to influence policy debates [T2].

The pension reform debate has become a flashpoint for broader concerns about social equity, economic security, and the role of the state in safeguarding workers’ livelihoods. The protests have highlighted the tension between fiscal responsibility and the protection of public services, with the closures of schools serving as a tangible reminder of the stakes involved. The situation remains fluid, with both the government and the unions continuing to negotiate and mobilize in the coming days [T3].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

From the government’s standpoint, the pension reform is part of a broader effort to modernise France’s social security system and address demographic changes that threaten its financial viability. Officials argue that raising the retirement age and adjusting benefit calculations are necessary steps to maintain the system’s solvency. However, unions and many teachers contend that the reforms disproportionately burden public‑sector workers, many of whom have lower incomes and longer working hours. They argue that the changes erode the protections that have historically been a cornerstone of France’s welfare state.

Teachers, in particular, have expressed concerns about job security and the impact of the reforms on their working conditions. Union spokespeople have called for a halt to the reforms, demanding that the state reconsider its approach to pension policy. They have also highlighted the broader implications for public services, arguing that the reforms could undermine the quality of education if teachers are forced to work longer hours or face reduced benefits. Teachers’ unions have organised strikes and protests that have led to the closure of schools, further illustrating the intersection between labour rights and public education [T3].

Students have voiced frustration over the disruption to their learning and the uncertainty surrounding exam schedules. Many are worried about how the closures will affect their academic progress, especially those preparing for critical assessments. Parents, meanwhile, have struggled to find reliable childcare options while their children are out of school. The closures have also had a ripple effect on local businesses, particularly those that rely on school workers and students for their customer base. The economic impact of the protests extends beyond the education sector, affecting a range of community services and local economies [T3].

The Ministry of Education has not yet confirmed the closures, and the lack of official communication has added to the confusion. Some schools have reported that they will remain open if the protests subside, while others have confirmed that they will remain closed until the situation stabilises. The uncertainty has prompted many parents and teachers to seek alternative arrangements, such as online learning platforms or community centres, to ensure that students can continue their studies [T2].

What to watch (future implications)

The immediate next steps will likely involve negotiations between the government and the unions, with the possibility of further strikes or concessions. The Ministry of Education may also issue clearer guidelines on how schools will operate during the protest period, potentially offering alternative learning arrangements or flexible schedules to mitigate the impact on students and teachers.

The broader implications of the protests for France’s social security system and public services will be closely monitored by policymakers, scholars, and citizens alike.
