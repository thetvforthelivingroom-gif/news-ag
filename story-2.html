More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> On Monday, more than 400 schools across France shut their doors as teachers’ unions, in the midst of a nationwide strike over pension reforms, halted the nation’s education system. The closures, announced amid a wave of protests, left thousands of students without a classroom and raised questions about the balance between public safety and the right to strike.

What happened

The French Ministry of Education announced that more than 400 schools would be closed on Monday, citing safety and precautionary measures amid the ongoing strike by teachers’ unions. The decision was made in response to widespread protests demanding changes to the pension system that would affect teachers’ retirement benefits. The closures were part of a broader pattern of disruptions that have characterized the current labor unrest across the country. While the exact number of schools affected remains uncertain, the figure of “more than 400” was reported by several national outlets, underscoring the scale of the impact on the education sector. The strike, which has already led to the cancellation of classes in many regions, is expected to continue beyond Monday, with unions warning that further school closures could occur if negotiations stall. Background and context

The strike is rooted in a series of pension reforms introduced by the French government, which have been criticized for reducing retirement benefits and increasing the retirement age for teachers. Teachers’ unions argue that the reforms undermine the professional status of educators and threaten the quality of education. In response, unions have demanded higher pay, improved working conditions, and a rollback of the pension changes. These demands reflect broader concerns about fiscal discipline and public sector wages that have been a recurring theme in French politics. The pension reforms, part of the government’s broader strategy to address the national debt, have faced opposition from a wide range of stakeholders, including public sector workers, unions, and some political parties. [2]

The current wave of protests is not the first time that pension reforms have sparked school closures in France. In 2019, similar strikes led to the shutdown of numerous schools, disrupting the education of thousands of students. Likewise, a nationwide teacher strike in 2023 resulted in widespread school closures and highlighted the persistent tension between the government’s fiscal agenda and the interests of public sector workers. These historical precedents illustrate how pension policy can become a flashpoint for labor disputes, with the education sector often bearing the brunt of the disruptions. [3]

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

From the government’s perspective, the Ministry of Education’s decision to close schools is framed as a precautionary measure to ensure safety and maintain public order during a period of heightened protest activity. The ministry has emphasized that the closures are temporary and aimed at protecting students and staff from potential disruptions. The government’s stance reflects its broader commitment to fiscal discipline and its belief that the pension reforms are necessary to safeguard the country’s long‑term economic stability. [2]

In contrast, teachers’ unions view the closures as a direct consequence of the strike, which they see as a legitimate exercise of their right to protest against policies that they argue undermine their professional status. Union leaders have characterized the strike as a stand against a system that they believe is unfair and unsustainable. They argue that the pension reforms disproportionately affect teachers, who already face high workloads and limited career advancement opportunities. The unions’ narrative emphasizes the need for a more equitable pension system that recognizes the contributions of educators. [2]

Students and families are caught in the middle of this conflict. While some parents express concern about the disruption to their children’s education, others see the strike as a necessary step to protect the future of the education system. The closures have sparked debates about the role of public sector workers in shaping national policy and the extent to which students should be affected by political disputes. The broader public safety concerns also factor into the discussion, as authorities weigh the risks of large‑scale protests against the rights of workers to strike. [2]

What to watch (future implications)

The ongoing strike raises several key questions about the future of France’s education system and its labor relations. One major concern is whether the current wave of protests will prompt a reassessment of the pension reforms. If the government were to modify its approach, it could signal a shift toward greater flexibility in public sector compensation. Conversely, a continued stance on fiscal discipline could lead to further tensions and potentially more school closures. The outcome of negotiations will likely influence the broader trajectory of labor relations in France, as other public sector unions observe the government’s handling of the teachers’ strike. [2]
