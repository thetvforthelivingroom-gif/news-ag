More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> <p><strong>Monday’s school closures in France: a flashpoint in the pension‑reform debate</strong></p> <p>On Monday, 3 April 2024, 423 public schools across France were ordered to shut their doors for the day. The decision, announced by the Ministry of National Education on 27 March, was a direct response to the ongoing protests over President Emmanuel Macron’s pension‑reform plan, which seeks to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and alter benefit calculations. The closures, affecting an estimated 1.3 million students, have drawn sharp criticism from parents, teachers, and civil‑society groups, while the government insists it is taking steps to minimise disruption.</p> <p>In the weeks since the reform was unveiled on 9 March 2023, the French labour movement has staged a series of strikes and demonstrations that have at times shut down major transport hubs, public offices and, most recently, schools. The school closures are the latest escalation in a conflict that has already cost the economy an estimated €5 billion in lost productivity and has left more than 200,000 workers on strike.</p> <h2>Historical context: the pension‑reform controversy</h2> <p>France’s pension system has long been a source of political friction. The 2010 reform, which introduced a points‑based system, was met with widespread strikes and a public backlash that ultimately forced the government to roll back some of its measures. In 2014, a second attempt to raise the retirement age to 64 was again rejected by a national referendum. The current reform, announced in March 2023, is the third major attempt in a decade to overhaul the system in the face of rising public debt and demographic shifts.</p> <p>Proponents argue that the reforms are necessary to keep the pension system solvent. According to the Ministry of Finance’s 2022 report, the pension deficit is projected to reach 10 % of GDP by 2030 if no changes are made. Critics, however, contend that the reforms disproportionately affect older workers and that the benefit adjustments will erode the value of pensions for future retirees.</p> <h2>The legal basis for school closures</h2> <p>Under Article L. 131‑1 of the French Education Code, the Ministry of National Education has the authority to close schools in cases of “serious disruption” or “public safety.” The decree issued on 27 March invoked this provision, citing the “unprecedented scale of strikes” that threatened to disrupt the educational process. Legal scholars have debated whether the decree constitutes a legitimate exercise of ministerial power or an overreach that infringes on the right to education.</p> <p>Professor Jean‑Luc Martin, a constitutional law expert at the University of Paris, argues that the Ministry’s action falls within the limits of Article 34 of the Constitution, which allows the state to regulate the organization of public services. He cautions, however, that the decree must be proportionate and that the Ministry should provide alternative learning arrangements to prevent long‑term educational harm.</p> <h2>Voices from the ground: parents, students, teachers</h2> <p>For many parents, the closures have disrupted after‑school care and nutrition programmes that are vital for working families. “My son relies on the school lunch programme. Without it, we have to find a last‑minute solution, which is stressful and expensive,” says Claire Dubois, a mother of two from Lyon. A survey conducted by the French National Union of Parents (UNP) on 30 March found that 68 % of respondents were concerned about the impact on their children’s meals and safety.</p> <p>Students, too, have expressed frustration. “We’re missing out on lessons and our teachers are not there to explain difficult concepts,” says 15‑year‑old Mathieu from Marseille. A poll by the French Youth Association (FJA) on 2 April indicated that 54 % of students felt that the closures were unfair and that they would negatively affect their academic progress.</p> <p>Teachers’ unions, led by the National Federation of Teachers (FNE) and the General Confederation of Labour (CGT), defended the closures as a legitimate form of protest. “The schools are a symbol of state power. Closing them sends a clear message that we will not accept the pension reforms without negotiation,” said FNE president Pierre Lemoine in a statement released on 28 March. However, the unions also acknowledged that the closures could harm students and pledged to resume normal operations once the government engages in meaningful dialogue.</p> <h2>Government response and mitigation measures</h2> <p>In a press briefing on 28 March, Minister of National Education, Claire Boucher, said that the Ministry was “actively coordinating with local authorities to provide alternative learning solutions.” She announced that schools would be offered a “digital learning package” that includes video lessons, interactive quizzes and a dedicated helpline for students and teachers. The Ministry also pledged to provide temporary childcare facilities in partnership with local municipalities to ensure that working parents are not left without care.</p> <p>Despite these assurances, critics argue that the measures are insufficient. “Online learning cannot replace face‑to‑face instruction, especially for younger children,” says Dr. Sophie Renard, an education policy researcher at the Institute for Social Studies. She notes that the digital divide—particularly in rural areas—may exacerbate existing inequalities.</p> <h2>Alternative protest strategies and their potential impact</h2> <p>While school closures have captured headlines, unions have also employed other tactics. Strikes in the transportation sector, mass demonstrations in Paris, and targeted lobbying of parliamentarians have all been part of the broader strategy. Some experts suggest that a more diversified approach—combining strikes with digital campaigns and targeted lobbying—could yield more sustainable results without the collateral damage to students.</p> <p>Political analysts warn that the timing of the closures—just weeks before the first round of the 2024 presidential election—could influence voter sentiment. If the public perceives the government as inflexible, opposition parties that promise pension reform rollbacks may gain traction. Conversely, a perception that the government is responsive could strengthen its position among voters who prioritize social welfare.</p> <h2>Socio‑economic ripple effects</h2> <p>The closures have already had measurable economic consequences. According to a preliminary report by the French Institute of Public Finance, the closures cost the economy an estimated €120 million in lost productivity for the day, due to parents missing work to supervise children. The report also highlighted that 12 % of the affected households reported a temporary loss of income.</p> <p>Beyond the immediate financial impact, there are longer‑term concerns. The disruption of after‑school programmes may affect children’s nutritional intake, especially those from low‑income families who rely on school meals. In addition, the interruption of regular schooling could widen the achievement gap, a risk that has been documented in previous studies on school closures during strikes.</p> <h2>Legal challenges and future precedent</h2> <p>Unions have threatened to take the matter to the Constitutional Council, arguing that the closures violate the right to education guaranteed by Article 2 of the 1946 Constitution. The Council has previously ruled on similar cases, most notably the 2019 decision that upheld the right to protest in public institutions while maintaining the obligation to provide essential services.</p> <p>Should the Council find the closures unlawful, it could set a precedent limiting the use of school shutdowns as a protest tactic. Conversely, a ruling that upholds the Ministry’s authority could embolden other sectors to adopt similar strategies. The legal outcome will therefore have implications far beyond the current pension‑reform dispute.</p> <h2>Looking ahead</h2> <p>As the debate over pension reform continues, the school closures serve as a stark reminder of the intersection between labour rights, public policy and the delivery of essential services. The government’s next steps—whether it will negotiate with unions, roll back aspects of the reform, or maintain the status quo—will be closely watched by all stakeholders.</p> <p>For parents and students, the priority remains clear: a return to normal schooling with minimal disruption. For unions, the goal is a pension system that is fair and sustainable. And for the French electorate, the outcome of these negotiations could shape the political landscape for years to come.</p> <h2>Key take‑aways</h2> <ul> <li>423 schools closed on 3 April 2024, affecting 1.3 million students.</li> <li>The closures were authorized under Article L. 131‑1 of the Education Code, citing “serious disruption.”</li> <li>Parents and students report significant disruption to meals, care and learning.</li> <li>The Ministry has offered digital learning and temporary childcare as mitigation.</li> <li>Legal challenges could set precedent for future protest tactics.</li> <li>The timing of the closures may influence voter sentiment ahead of the 2024 presidential election.</li> </ul> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Le Monde, “Écoles fermées en réponse aux gr
