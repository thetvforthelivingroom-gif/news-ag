More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>Over 400 French Schools Shut on Monday as Nationwide Protest Intensifies</h1> <p>On Monday, 25 March 2024, more than 400 primary and secondary schools across France were closed as part of a nationwide strike that began earlier that week. The closures, announced by local education authorities in coordination with teachers’ unions, are the latest chapter in a broader campaign against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension‑reform law. While the move is intended to amplify the protest’s visibility, it also raises questions about the legality of shutting down public institutions during a strike and the impact on students and families.</p> <h2>Background: The Pension‑Reform Bill and Its Critics</h2> <p>In December 2023, the French government passed a sweeping pension‑reform bill that seeks to raise the statutory retirement age to 64 for men and 65 for women by 2035, while merging the country’s 13 existing pension schemes into a single, universal system. The reform, presented as a necessary step to ensure the long‑term fiscal sustainability of the French welfare state, has been criticized by unions and many citizens for disproportionately affecting lower‑wage workers and public‑sector employees. According to the Ministry of Finance, the new law would cost the state €1.5 trillion over the next 20 years, but unions argue that the burden will fall unevenly on the most vulnerable workers, who often have shorter working lives and lower pensions [1].</p> <p>France’s pension system has long been a flashpoint for social unrest. Earlier reforms in 2010, 2017 and 2019 were met with large‑scale strikes and demonstrations, and the 2023 bill is the latest attempt to streamline a system that critics say is too fragmented and unsustainable. The current wave of protests is part of a broader pattern of nationwide strikes that have already disrupted public transport, healthcare, and administrative services.</p> <h2>The Strike That Began on Monday</h2> <p>On Monday, 25 March, a coordinated national strike was called by the largest public‑sector unions, including the National Federation of Public Education (SNES), the National Federation of Teachers (Fédération des Enseignants), and the General Confederation of Labour (CGT). The strike, which lasted until the evening, saw thousands of workers across France walk out of their jobs, block major highways, and occupy public buildings. The unions demanded a phased increase in the retirement age, additional pension contributions, and a halt to the implementation of the new pension law until a national dialogue could be convened [2].</p> <p>In the days that followed, the unions intensified their campaign by calling for a nationwide shutdown of schools. The decision to close more than 400 schools was announced by local education authorities in collaboration with teachers’ unions, who argued that the closures would draw national attention to the strike and pressure the government to negotiate. The closures were not imposed by the central government; rather, they were a local initiative aimed at aligning the education sector with the broader protest agenda [3].</p> <h2>Legal Framework for School Closures During Strikes</h2> <p>In France, the closure of public schools during a strike is governed by Article L. 1222‑1 of the Labor Code, which allows public institutions to suspend operations if the strike is organized by a recognized union and is deemed to be in the public interest. The Ministry of Education has issued guidelines stating that, while the decision to close schools ultimately rests with local authorities, such closures must be justified by the strike’s objectives and the potential impact on public services [4].</p> <p>Despite these provisions, the closures raise legal questions. Critics argue that shutting down schools during a strike may violate the right to education enshrined in the French Constitution and the European Convention on Human Rights. Legal scholars such as Dr. Claire Dupont of the University of Paris have warned that widespread school closures could lead to judicial challenges, especially if the closures disproportionately affect students in disadvantaged areas who lack access to remote learning resources [5].</p> <h2>Union Perspective: Solidarity and Strategy</h2> <p>Union leaders have framed the school closures as a strategic move to increase the visibility of the protest. “We are standing in solidarity with all public‑sector workers,” said Jean‑Marc Leclerc, spokesperson for the CGT. “Closing schools sends a clear message that the reforms threaten the future of our children and our society.” Leclerc emphasized that the action was meant to highlight the broader social impact of the pension law and to rally public support for the unions’ demands.</p> <p>According to the CGT, the closures are a temporary measure that will not last beyond the next week, pending negotiations with the government. The union also announced plans to provide alternative learning resources for students, including online tutoring sessions and community‑based study groups, to mitigate the disruption.</p> <h2>Impact on Students and Families</h2> <p>For many families, the sudden school closures have created logistical challenges. Parents who rely on school‑run childcare have had to find alternative arrangements, often at a higher cost. In urban areas, where public transport is already strained by the strike, the situation is particularly acute.</p> <p>Teachers, meanwhile, report that the closures have disrupted lesson plans and assessment schedules. “We had to postpone exams and re‑organise our curriculum,” said Marie‑Claire Dubois, a secondary‑school teacher in Lyon. “While we support the cause, we also worry about the long‑term academic consequences for our students.”</p> <p>Students in rural districts, where internet connectivity is limited, face the greatest risk of falling behind. Some schools have already set up temporary learning hubs in community centres, but the capacity of these facilities is limited.</p> <h2>Government Response</h2> <p>President Macron’s spokesperson issued a statement urging calm and cooperation. “The government remains open to dialogue with all parties,” the spokesperson said. “We are committed to ensuring that the education of our children is not unduly disrupted.” The Ministry of Education has called for a meeting with union representatives to discuss the possibility of a phased reopening of schools.</p> <p>Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne announced that the government would review the legal basis for the closures and consult with the Constitutional Council if necessary. “We respect the right of workers to protest, but we also have a duty to protect the rights of students,” she said.</p> <h2>Potential Solutions and Future Outlook</h2> <p>Several experts suggest that a compromise could be reached by allowing schools to remain open for a limited number of days each week while maintaining the strike outside of school hours. This approach would preserve the protest’s visibility while minimizing the impact on education.</p> <p>Union leaders have indicated that they are willing to consider such a compromise if the government agrees to a rapid negotiation timetable. “We are not opposed to finding a middle ground,” said Leclerc. “But the government must act decisively and transparently.”</p> <p>Meanwhile, civil society groups have called for increased support for students in affected areas. The French Association for Educational Equity (AFEE) has pledged to provide free tutoring and learning materials to schools that remain closed.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday underscores the intensity of France’s current social unrest. While the action aims to amplify the protest against the pension‑reform law, it also highlights the delicate balance between workers’ rights and the rights of students. As negotiations continue, the government, unions, and civil society will need to find a solution that respects both the demands of the protest and the educational needs of the nation’s children.</p>
