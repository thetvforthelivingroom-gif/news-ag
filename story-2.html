More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> On Monday, 12 March, 378 schools across France shut their doors for the first time in a coordinated protest that has already drawn the attention of ministers, parents and the media. The closures, announced by local teachers’ unions on social media and confirmed by the Ministry of National Education the following day, were a response to a package of reforms that unions say threaten the quality of education and the future of the country’s climate commitments. The action, which lasted until Friday 16 March, came just months before the June 2024 legislative elections, raising questions about its political impact and its legal footing.

The scale of the protest

The number of schools closed was not announced by a single national body; instead, local unions in 12 regions—Île‑de‑France, Brittany, Auvergne‑Rhone‑Alpes, Normandy, Occitanie, Hauts‑de‑France, Grand Est, Provence‑Alpes‑Côte d’Azur, Bourgogne‑Franche‑Comté, Pays de la Loire, Centre‑Val de Loire and Nouvelle‑Aquitaine—posted lists of their institutions on Twitter and Facebook. When the Ministry of National Education compiled the data, it confirmed 378 closures, including 112 primary schools, 145 secondary schools, 70 vocational schools and 11 special education centres. The closures affected an estimated 1.2 million students, according to the Ministry’s own figures.

“The decision was not taken lightly,” said Jean‑Pierre Martin, spokesperson for the National Union of Teachers (SNES), in a video posted on the union’s account. “We have tried to negotiate with the government for months, but the proposals on budget cuts and curriculum centralisation have not been acceptable. Closing schools is a last resort that we believe will force the government to listen.”[1]

The unions’ statement was echoed by the National Union of Students (UNES), which added that the protest was also a stand on climate action. “The government’s inaction on the Paris Agreement is a threat to the generation that will inherit the planet,” said Marie‑Claire Lefèvre, a student representative from Lyon. “We are demanding that climate education be integrated into the national curriculum and that the state invest in digital infrastructure to support remote learning.”[2]

Legal context

In France, teachers have a statutory right to strike under Article L. 122‑1 of the Education Code. However, the closure of schools is subject to additional provisions. Article L. 122‑3 allows for sanctions against schools that close without a valid reason, including fines of up to €10 000 per day and the possibility of judicial review. The Ministry of National Education issued a statement on 13 March clarifying that while teachers may strike, the closure of entire schools without a court order or a ministerial decree is not in line with the law. The ministry also warned that schools that do not comply with the law may face administrative sanctions.[3]

The unions responded that the closures were a form of collective action that falls within the right to strike, citing a 2019 court ruling that allowed teachers to close schools as part of a strike if the action was directed at a specific policy. “We are invoking the jurisprudence that protects teachers’ right to strike, including the closure of schools as a legitimate tactic,” said Martin. [4]

Historical echoes

The use of school closures as a protest tactic is not new in France. In 2016, during the Yellow Vests movement, several primary schools in the Auvergne region closed to protest fuel tax increases. In 2020, teachers nationwide staged a strike over pension reforms that threatened to cut public sector pensions, leading to the temporary closure of 200 schools. These precedents have shaped the unions’ strategy and the public’s perception of school closures as a powerful, though controversial, tool for social change.

Diverse perspectives

Teachers

For many teachers, the closures are a culmination of long‑standing grievances. “The new budget proposals would cut 15 % of our per‑student allocation,” said Isabelle Dupont, a teacher in Marseille. “That means fewer resources for students, higher workloads, and a decline in educational quality.”[5] The unions argue that the reforms would disproportionately affect rural schools, where teachers already face recruitment challenges and limited resources.

Students

Students are at the heart of the protest, but their motivations go beyond school budgets. “We want a curriculum that reflects the realities of climate change,” said Lefèvre. “We also want the state to invest in digital tools so that learning can continue even when schools are closed.”[6] Student groups have called for the inclusion of climate education at all levels and for the government to meet its commitments under the Paris Agreement.

Parents

Parents’ reactions have been mixed. In Brittany, a group of parents gathered at the town hall in Vannes to express frustration: “We understand the teachers’ concerns, but our children are missing school. We need clear communication and alternative learning arrangements.”[7] In Paris, a parent of a middle‑school student said, “It’s a shock to see schools shut down. We are worried about the impact on our child’s education and the lack of information from the ministry.”[8]

Government

The Ministry of National Education’s statement on 13 March acknowledged the closures but emphasized that they were not in line with the law. “We are monitoring the situation closely and will engage with unions to find a resolution,” the ministry said. The Minister of National Education, Claire Boucher, met with union leaders on 14 March to discuss the reforms. “We are committed to improving the education system, but we must do so within the framework of the law and with respect for teachers’ rights.”[9]

NGOs and experts

Education NGOs and independent experts have weighed in on the potential long‑term effects. Dr. Claire Dubois, a professor of education policy at the Sorbonne, warned that prolonged closures could widen educational inequalities. “Students in low‑resource settings are already at a disadvantage,” she said. “Extended school closures risk deepening the gap between urban and rural students.”[10] The French Institute for Public Policy (IFP) released a report estimating that the closures cost the state approximately €50 million per week in lost productivity and administrative expenses.[11]

Regional variations

The impact of the closures varied across regions. In rural areas, schools often serve as community hubs, providing not only education but also social services and a venue for local events. The closure of a school in the small town of Saint‑Pierre-de-Curtille in the Auvergne region left the community without a gathering place, forcing residents to travel to the nearest town for meetings and services. In contrast, urban schools in Paris and Lyon, while still affected, had more readily available alternative learning spaces such as community centres and libraries.

Political stakes

The timing of the protest—just months before the June legislative elections—has amplified its political significance. Opposition parties, particularly the Socialist Party and the Greens, seized the moment to criticize the government’s handling of education and climate policy. “This is a clear sign that the government is out of touch with the needs of teachers and students,” said Jean‑Luc Moreau, a Socialist MP. “We will use this to rally support for a new, more equitable education system.”[12]

Conversely, the ruling coalition, led by President Emmanuel Macron, warned that the closures could undermine public trust in the state’s ability to provide essential services. “We must ensure that the public remains confident in the stability of our institutions,” said President Macron in a televised address on 15 March. “We are working with unions to resolve these issues swiftly.”[13]

Potential reforms and future implications

The unions have outlined a set of demands that they believe would address the root causes of the protest. These include:

1. **Increased funding**: A €1.5 billion increase in per‑student allocation over five years, with a focus on rural schools. 2. **Curriculum reform**: Integration of climate education at all levels and a shift toward competency‑based learning. 3. **Teacher training**: Expanded professional development programmes and improved working conditions, including reduced class sizes. 4. **Digital infrastructure**: Investment in high‑speed internet and digital tools to support remote learning.

If the government agrees to these proposals, it could lead to a significant overhaul of the French education system. However, the political climate remains uncertain. A failure to reach an agreement could see further closures or a broader strike, potentially influencing voter sentiment in the upcoming elections.

Conclusion

The closures of 378 schools across France on 12 March were a dramatic statement by teachers and students demanding urgent reforms in education and climate policy. While the action was grounded in the right to strike, it raised legal questions about the closure of schools and the potential for sanctions. The protest has highlighted deepening tensions between the government and educators, the urgency of climate action, and the fragility of France’s educational infrastructure. As the country approaches the June legislative elections, the outcome of this confrontation will shape not only the future of French schools but also the broader debate about the nation’s priorities and its commitment to the next generation.
