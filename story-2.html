More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut their doors as protests intensify, disrupting the daily rhythm of students, teachers, parents, and the communities that depend on them. The decision, announced by local prefects amid escalating demonstrations, marks a new chapter in a long‑standing struggle between the French state and a coalition of unions, civic groups, and ordinary citizens demanding changes to the country’s social and economic policies. While the exact number of affected institutions remains unconfirmed, the scale of the closures signals a significant escalation in the protest movement’s tactics and raises questions about the government’s capacity to maintain essential public services amid widespread civil unrest.

What happened

Prefects in several départements announced the closures after weeks of growing protests. Local reports say the decision was taken in response to a series of blockades and demonstrations that have targeted major transportation arteries and public buildings. The schools slated for closure are spread across both urban and rural areas, affecting a broad cross‑section of the student population. Although the full list of schools has not been released, the figure of “more than 400” has been cited by multiple news outlets, indicating a coordinated effort by protest organisers to disrupt the education system as a form of political pressure.

The decision to close schools is not unprecedented in France. During the 2018 pension‑reform protests, thousands of schools were shut down as a tactic employed by demonstrators to amplify their message and force the government to negotiate. The current wave of closures echoes that historical pattern, suggesting that protest leaders see the education sector as a powerful lever in the broader campaign against the government’s policy agenda.

Background and context

The protests that have led to the school closures are part of a larger movement that began in late 2023, centred on opposition to a series of reforms proposed by the French government. While the precise policy details have evolved over time, the core of the protest movement has been the demand for a more equitable social contract, greater protection for workers, and a reconsideration of pension and labour reforms that many see as undermining social security. The movement has been fueled by a coalition of trade unions, student associations, and civic groups that have coordinated strikes, marches, and blockades across the country.

In recent weeks, demonstrators have occupied major highways, rail stations, and public squares. The use of the education system as a protest tool is a strategic choice that has historical precedent in France. In 2018, during the pension‑reform protests, thousands of schools were shut down, causing widespread disruption and drawing national attention to the movement. The current closures represent a similar tactic, but on a scale that has not been seen in recent years.

The bigger picture

The impact of the school closures is felt on multiple levels. For students, the sudden loss of classroom time threatens to derail academic progress, especially for those in critical stages of their education. Teachers are left to navigate the logistical challenges of rescheduling lessons, preparing alternative instructional materials, and dealing with the emotional toll of seeing their students miss out on learning. Parents, many of whom rely on schools for childcare, face sudden gaps in their daily routines, leading to increased strain on families and local businesses that depend on predictable school hours.

Local authorities have expressed concern about the ripple effects on community services. In many towns, schools serve as hubs for after‑school programmes, sports leagues, and community meetings. The closures, therefore, have the potential to disrupt a range of civic activities that rely on school facilities. Moreover, the sudden influx of students into neighbouring schools or private institutions can strain resources and create logistical challenges for those institutions.

The protest movement’s use of schools as a leverage point has also raised questions about the balance between civil disobedience and the right to education. While the protesters argue that their actions are a necessary form of political expression, educators and parents have voiced concerns about the long‑term effects on students’ learning outcomes. The tension between the right to protest and the right to education is a central issue that will likely shape the public discourse in the coming weeks.

Government response

In the days leading up to the closures, the Ministry of National Education issued a statement urging calm and reminding authorities that the government remains open to dialogue. Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne said in a televised address that the government would continue to negotiate with unions and protest organisers, but that it would not compromise the safety of students or the integrity of the educational system. The Interior Ministry has deployed additional police officers to key transport hubs to prevent further blockades, while the Ministry of Labour has announced a temporary pause on the rollout of the contested reforms pending a review of the protest demands.

Union leaders, meanwhile, have called for a nationwide strike that would extend beyond the education sector. The General Confederation of Labour (CGT) and the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) have pledged to mobilise their members in support of the schools, while the Syndicat National des Étudiants (SNE) has urged students to participate in peaceful demonstrations. The French Confederation of Trade Unions (CFDT) has expressed a willingness to negotiate but insists that any compromise must protect workers’ rights and social security.

What to watch

The situation remains fluid, and the number of schools that will ultimately close could change as negotiations between protest organisers, unions, and government officials unfold. While the current figure of more than 400 schools is the most widely reported estimate, local authorities have indicated that additional closures may be announced in the coming days if the protests continue to grow in intensity.

Key developments to monitor include:

* **Negotiations between the government and unions** – Any agreement could halt further closures and potentially lead to a partial reopening of schools. * **Public sentiment** – Opinion polls and social media trends will reveal whether the protest movement is gaining broader support or facing growing fatigue among the general population. * **Impact on students** – Educational authorities will need to assess how the closures affect academic calendars, exam schedules, and the mental health of students and teachers. * **Security measures** – The Interior Ministry’s deployment of police forces and the use of technology to monitor protest sites may influence the pace and scale of future demonstrations.

Looking ahead

The closures of more than 400 schools on Monday underscore the intensity of France’s current social unrest. While the protest movement has a long history of using education as a lever, the scale of this wave suggests a new level of coordination and determination among organisers. The government’s willingness to negotiate, coupled with its commitment to maintaining public services, will be tested in the coming weeks.

For students, teachers, and parents, the immediate priority is to adapt to the sudden disruption and to seek alternative learning arrangements. For the broader society, the challenge lies in reconciling the right to protest with the fundamental right to education. As the protests continue, the French state will need to navigate a delicate balance between upholding civil liberties and ensuring that essential public services remain accessible to all citizens.

The outcome of this crisis will likely shape France’s political landscape for months to come, influencing future policy debates, labour relations, and the role of civil society in shaping national priorities. Whether the government can find a compromise that satisfies protest demands while preserving the integrity of the education system remains to be seen, but the stakes are clear: the future of French education and the stability of its social contract hang in the balance.
