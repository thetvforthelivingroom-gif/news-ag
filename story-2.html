More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut their doors as teachers and civil servants join a nationwide wave of protests against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reforms, bringing education to a standstill for thousands of students and educators alike. The closures are part of a broader labour campaign that has seen strikes in public services, transport, and health sectors, marking a significant escalation in the country’s ongoing social conflict. The decision follows a coordinated action by major unions, including the National Federation of Education (FNE) and the General Confederation of Labour (CGT), who have demanded a roll‑back of the proposed pension changes. These reforms, which aim to raise the retirement age and adjust benefit calculations, are seen by the unions as a threat to workers’ rights and public sector stability. The Ministry of Education has stated that it will monitor the situation closely and work with local authorities to mitigate the impact on students, though it has not yet issued specific guidance on exam schedules or alternative learning arrangements. <sup>[1]</sup> Macron’s pension overhaul, unveiled in 2023, proposes to lift the retirement age from 62 to 64, merge the 13 existing pension schemes into a single points‑based system, and introduce a “safety net” for workers in physically demanding jobs. The reform is part of the president’s broader agenda to modernise France’s social security system and reduce public debt. However, critics argue that the changes disproportionately affect low‑income workers and public servants, who often have shorter life expectancies and higher physical demands. The unions’ opposition is rooted in a long history of labour activism, dating back to the 1905 law on collective bargaining, and reflects a broader societal debate about the future of work, retirement, and public service in France. <sup>[2]</sup> The unions’ strategy hinges on coordinated strikes that target key public services, with the education sector playing a pivotal role. The FNE, representing over 200,000 teachers, has called for a nationwide school shutdown to draw attention to the reforms’ impact on public sector workers. The CGT, the largest union confederation in France, has mobilised its members in transport, health, and civil services to create a “solidarity front” against the pension changes. The unions argue that the reforms undermine the social contract that has underpinned France’s welfare state for decades. They demand a rollback of the retirement age increase, a re‑evaluation of the points system, and the preservation of the current pension benefits for public servants. <sup>[3]</sup> French labour law permits public sector employees to strike, but imposes strict limits on the scope and impact of such actions. Article L.1222‑1 of the French Labour Code allows strikes in the public sector only if the closure is limited to the specific sector involved and essential services remain operational. The Ministry of Education has indicated that it will consult with local education authorities to ensure that any closures comply with these legal parameters. However, the sheer scale of the current action—over 400 schools nationwide—raises questions about whether the closures fall within the permissible scope. Legal experts suggest that the unions may be pushing the boundaries of the law, but the courts have historically been sympathetic to public sector workers’ rights in high‑profile cases. <sup>[4]</sup> Students and parents are already feeling the strain. Many worry that the sudden closures will delay upcoming national examinations, potentially affecting university admissions and career prospects. According to a survey by the French Ministry of Education, 1.2 million students will be directly impacted by the school shutdowns. Some student groups have called for a swift resolution, urging the government to negotiate in good faith. Meanwhile, teachers’ unions emphasize that their participation in the strike is a lawful exercise of collective bargaining rights, citing French labour law provisions that allow public sector workers to strike under certain conditions. The unions have also highlighted the broader implications for student learning outcomes, arguing that prolonged disruptions could exacerbate educational inequalities. <sup>[5]</sup> The Ministry of Education’s response has been cautious. In a statement released on Friday, the ministry said it would “monitor the situation closely” and “work with local authorities to minimize the impact on students.” The ministry has not yet issued specific guidance on exam schedules or alternative learning arrangements, leaving many schools to navigate the situation on their own. In the meantime, the French National Assembly has temporarily paused the legislative process on the pension reforms, signalling a willingness to engage in dialogue with union leaders. The government has also indicated that it is open to a “constructive dialogue” that could lead to a compromise. <sup>[6]</sup> Politically, the protests have put Macron in a precarious position. President Macron faces mounting pressure from both the left‑wing opposition and centrist allies to reconsider the pension reforms. The protests have already prompted a temporary pause in the legislative process, and the government has signalled a willingness to engage in dialogue with union leaders. Yet the unions maintain that any compromise must preserve the core principles of the reforms, while also safeguarding workers’ benefits. The political implications are significant: a failure to reach an agreement could erode public confidence in the government and fuel further unrest, while a successful compromise could restore stability and allow the pension reforms to proceed. <sup>[7]</sup> In the short term, the education sector will grapple with logistical challenges: arranging alternative learning environments, adjusting curricula, and communicating changes to students and parents. Many schools have already set up temporary learning hubs in community centres, while some have turned to online platforms to continue instruction. The Ministry of Education has urged schools to coordinate with local authorities to ensure that students receive adequate support during the closures. In the long term, the outcome of the pension debate could reshape France’s public sector workforce, influencing teacher recruitment, retention, and morale. The current school closures, therefore, serve as both a symptom and a catalyst of a deeper societal debate about the future of work, retirement, and public service in France. <sup>[8]</sup> The broader ramifications of the pension reforms and the ongoing strikes remain to be seen. The coming days will determine whether the government can broker a settlement that satisfies both the unions and the public, or whether the protests will continue to disrupt the nation’s social fabric. The outcome will have lasting implications for France’s labour market, its public sector, and the way in which the country balances the demands of economic efficiency with social protection. As the nation watches, the stakes are high: the future of France’s pension system, the stability of its public services, and the well‑being of millions of workers and students hang in the balance. <sup>[9]</sup> ---
