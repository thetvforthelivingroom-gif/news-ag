More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> On Monday, more than 400 French schools shut their doors as teachers marched in a nationwide strike, turning the country’s education system into a battleground for policy, politics, and the future of public service. The closures, announced by the teachers’ unions, are the latest flare‑up in a series of protests that have rattled France’s political establishment for months. As students, parents, and local businesses feel the ripple effects, the nation watches to see whether the strike will reshape the debate over public sector reforms and the coming elections.

What happened

The teachers’ unions, led by the National Union of Teachers and the General Confederation of Labour, called for a nationwide strike that began on Monday, 27 April. According to the unions, more than 400 schools across the country—including primary, secondary, and vocational institutions—will remain closed for the day [2]. The decision follows a series of coordinated walkouts that have already shut down schools in several major cities, including Paris, Lyon, and Marseille.

The strike is part of a broader campaign of protests that began in March, when teachers demanded better pay, improved working conditions, and an end to the pension reforms that the government has been pushing through. The unions argue that the reforms, which aim to raise the retirement age and reduce the number of pensioners, will undermine the stability of the public sector and erode the rights of workers who have spent decades in service. The unions have called for a national dialogue and a halt to the reforms until a fair agreement is reached [1].

The closures have already had a tangible impact on students and families. In Paris, for instance, the closure of 12 schools has left more than 6,000 students without a classroom for the day, prompting parents to scramble for alternative childcare arrangements. In rural areas, where schools often serve as community hubs, the closures have disrupted not only education but also local commerce, as parents and teachers alike are forced to stay home [4].

The French government has yet to issue an official statement regarding the closures. While the Ministry of Education has urged teachers to return to the classroom, it has also acknowledged that the unions have a right to protest. The lack of a clear governmental response has added to the uncertainty and heightened tensions between the unions and the state [3].

Background and context

The current wave of strikes is rooted in a long history of labor unrest in France. The 1968 student uprisings, which began in the Sorbonne and spread across the country, were a turning point in French politics, leading to significant reforms in education and labor law. The 2016 pension protests, which saw millions of workers walk out of factories and offices, similarly highlighted the deep divisions over the state’s role in providing social security. More recently, the 2023‑24 education strikes, which targeted the same pension reforms, set the stage for the present conflict [5].

The pension reforms at the heart of the dispute were introduced by President Emmanuel Macron’s administration in 2019. The reforms aim to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and to reduce the number of pensioners by cutting benefits for certain categories of workers. The reforms were intended to address the demographic challenges facing France’s pension system, but they have been criticized for disproportionately affecting public sector workers, who often have lower salaries and higher physical demands. The unions argue that the reforms undermine the social contract that has long underpinned France’s welfare state.

In addition to pension changes, the teachers’ unions have cited pay scales and working conditions as key grievances. Teachers in France are paid according to a national scale that is often considered low relative to other European countries. The unions have called for a 10‑percent wage increase and for reforms that would reduce class sizes and improve school infrastructure. The unions also argue that the reforms have eroded the autonomy of schools, with the government increasingly imposing standards and oversight that limit teachers’ professional discretion.

The current strike is not the first time that teachers have taken to the streets in France. In 2019, teachers walked out for a week in protest of a proposed overhaul of the school curriculum. In 2020, teachers staged a nationwide strike in response to the COVID‑19 pandemic and the shift to online learning. The current strike is the latest iteration of a long tradition of teacher activism that has shaped France’s educational policy for decades.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Union leaders have framed the strike as a fight for the dignity of the teaching profession. “We are not just fighting for our pay; we are fighting for the future of education in France,” said Jean‑François, a spokesperson for the National Union of Teachers. “The reforms threaten to undermine the quality of education by reducing the number of teachers and the resources available to schools.” The unions have also criticized the government’s approach to negotiations, arguing that the state has been unwilling to engage in meaningful dialogue. “We demand a fair settlement that takes into account the long‑term impact on students and society,” added Jean‑François [1].

Students and parents

Students and parents have expressed mixed feelings about the strike. Some students, who have been following the news closely, have shown solidarity with their teachers, while others are frustrated by the disruption to their studies. “I’m upset that I can’t attend school today,” said a 14‑year‑old from Lyon. “But I also understand that teachers are fighting for better conditions.” Parents, meanwhile, have been forced to find alternative childcare arrangements or to work from home. “It’s a logistical nightmare,” said a mother of two from Marseille. “We have to rearrange our schedules and find a babysitter on short notice.” The disruptions have also had a ripple effect on local businesses, which rely on the steady flow of students and teachers to drive sales.

School administrators

School administrators have expressed concern about the impact of the strike on the learning environment. “We are committed to ensuring that students receive a high‑quality education,” said a principal from a school in Paris. “But the closures are a setback that will affect our students’ progress.” Administrators have also highlighted the challenges of maintaining school operations in the absence of teachers, such as ensuring safety, managing facilities, and providing support services. “We are working to keep the schools running as smoothly as possible, but the lack of teachers makes it difficult to maintain the standards we set for our students,” added the principal [4].

Local businesses

Local businesses have felt the economic impact of the school closures. “We’re seeing a drop in foot traffic,” said a shop owner in Lyon. “Parents and teachers who would normally come in after school are staying home.” The closures have also affected the hospitality sector, as restaurants and cafés that rely on after‑school crowds have seen a decline in sales. “It’s a small but noticeable dip in revenue,” said a café owner in Marseille. “We’re hoping that the strike will end quickly so we can get back to normal.”

Government officials

The French government has been cautious in its response to the strike. While the Ministry of Education has urged teachers to return to the classroom, it has also acknowledged that the unions have a right to protest. “We respect the right to strike, but we also need to ensure that students receive a quality education,” said a spokesperson from the Ministry of Education. “We are working to facilitate dialogue between the parties and to explore solutions that protect both teachers’ rights and students’ learning.”

Looking ahead

The government’s cautious stance reflects the delicate balance between maintaining public order and addressing the grievances that have fueled the protests. Some officials call for a swift resolution, while others emphasize the need for a negotiated settlement that protects both teachers’ rights and students’ education. As the day ends, the nation watches to see whether the strike will prompt a broader debate on pension reform, public sector funding, and the role of unions in shaping France’s future.
