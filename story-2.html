More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> **More Than 400 Schools Shut in France as Nationwide Protest Over Pension Reform Continues** On Monday, more than 400 schools across 12 French regions were closed after teachers, civil servants and students joined a nationwide strike to protest President Emmanuel Macron’s pension overhaul. The Ministry of National Education (MEN) announced the closures in the early hours, citing the need to “prevent unnecessary disruptions” while respecting the right to protest [1]. Parents scrambled for childcare, and teachers had to cancel lessons, underscoring the tangible impact of the protest on daily life. The strike follows a week‑long escalation that began when the government unveiled a plan to raise the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 and to replace France’s 42 separate pension schemes with a single, points‑based system. The reform, part of Macron’s broader fiscal‑discipline agenda, has been met with fierce opposition from unions that view it as a threat to workers’ rights and the social safety net [2]. Teachers, civil servants and students have joined the movement, demanding better education conditions and opposing what they see as the erosion of public services [3].

The Day of the Strike

In the early morning, the MEN issued a notice that schools in the affected regions—Bourgogne‑Franche‑Comté, Grand Est, Île‑de‑France, Normandie, Nouvelle‑Aquitaine, Occitanie, Pays de la Loire, Provence‑Alpes‑Côte‑d’Azur, Auvergne‑Rhone‑Alpes, Hauts‑de‑France, Centre‑Val‑de‑Loire and Brittany—would be closed for the day. The announcement followed a coordinated strike by teachers’ unions and student associations, who had called for a national day of action to protest the pension reforms. According to Reuters, the shutdown will affect about 400 schools nationwide, a figure that underscores the scale of the protest movement [1].

The government’s official statement noted that the decision was made “in consultation with the unions” and that it aimed to avoid “unnecessary disruptions” while respecting the right to protest [1]. The statement also emphasized that the closures were a temporary measure to allow for dialogue and to maintain public order.

Pension Reform: Fiscal Necessity or Social Attack?

The pension overhaul, unveiled in March, aims to reduce the national deficit by cutting pension expenditures. It proposes a universal points‑based system that would replace the existing 42 schemes, thereby simplifying the system but also raising the retirement age and introducing a “pension index” that would adjust benefits based on life expectancy and inflation. Unions argue that the reforms would disproportionately affect workers in physically demanding jobs and would erode the generosity of the French social safety net. The government insists that the changes are necessary to ensure the long‑term sustainability of the pension system, citing demographic shifts and rising life expectancy as key drivers [2].

The reform has been described by the French government as a “necessary step” to bring the pension system in line with the country’s fiscal realities. The Ministry of Finance has stated that the pension system’s current trajectory would lead to a deficit of 1.5 % of GDP by 2035 if no changes were made [2]. In response, the unions have called for a complete halt to the reforms and a return to the previous pension system.

Student & Labor Coalition

The student movement, organized by the National Union of Secondary Education Students (UNES), has aligned itself with teachers’ unions such as the National Federation of Teachers (FNCT) and the General Confederation of Labour (CGT). The coalition’s demands extend beyond pensions to include increased funding for schools, better teacher pay, and a halt to the privatization of certain public services. The use of school closures as a protest tactic is a strategic choice that amplifies the visibility of the movement while directly impacting the daily lives of students and teachers alike. The coalition’s slogan, “Education first, pensions second,” encapsulates the perceived hierarchy of public priorities in the current debate [3].

UNES president Marie‑Claire Dubois said that the students were “not only fighting for their own future but for the future of the entire nation.” She added that the protests were a “call to action for all citizens who believe in a fair and equitable society.” The teachers’ unions, meanwhile, have demanded higher salaries, smaller class sizes, and more resources for schools. They argue that the pension reforms divert attention and resources away from education, thereby compromising the quality of schooling.

Historical Echoes

The current protests echo earlier moments of civil unrest in France. The 1968 student protests, which began in the Sorbonne and spread nationwide, were a pivotal moment in French history that challenged the status quo and led to significant social reforms. More recently, the Yellow Vests movement of 2019‑2020 began as a protest against fuel tax increases but evolved into a broader critique of economic inequality and government policies. In both cases, the state’s response involved a mix of concessions and crackdowns, illustrating the delicate balance between maintaining public order and addressing citizen grievances. The current school closures can be seen as part of this continuum of protest tactics that have historically shaped French public policy [4].

The Bigger Picture

State’s Fiscal Discipline vs. Unions’ Demands

From the government’s perspective, the pension reform is a fiscal necessity. Macron’s administration argues that without reform, France’s pension system will become unsustainable, leading to higher taxes or reduced benefits for future generations. The unions counter that the reforms undermine the social contract that has been a hallmark of the French welfare state. They argue that the reforms disproportionately affect lower‑income and physically demanding workers, who are already vulnerable. The debate has become a symbolic clash between the state’s commitment to fiscal prudence and the unions’ defense of social protections.

Students’ Push for Better Education Conditions

Students have seized the opportunity to highlight deficiencies in the education system, such as overcrowded classrooms, insufficient resources, and declining teacher morale. They argue that the pension reforms divert attention and resources away from education, thereby compromising the quality of schooling. The student leaders have called for a “national dialogue” that includes teachers, parents, and policymakers, emphasizing that education is a public good that should not be sacrificed for fiscal consolidation.

Legal Authority vs. Protest Tactics

The French legal framework grants the Ministry of National Education the authority to close schools in extraordinary circumstances, such as strikes or public safety concerns. However, the use of closures as a protest tactic has raised questions about the limits of state power and the right to protest. Some legal scholars argue that while the state can close schools, it must also ensure that students receive alternative educational arrangements. The unions have challenged the legality of the closures, claiming that they violate the right to education, while the government maintains that the closures are a necessary measure to maintain public order.

Public Perception and Trust in Institutions

The protests have polarized public opinion. Some citizens view the closures as a legitimate expression of dissent, while others see them as an unnecessary disruption to education and a sign of government overreach. Trust in public institutions has been eroded by the perception that the government is prioritizing fiscal consolidation over social welfare. The ongoing debate has highlighted the fragility of the social contract and the need for transparent dialogue between the state and its citizens.

What to Watch

Negotiation Space

The government has signaled a willingness to engage in dialogue with unions and student representatives. A recent statement from the Ministry of Finance indicated that the pension reform could be “revisited” if the public demands are compelling enough. However, the unions have demanded a complete halt to the reforms and a return to the previous pension system. The outcome of these negotiations will likely shape the trajectory of France’s social policy for years to come.

Legal Challenges

The unions are preparing legal challenges to the school closures and the pension reforms. A potential court ruling could set a precedent for the limits of state power during protests, particularly regarding the right to education. The outcome of these legal battles will influence how future protests are managed and how the state balances public order with civil liberties.

Long‑Term Impact on Education

If the protests succeed in halting or modifying the pension reforms, the French education system could receive increased funding and resources, potentially reversing trends of austerity. Conversely, if the reforms proceed, the education sector may face further budget cuts, affecting teacher salaries, class sizes, and educational quality. The student movement’s success will hinge on its ability to maintain momentum and secure tangible concessions from the government.
