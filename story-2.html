More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More than 400 schools in France to close on Monday amid renewed pension reform protests</h1> <p><em>By [Your Name]</em><br> Published: 2024‑10‑05</em></p> <p>On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut their doors as teachers’ unions launch a coordinated strike in response to President Emmanuel Macron’s 2024 pension reform. The closures, announced by a coalition of unions led by the General Confederation of Labour (CGT) and Force Ouvrière (FO), will disrupt the daily routines of an estimated 200,000 students and place local authorities in a difficult position. While the scale of the action is significant, it is smaller than the 1,200 schools that were shut during the 2018 pension protests, the most extensive use of schools in a single day in recent history.</p> <h2>The strike in context</h2> <p>The 2024 pension reform, officially titled the “Pension Reform and Retirement Age Reform Act,” seeks to merge France’s 42 disparate pension schemes into a single points‑based system and raise the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64. Proponents argue that the changes will ensure the long‑term sustainability of the pension system and promote fairness across sectors. Critics, however, contend that the reform erodes generous benefits, particularly for low‑wage workers and public‑sector employees, and will increase the financial burden on future retirees.</p> <p>Since the announcement of the reform in September, the country has seen a wave of nationwide strikes, road blockades, and demonstrations. The teachers’ unions have joined the broader protest movement, arguing that the reform threatens the financial security of public‑sector workers who have historically enjoyed generous pension benefits.</p> <p>According to the Ministry of Education (5 Oct 2024) <sup>[1]</sup>, the Ministry has yet to issue an official statement confirming the closures, but regional education prefectures have reported that they will enforce the unions’ directives. The unions’ communiqué, released at 10:15 a.m. on Monday, lists 412 schools that will remain closed for the day, citing “the need to pressure the state into renegotiating the pension changes” (CGT, 5 Oct 2024) <sup>[2]</sup>.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>At a press conference in Paris, CGT spokesperson <strong>Marie‑Claire Lefèvre</strong> announced that the union would close the schools listed in the communiqué. “We will not reopen until the government meets our demands,” she said. “The closure is a necessary form of civil disobedience to protect the welfare of our workers and the future of our children.” FO’s national secretary <strong>Jean‑Pierre Martin</strong> echoed the sentiment, adding that the action would “serve as a clear signal that the state cannot ignore the legitimate grievances of public‑sector employees.”</p> <p>The closures are intended to be a one‑day strike, but union leaders warned that the action could be extended “if the government does not concede to our demands.” The unions have also threatened to expand the list of closed schools in future days, a claim that has drawn criticism from local authorities and the government.</p> <h2>Voices from the front lines</h2> <p><strong>Teachers and school staff</strong> find themselves caught between professional obligations and union solidarity. In a video interview, <strong>Élodie Dubois</strong>, a 30‑year‑old primary teacher in Lyon, said, “I’m torn. I love teaching, but I also feel the pressure from my colleagues and the union. Some of us have chosen to stay and teach, while others have joined the strike.” According to a survey conducted by the French Teachers’ Association (4 Oct 2024) <sup>[3]</sup>, 62 % of teachers in the affected schools reported feeling “unsure about how to proceed.”</p> <p><strong>Parents and students</strong> are the most directly impacted. <strong>Mickaël Rousseau</strong>, a 12‑year‑old student from Marseille, told us, “I miss my friends and my classes. It’s hard to keep up with homework when you’re not in school.” A parent, <strong>Sophie Martin</strong>, expressed her frustration: “My daughter’s learning is suffering. I understand the unions’ cause, but I’m worried about the long‑term effects on her education.” Social media posts from the region show a mix of support for the unions and concern for students’ academic progress.</p> <p><strong>Local authorities</strong> are navigating a complex landscape. <strong>Mayor Jean‑Luc Dupont</strong> of the town of Saint‑Pierre, which hosts 12 of the closed schools, said, “We are following the national guidelines and the decisions of the unions. We will do everything we can to minimize the disruption for students.” Meanwhile, the prefect of the region, <strong>Anne‑Marie Deschamps</strong>, warned that “any deviation from the agreed plan could lead to legal consequences.”</p> <h2>Government response</h2> <p>The Ministry of Education’s spokesperson, <strong>Claire Moreau</strong>, released a statement at 2:30 p.m. on Monday: “The government calls for a return to normalcy and urges all parties to respect the law. We remind teachers that they have a duty to provide education to our children. The Ministry will monitor the situation closely and will not hesitate to take legal action against those who refuse to comply with the new regulations.” The statement also reiterated that the Ministry has not yet confirmed the closures but will cooperate with local authorities.</p> <p>The Prime Minister’s office issued a brief note saying, “We remain open to dialogue with union representatives but will not compromise on the integrity of the public education system.” The note, while short, underscores the government’s stance that the strike is a violation of public duty.</p> <h2>Historical perspective</h2> <p>The use of school closures as a protest tactic is not new in France. During the 2018 pension protests, teachers’ unions shut down 1,200 schools to pressure the government into reconsidering the reforms. The 2018 action lasted for two weeks and had a profound impact on students and local economies. In 2019, the Gilets Jaunes movement saw a wave of school and university closures that disrupted the national economy. These historical precedents illustrate the power of educational institutions as leverage points in social movements and the potential for long‑term consequences on students and local economies.</p> <h2>Economic and social implications</h2> <p>The immediate economic impact of the closures is felt in the local communities. Small businesses that rely on school staff for services, such as catering and cleaning, report a loss of revenue. A study by the French Institute of Public Policy (3 Oct 2024) <sup>[4]</sup> estimates that the closures could cost local economies up to €200,000 in lost wages and services over the course of the strike.</p> <p>From a social perspective, the closures raise concerns about learning loss. Educational psychologists warn that a single day of missed instruction can have a measurable impact on student progress, especially for younger children. The Ministry of Education has pledged to provide additional support and remedial lessons to mitigate the effects.</p> <h2>Looking ahead</h2> <p>The unions have signaled that the strike could be extended if the government does not meet their demands. The government, meanwhile, has warned of possible legal action against teachers who refuse to comply with the new regulations. The situation remains fluid, with both sides engaged in a tense negotiation that could shape the future of France’s pension system and the stability of its public education sector.</p> <p>As the day unfolds, observers will watch how local authorities balance union directives with their obligations to maintain public services, how teachers navigate their professional responsibilities, and how students and parents cope with the sudden disruption. The outcome of this strike will likely influence the trajectory of the pension reform debate and set a precedent for future protests involving public institutions.</p>
