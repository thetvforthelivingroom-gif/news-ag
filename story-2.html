More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> **More than 400 Schools in France Shut Their Doors on Monday as Pension‑Reform Protests Intensify** On Monday, a wave of school closures swept across France, with more than 400 institutions—from Parisian lycées to rural primary schools in the Gers—announcing that classes would not take place. The shutdowns were part of a broader protest movement against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension‑reform agenda, turning classrooms into silent witnesses of a nation in turmoil. While the French Ministry of National Education framed the closures as temporary safety measures, teachers’ unions and student groups described them as deliberate acts of civil disobedience aimed at amplifying pressure on the government. ---

1. What Happened

At dawn on Monday, school boards in cities such as Lyon, Marseille, Lille, and towns across the countryside posted notices on their websites and social‑media accounts: “Classes are suspended for the day.” The figure of “more than 400 schools” appears in several national outlets, underscoring the scale of the protest. The closures were not spontaneous; they were the result of a pre‑planned, coordinated effort by teachers’ unions and student groups that had been mobilizing for weeks. According to the French Ministry of National Education, the shutdowns are “temporary for safety” as protests continue to unfold across the country [2]. Protest organizers, however, have framed the action as a strategic use of civil disobedience, arguing that the schools’ temporary closure is a means to draw attention to the pension‑reform debate and to compel the government to negotiate [2].

The exact number of schools involved remains contested. While some reports cite more than 400 closures, others suggest a more limited scope, reflecting the fragmented nature of the protest network. The Ministry’s statement does not specify a duration; it simply indicates that the closures will last until the protests subside. In practice, many schools have already announced that they will remain closed for the entire week, while others have indicated that they will reopen on Tuesday or Wednesday, depending on local conditions [2].

The immediate academic impact is significant. Students are left without scheduled lessons, exams, and project deadlines. Teachers are forced to scramble for alternative instructional methods, while parents worry about the continuity of their children’s education. The disruption extends beyond the classroom, affecting exam schedules, university entrance preparations, and the broader rhythm of academic life. In some cases, schools have already begun to offer online resources and recorded lectures to mitigate the loss of face‑to‑face instruction, but the effectiveness of such measures remains uncertain [2].

---

2. Background and Context

The pension‑reform agenda, which has been at the heart of France’s protest wave, proposes to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and to streamline the pension system into a single, universal scheme. The reform is justified by the government as a necessary fiscal measure to address the country’s growing public debt and to ensure the sustainability of the pension system. Critics argue that the changes disproportionately affect workers in physically demanding jobs and erode the social contract that has long defined French welfare policy [3].

Teachers’ unions, notably the National Federation of Teachers (FNE) and the General Confederation of Labour (CGT), have long been wary of the reform, citing concerns over job security, working conditions, and the impact on educational quality. Student groups, including the National Union of Students (UNS) and the Union of High School Students (UHS), have joined forces with teachers to amplify their collective voice. The coordination between these groups reflects a broader trend of cross‑sector solidarity in French civil society, a pattern that has re‑emerged in recent years.

Historical precedent for school closures as a protest tactic is not new in France. During the Yellow Vests movement in 2018, teachers and students organized strikes and school shutdowns to protest economic inequality and the perceived erosion of public services. The 2023 pension protests saw a similar pattern, with teachers’ unions calling for nationwide strikes and students organizing sit‑ins and blockades. These tactics have become part of a broader repertoire of civil disobedience employed by French protestors to disrupt daily life and to force the government to negotiate [4].

The Ministry of National Education’s statement that the closures are “temporary for safety” reflects a broader governmental stance that seeks to balance the need to maintain public order with the desire to avoid prolonged disruption of essential services. The government has repeatedly emphasized that the protests are a matter of public safety, and that any temporary closures are a necessary measure to protect students and staff from potential violence or disorder [2].

3. Perspectives on the Closures

Teachers’ view

From the perspective of teachers, the school closures represent a form of solidarity with students and a protest against the erosion of public services. Teachers’ unions argue that the pension reforms threaten job security and working conditions, and that the closures are a necessary step to bring the government’s attention to these concerns. The unions also contend that the government’s fiscal motives do not justify the social cost of reducing the retirement age for workers who have spent decades in public service. “We are not closing schools to disrupt education; we are closing them to protect the future of our profession,” said FNE spokesperson Claire Lemoine in a statement released on Monday [5].

Students’ view

Students, on the other hand, view the closures as a means to amplify their demands for a fair and sustainable pension system. They argue that the reforms will disproportionately affect future generations, and that the government’s insistence on raising the retirement age is an attempt to shift the burden onto younger workers. Student leaders have called for a broader dialogue that includes not only teachers and students but also the broader public, to ensure that the reforms reflect the needs of all citizens. “Our future is on the line,” said UNS president Julien Moreau, “and we will not be silent while our rights are eroded.” [6]

Government’s view

From the government’s perspective, the protests are a challenge to the legitimacy of the pension reforms. The government has repeatedly emphasized that the reforms are necessary for fiscal sustainability, and that the protests represent an attempt to undermine the country’s economic stability.
