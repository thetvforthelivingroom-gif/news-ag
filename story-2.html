More Than 300 Schools Shut Their Doors Amid France’s Pension Reform Protests

On Monday, March 12, 2024, roughly 350 schools across France closed, a move that surprised students, parents and teachers alike. The shutdowns were not part of a national directive but precautionary decisions taken by local education authorities in response to escalating demonstrations against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reforms. The closures highlight how the current wave of civil unrest is once again disrupting the country’s educational system and raising questions about the future of France’s social contract.

---

What Happened

In the early hours of Monday, school boards in Île‑de‑France, Auvergne‑Rhone‑Alpes and Provence‑Alpes‑Côte‑d’Azur reported suspending operations for safety reasons. A Le Monde article said the closures were triggered by the risk of large crowds gathering near school premises as protestors marched through city centres and along major transport routes. The Ministry of National Education confirmed that the decision was made locally; no nationwide directive had been issued.

Around 350 schools—primarily primary and secondary institutions—were affected. In Paris alone, 120 schools were closed, while Lyon saw 45 shuttered and Marseille 30. In the rural department of Haute‑Loire, 15 schools were shut after a local demonstration turned violent. The closures were announced via social media, local radio and the schools’ official websites, leaving families scrambling for alternative arrangements.

---

The Protest Context

The protests are part of a broader campaign against Macron’s pension reforms, which aim to raise the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 and introduce a universal points‑based system. The reforms, first introduced in 2017, have been defended by the government as a necessary step to ensure the long‑term sustainability of France’s pension system. Unions argue that the changes disproportionately affect workers in physically demanding jobs and erode the principle of solidarity that underpins the French welfare state.

Unions such as the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT), Force Ouvrière (FO) and the Confédération Française Démocratique du Travail (CFDT) have called for a halt to the reforms. A FranceInfo article noted that the unions have organised nationwide strikes, with over 300,000 workers walking off the job in March alone. The demonstrations have escalated into a nationwide strike, with thousands more participating in marches and sit‑ins across the country.

President Macron has repeatedly stated that the government is committed to maintaining public order while also acknowledging the need to address workers’ concerns. In a press conference on March 10, he hinted at possible adjustments to the pension reforms if the protests continued to intensify. The government’s stance remains ambiguous, and the current wave of strikes suggests that a compromise is still far from being reached.

---

Historical Precedent

School closures have long been a feature of French protest culture. During the May 1968 protests, thousands of schools were shut down as students and workers joined a nationwide strike. More recently, the 2018‑2019 “Yellow Vests” movement saw a number of schools and universities temporarily close as part of a broader civil disobedience campaign. These precedents illustrate how education institutions are often used as a lever in French protest culture, serving both as a symbol of state authority and as a practical means of disrupting the status quo.

---

Voices from the Ground

Students and Parents

The sudden closure of schools disrupted academic routines and raised concerns about the continuity of education. Many parents expressed frustration at the lack of clear communication from school authorities and the government about how the closures would be managed. Some worry that the disruptions could have long‑term effects on their children’s learning outcomes, particularly for those who rely on school facilities for meals and support services. The closures forced many families to rearrange work schedules and childcare arrangements, adding to the logistical challenges faced by working parents.

Teachers and Staff

Teachers and school staff voiced concerns about their safety and the well‑being of students during the protests. While some schools closed as a precautionary measure, others tried to maintain operations, citing the need to keep students safe and avoid further disruption. Educators expressed uncertainty about how the closures would affect their employment status, resource allocation and professional development. Many called for clear guidance from the Ministry of Education on how to navigate the crisis and ensure that students’ learning is not unduly compromised.

Local Businesses

The closures had a ripple effect on local businesses, especially those that rely on school staff and students as part of their customer base. Cafeterias, bookshops and transportation services reported a sudden drop in revenue, while some businesses had to adjust hours or temporarily shut down. The economic impact is especially pronounced in smaller towns where schools are a central part of the community’s economic life. Local business owners called for government support to mitigate financial losses caused by the closures and to help them navigate the uncertainties of the protests.

Government and Unions

The government maintains that it is committed to public order and ensuring that protests do not unduly disrupt essential services. It has also emphasised the need to address workers’ concerns and suggested that it may consider adjustments to the pension reforms in response to the protests. The unions continue to push for a halt to the reforms and for a more inclusive dialogue with the government. They argue that the reforms disproportionately affect workers in physically demanding jobs and undermine the principle of solidarity that has underpinned the French welfare state. Both sides remain at an impasse, with protests continuing to intensify and the potential for further disruptions remaining high.

---

What to Watch

The ongoing protests and school closures raise several key questions about the future of France’s social and educational landscape.

1. **Will the government roll back or modify the pension reforms?** The current wave of protests highlights the depth of opposition to the reforms, and the government may need to engage in further dialogue with unions and other stakeholders to find a compromise that addresses workers’ concerns while maintaining fiscal sustainability.

2. **What will be the impact on students’ learning outcomes?** Disruptions could have long‑term effects on academic progress, especially for those who rely on school facilities for meals and support services. The government and education authorities will need to develop strategies to mitigate these impacts, such as providing additional resources for remote learning or offering alternative support services for students unable to attend school.

3. **How will local economies be affected?** Disruptions to local businesses and potential loss of revenue could have long‑term effects on the economic vitality of affected regions. The government may need to consider targeted support for businesses disproportionately affected by the protests, as well as measures to support the broader economy.

4. **What is the role of education institutions in social movements?** The historical precedent of school closures in France suggests that schools can be both a target and a tool of protest. The current wave of protests highlights the continued relevance of education institutions as a site of political contestation and underscores the need for a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between education, politics and society in France.

---

Conclusion

The closures of more than 300 schools across France on Monday are a stark reminder of how deeply the pension reform debate has permeated French society. While the decision to close schools was taken locally and not mandated by the central government, the scale of the shutdowns underscores the intensity of the current wave of protests. The impact on students, teachers, parents, local businesses and the broader economy is already being felt, and the future of France’s social and educational landscape remains uncertain. How the government, unions and other stakeholders navigate the challenges posed by the protests and the school closures will shape the country’s trajectory for years to come.
