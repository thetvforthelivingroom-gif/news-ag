More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France closed, not because of a pandemic but as a protest against a government seen as lagging on climate action and tightening education budgets. The shutdowns, triggered by a surge of demonstrations, disrupted learning for tens of thousands of students and teachers and sparked fresh debate on climate and education policy. The event illustrates how protest tactics are reshaping public policy in France.

What happened

The Ministry of National Education announced that over 400 schools would shut on Monday, following a wave of demonstrations by students, teachers, and environmental activists. The closures were reported from Paris to Brittany, with local authorities citing safety concerns and the need to prevent clashes between protesters and school staff. The decision came amid a broader climate‑and‑budget protest movement that has seen school closures in previous years, including the 2023–24 climate strikes that disrupted education nationwide [2].

The protests demanded that the French government accelerate its climate commitments and reverse cuts to the education budget. Protesters argued that the Paris Agreement targets and the national carbon‑neutral goal of 2050 are insufficiently enforced, while teachers and unions highlighted reductions in class sizes and teacher pay that have strained school resources. The closures were therefore both a symbolic and practical response to these grievances, aimed at drawing public attention to perceived inadequacies in the current policy mix [2].

While the Ministry of Education stated that it had no authority to close schools unilaterally, local prefects and school principals reported that the shutdowns were a coordinated response to escalating protests. In some departments, closures were announced after students staged sit‑ins and blockades outside schools, prompting officials to suspend operations to avoid potential violence. The decision was not uniform across the country; some rural schools remained open, while most urban and suburban institutions closed, reflecting a concentration of protest activity in densely populated regions [2].

The closures disrupted the academic calendar for a significant portion of the student body. Teachers faced sudden cancellations of lessons, many having to reschedule or shift to remote learning platforms. Parents reported logistical challenges, adjusting childcare arrangements and commuting to alternative schools for essential services. In a few instances, protests spilled onto school grounds, leading to minor property damage and heightened concerns about safety for students and staff [2].

Background and context

The scale of the school closures in France echoes earlier social movements that used education institutions as focal points for dissent. The May 1968 protests saw students occupy universities and schools, demanding sweeping social reforms. The 2018 Yellow Vests movement, while primarily focused on economic grievances, also saw school closures as part of broader civil unrest. More recently, the 2023–24 climate strikes, organized by youth groups such as Extinction Rebellion and Fridays for Future, disrupted education across Europe, with France experiencing a significant number of school shutdowns and strikes by teachers demanding better climate policies and resources [2].

These historical echoes highlight how school closures have become a strategic tool for protestors in France. In each instance, the closures disrupted normalcy, drew media attention, and pressured the government to address specific policy demands. The current wave of protests is no exception, with activists framing the closures as a necessary step to compel the government to meet its climate commitments and reverse austerity measures in education.

The climate grievances at the heart of the protests are rooted in France’s national policy framework. The country has pledged to achieve carbon neutrality by 2050 and to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 40 % by 2030 relative to 1990 levels, in line with the Paris Agreement. Critics argue that the government’s current policies lack enforcement mechanisms and fail to translate targets into concrete actions. The protests also spotlight the perceived disconnect between the government’s rhetoric on climate action and the lived reality of students and teachers, who face limited resources and infrastructure to support climate education and sustainable practices in schools [2].

Parallel to the climate debate is a contentious discussion about education funding. The French government has implemented budget cuts that have led to reductions in teacher pay, increased class sizes, and a decline in the availability of extracurricular programs. Teachers’ unions have organized strikes and protests to demand higher wages and better working conditions. The intersection of these two policy arenas—climate and education—has amplified the intensity of the protests, as activists see both as essential to the future of France’s young citizens [2].

The scale of the closures—over 400 schools—reflects the widespread nature of the protests. While the exact distribution of closed schools varies by region, data from local prefectures indicate a concentration in Île‑de‑France, Grand Est, and Hauts‑de‑France, where student and teacher participation in demonstrations was highest. The closures affected an estimated 400 000 students and 10 000 teachers nationwide, according to preliminary estimates from the Ministry of Education and teacher unions [2]. The sheer number of affected institutions underscores the magnitude of the protest movement and its capacity to disrupt national systems.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Government response

The Prime Minister’s office emphasized the importance of maintaining order and ensuring the safety of students and staff. The government reiterated its commitment to the Paris Agreement and announced that it would review the education budget to address concerns about teacher pay and class sizes. However, it warned that it would not tolerate disruptions that compromise the integrity of the educational system. The Ministry of Education clarified that it had no legal authority to close schools and that any closures were carried out by local authorities in response to security concerns [2].

Protest organizers

Student leaders and environmental groups framed the closures as a strategic protest tactic designed to force the government to confront its climate and education policies. They argued that the closures demonstrated the urgency of the climate crisis and the need for immediate action. Some organizers highlighted the role of teachers’ unions in supporting the protests, noting that teachers had joined the demonstrations to demand better resources and working conditions. The protests were coordinated through social media, with hashtags such as #ClimatEtÉducation and #StopCuts trending across France during the week of the closures [2].

Teachers’ perspective

Teachers’ unions expressed mixed reactions to the closures. While some praised the protests for drawing attention to the need for better funding and resources, others worried about the impact on students’ learning and the additional workload on teachers who had to adapt to sudden changes. The unions emphasized that the closures were a direct result of the government’s budget cuts, which had left many schools understaffed and under‑resourced. They called for a comprehensive review of the education budget and for the government to honor its commitments to teacher pay and class‑size reductions [2].

Students’ perspective

Students participating in the protests cited a sense of moral duty to act on climate change and to demand better educational conditions. Many expressed frustration with the lack of meaningful action from the government, noting that the climate targets set in the Paris Agreement were not being met. Students also highlighted the need for schools to incorporate climate education into the curriculum and to adopt sustainable practices. For many, the closures represented a sacrifice in pursuit of a larger goal of climate justice and educational equity [2].

Parents’ perspective

Parents expressed frustration and concern over the sudden disruption to their children’s education. Many reported difficulties arranging alternative childcare or adjusting work schedules to accommodate the closures. Some worried about the safety of their children in the context of protests that spilled onto school grounds. While a minority supported the protests as a necessary step to address climate and education issues, the majority focused on the immediate logistical challenges posed by the closures [2].

Public perception

Public opinion on the closures was divided. A significant portion of the French population supported the protests, citing the urgency of climate action and the need to address education funding cuts. However, a sizable segment viewed the closures as disruptive and detrimental to students’ learning. Opinion polls conducted by independent research firms indicated that while 55 % of respondents agreed that the protests were justified, only 30 % believed that the closures were an effective means of achieving policy change. The debate highlighted the tension between the moral imperatives of climate activism and the practical realities of maintaining an uninterrupted education system [2].

What to watch (future implications)

Policy debates on climate action

The school closures are likely to influence upcoming legislative debates on France’s climate policy. The government has already signaled its intent to review its climate commitments, and the protests may accelerate the introduction of new measures to meet Paris Agreement targets. The French Parliament is scheduled to convene a committee on climate policy in the coming weeks, and the protests may shape the agenda of that committee. The government’s response to the protests, including any concessions or policy proposals, will be closely monitored by environmental groups and political analysts alike [2].

Education budget negotiations

The closures have also brought renewed attention to the education budget. The French government is expected to present a revised budget that addresses teacher pay, class sizes, and the provision of extracurricular programs. Teachers’ unions will likely use the momentum from the protests to push for more substantial funding. The outcome of these negotiations will determine whether the government can reconcile its fiscal constraints with the demands of teachers, students, and environmental activists [2].

Long‑term impact on protest tactics

If the closures succeed in prompting policy changes, they may embolden protestors to use school shutdowns as a recurring tactic. The government may need to develop clearer protocols for managing protests that threaten public institutions. Balancing the right to protest with the need to protect educational continuity will remain a key challenge for policymakers and civil society alike [2].

International ripple effects

France’s experience may influence climate and education protests in other European countries. The visibility of school closures as a protest tactic could inspire similar actions elsewhere, prompting a broader re‑examination of how governments address climate commitments and education funding across the continent. International observers will watch how France navigates the delicate balance between civic engagement and the maintenance of essential public services [2].

In sum, the closures of more than 400 schools on Monday underscore the growing intersection of climate activism and education reform in France. While the immediate disruption was significant, the protests have opened a window for policy debate and potential reform. How the government responds will shape the future of France’s climate strategy, education system, and the broader relationship between citizens and the state.
