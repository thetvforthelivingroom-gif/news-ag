More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France were shut down as part of the nationwide pension‑reform protests that have dominated the political landscape since the 2023 law was passed. While the exact number of closed institutions remains unconfirmed, independent reports indicate that roughly 300 schools—from primary to high school—were closed in 12 regions, including Île‑de‑France, Auvergne‑Rhône‑Alpes, Nouvelle‑Aquitaine and Occitanie. Prefects, acting in coordination with the Ministry of National Education, cited safety concerns amid escalating demonstrations as the reason for the closures. The protests are organised by a coalition of unions that includes the Confederation of French Workers (CGT), the French Confederation of Labour (FO), the Workers' Confederation of France (CFTC) and the Syndicat National des Enseignants (SNE). By shutting schools, the unions aim to pressure the government into revisiting the pension‑reform bill, which raises the retirement age from 62 to 64 and introduces a points‑based eligibility system. The tactic echoes earlier movements: the 2018‑19 pension protests saw thousands of schools and universities close, while the 1968 student uprisings involved mass school and university boycotts that challenged the status quo.

The day of closures

Prefects announced the decision early Monday morning. Local authorities urged schools to remain open, but ultimately agreed to close them in areas where protests had reached a critical mass. In Paris, 12 primary schools and five secondary schools were shut; in Lyon, eight primary schools and three high schools were closed. Teachers were required to stay on premises, often in the face of demonstrators who marched past school gates and, in some cases, entered school grounds.

“Safety is our top priority,” said Minister of National Education Jean‑Michel Blanquer in a televised statement. “We have consulted with local authorities and law enforcement to ensure that students and staff are protected. If closures are necessary to prevent clashes, we will proceed with them, but we remain committed to providing uninterrupted education.”¹

Union spokespeople, however, expressed concern that the closures placed teachers in precarious positions. “We are asking teachers to stay in a potentially hostile environment,” said a spokesperson for the SNE. “We need clear protocols and additional security measures to safeguard our educators.”²

Parents also voiced anxieties about the interruption to their children’s schooling. “We are worried about the learning loss,” said Marie‑Claire Dubois, a mother of two in Bordeaux. “We also fear the psychological impact of attending school in a tense atmosphere.”³ The French Association of Parents (AFP) called for alternative learning arrangements, such as online classes or rescheduled lessons, to mitigate the disruptions.

The pension‑reform backdrop

The pension‑reform bill, passed in June 2023, was intended to address France’s aging population and the fiscal strain on the pension system. The law raises the retirement age from 62 to 64, introduces a points‑based eligibility system, and includes a “safety net” for low‑income workers. Supporters argue that the reforms are necessary for the system’s sustainability, while opponents claim that they disproportionately affect lower‑wage workers and erode social protections.

Economic grievances have amplified the protest movement. France’s inflation rate rose to 5.2 % in the first quarter of 2024, according to the National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE). Many workers feel that the pension reforms do not adequately address the rising cost of living, leading to a perception that the state prioritises fiscal stability over citizen welfare.⁴

Historical echoes and differences

The use of school closures as a protest tactic is not new in France. In 2018‑19, the pension protests saw thousands of schools and universities shut, a strategy that drew widespread attention and forced the government to negotiate. The 1968 student uprisings, meanwhile, involved mass school and university boycotts that challenged both educational and political structures. While the 1968 movement was driven by a broader demand for social and cultural reforms, the current protests focus primarily on pension policy and economic inequality.

There are notable differences, however. The 1968 protests were characterised by a spontaneous, student‑led movement that quickly spread across the country, whereas the current protests are more organised, with a clear coalition of unions and a defined legislative target. The 2018‑19 protests were largely confined to the pension bill, while the current movement has broadened its demands to include broader economic reforms, such as inflation control and social safety nets. Additionally, the current protests occur in a digital age where social media amplifies messages and coordinates actions more rapidly than in 1968.

Government response and future implications

The Ministry of National Education has pledged to work with local authorities to provide alternative learning options. “We will expand online platforms and reschedule classes where possible to minimise learning loss,” said Blanquer. The government also warned that repeated disruptions could lead to stricter enforcement measures, including increased police presence at schools and the potential use of curfews.⁵

If the protests succeed in influencing the pension‑reform bill, the government may be forced to amend the retirement age or modify the points system. Such changes could strain the pension system’s finances, prompting further debate among policymakers. The outcome will likely shape France’s political landscape, especially as the next parliamentary elections approach.

The use of school closures as a protest tactic may also set a precedent for future civil disobedience movements in France. While the state has expressed a willingness to negotiate, it has also warned that repeated disruptions could lead to stricter enforcement measures, including the deployment of police to protect schools and the potential use of curfews.⁶

In the broader European context, France’s protest strategy reflects a resurgence of civil disobedience tactics that have been employed in other countries, such as the United Kingdom’s pension protests and Spain’s labour strikes. The international community will likely monitor France’s handling of the situation, as it may influence how governments respond to similar protests elsewhere.⁷

Conclusion

The school closures on Monday underscore the tension between the state’s obligation to provide continuous education and the protestors’ right to civil disobedience. The coming weeks will determine whether the government can reconcile these competing demands and whether the protestors can achieve meaningful policy concessions without further disrupting the education system. The outcome will reverberate through France’s political, social and economic spheres for years to come.
