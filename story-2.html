More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> On Monday, 347 schools across France were temporarily closed in the wake of a nationwide wave of protests over pension and labor reforms. The closures were announced by local education authorities after pressure from unions and demonstrators, and were intended to prevent clashes between protestors and students on school grounds. According to a statement from the Ministry of National Education (MNE), the move was “necessary to ensure the safety of pupils and staff” and would be lifted as soon as the situation stabilized.¹ The decision affected an estimated 1.3 million students and 42,000 teachers, according to the MNE’s daily briefing. Local school boards reported that the closures were coordinated with police and transport authorities to avoid disruptions on roads already closed for demonstrators. In Paris, the École Normale Supérieure, a prominent higher‑education institution, temporarily suspended classes, while dozens of primary and secondary schools in the 11th arrondissement shut their doors.² The closures were not limited to urban centres; rural schools in the Auvergne‑Rhône-Alpes and Nouvelle‑Aquitaine regions also paused operations, citing safety concerns amid reports of clashes near school premises.³ The Ministry’s spokesperson, Claire Lefèvre, said that the national exam timetable would be postponed by at least two weeks to accommodate the disruptions. “We are working closely with regional education authorities to provide alternative learning arrangements, including online lessons and tutoring sessions,” she added.⁴ The MNE also announced that it would provide additional support to families affected by the closures, including temporary childcare vouchers for parents who had to take time off work.⁵ Unions have framed the closures as a necessary escalation to force the government to negotiate. The Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) issued a statement on Monday, calling the school shutdowns a “strategic pause” aimed at preventing further escalation of the protests. The CGT’s president, Jean‑Pierre Bouchard, said, “We are not asking for a halt to education, but for a pause that allows the government to listen to legitimate demands on pensions and labor rights.”⁶ The union also urged parents to support their children’s learning at home and to cooperate with teachers who would provide digital resources.⁷ Teachers, meanwhile, expressed mixed feelings. While some sympathised with the unions’ grievances, many feared that the closures could jeopardise their employment, as the government has hinted at restructuring the education sector in tandem with labor reforms. A spokesperson for the Syndicat National des Enseignants (SNE) said, “We are concerned about the long‑term impact on students’ learning outcomes, especially those in critical examination years.”⁸ Teachers in Marseille reported that the sudden pause left them scrambling to find alternative teaching methods, with some turning to online platforms to keep lessons running.⁹ Parents have been disproportionately affected by the sudden school closures. Many had to arrange overnight stays or rely on extended family to care for their children. “I had to take a day off work to supervise my son, who was in the middle of a math lesson,” said Marie‑Claire Dubois, a mother of two from Lyon.¹⁰ She added that the lack of clear communication from school authorities had added to her stress.¹¹ The MNE’s press release acknowledged the inconvenience and promised a “clear communication plan” to keep parents informed of any changes.¹² The closures are part of a broader strategy employed by unions to pressure the government. Historically, school shutdowns have been a recurring tactic in French protest movements. During the 2018 pension strikes, several schools were closed to prevent students from becoming targets of police action or to avoid the spread of unrest within educational settings.¹³ The 2019 Gilets Jaunes protests also saw sporadic school shutdowns, though on a smaller scale, as a symbolic gesture of solidarity with the broader movement.¹⁴ These precedents suggest that the current closures are part of a long‑standing strategy employed by protest leaders to exert pressure on the state by disrupting essential public services.¹⁵ From a legal standpoint, the MNE confirmed that the closures were in line with French law, which allows local authorities to suspend school operations in cases of public safety concerns. The ministry also stated that it would monitor the situation closely and would not allow schools to be used as protest venues.¹⁶ The Prime Minister’s office released a statement affirming that the government remains open to dialogue but will not compromise on the reforms. “We respect the right to protest, but we also have a duty to ensure that education continues uninterrupted,” said the Prime Minister’s spokesperson, Sophie Martin.¹⁷ Transportation authorities warned that school buses would be unable to operate safely on roads that were closed to traffic, further complicating access for students who rely on public transport. The national transport agency, SNCF, issued a notice that school buses would resume operations only after the protest routes were cleared.¹⁸ The MNE also announced that it would provide temporary digital learning platforms to ensure that students could continue their studies from home.¹⁹ The closures have drawn criticism from some politicians, who argue that disrupting education is an unnecessary sacrifice of continuity for political ends. The Socialist Party’s parliamentary group called for a review of the decision, stating that “education is a public good that should not be compromised by protest tactics.”²⁰ The opposition also urged the government to find a more balanced approach that protects students’ rights while addressing the protesters’ demands.²¹ Looking ahead, the Ministry of National Education expects to resume classes by the end of the week, contingent on the easing of protest activity. The MNE will keep the public informed of any further changes and will provide additional support to teachers and families as needed. The government remains committed to negotiating with unions while ensuring that the education system remains stable and that students receive the support they need during this turbulent period.²²
