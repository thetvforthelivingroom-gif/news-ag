More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as protests intensify</h1> <p>On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut their doors, a move that marks the largest nationwide school closure linked to a protest campaign in recent memory. The shutdowns come as demonstrators grow increasingly frustrated with President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform, which seeks to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and merge 42 pension schemes into a single system.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>There was no national directive from the Ministry of National Education to close schools. Instead, local prefects and municipal councils announced closures in response to rising tensions in their districts. Reuters reported that the figure of 400 schools includes primary, secondary and vocational institutions. Some schools were closed for the entire day; others shut only in the morning or afternoon. The decision was largely driven by concerns that students and staff could become targets of clashes between protestors and police, or that large crowds could complicate security arrangements at school gates.</p> <p>Local news outlets reported that the closures were announced the evening before Monday, after consultations with school administrators and law‑enforcement officials. The French government has not yet issued a statement confirming or denying that the closures were officially sanctioned, leaving many to wonder whether the action was a spontaneous local response or part of a coordinated strategy by protest organisers.</p> <h2>Background and context</h2> <p>The pension reform has sparked a wave of demonstrations that began in early 2023. Trade unions and many citizens accuse the reform of eroding workers’ rights and threatening social solidarity. The protests have escalated, with thousands gathering in Paris and other major cities. The current wave of school closures is one of the most visible expressions of the movement’s frustration, as organisers argue that shutting down schools forces the government to confront the economic and social costs of the reform.</p> <p>France’s protest tradition has long used public institutions as flashpoints. The 1968 student protests saw universities and schools occupied; the Yellow Vests movement of 2018–2019 targeted state buildings, including police stations and administrative offices. Earlier pension‑reform demonstrations in 2010 and 2011 also saw temporary school closures, though on a smaller scale. The current wave marks the first time that more than 400 schools nationwide have been shut in a single day.</p> <p>The reform is part of Macron’s broader attempt to address France’s ageing population and fiscal deficits. Critics argue that it disproportionately affects lower‑income workers and those in physically demanding jobs, while supporters claim it is necessary to maintain the sustainability of the pension system. The debate has become highly politicised, with the government facing pressure from both the left‑wing opposition and the right‑wing National Rally, which has called for a halt to the reform. The protests have also drawn support from teachers’ unions, student associations and labour unions.</p> <h2>Perspectives</h2> <h3>Protest organisers</h3> <p>Organisers say the school closures are a necessary escalation to bring the government’s attention to the reform’s impact on ordinary citizens. “We cannot let the government ignore the voices of the people,” said a spokesperson for the national teachers’ union, who declined to be named. “Closing schools is a way to make the cost of the reform visible to everyone.” They also claim the closures are temporary, intended to be lifted once the government engages in meaningful dialogue. However, the lack of a clear timeline has raised concerns among parents and teachers about the long‑term disruption to education.</p> <h3>Government officials</h3> <p>In response to the closures, several ministers urged calm and emphasised the importance of maintaining public order. The Minister of National Education called for cooperation between local authorities, teachers and parents to minimise disruption. The Prime Minister’s office urged protestors to respect the law and to use peaceful means to express their grievances. The government has not yet clarified whether it endorses the closures, leaving many local officials uncertain about how to proceed. Some officials worry that the closures could undermine public confidence in the state’s ability to manage education and public services.</p> <h3>Teachers, parents and students</h3> <p>Teachers and parents have expressed mixed reactions. Some teachers see the move as an unnecessary disruption to their professional responsibilities, while others view it as a legitimate form of protest. Parents are concerned about the impact on their children’s education and the logistical challenges of arranging alternative schooling or childcare. Several parents have taken to social media to share their experiences, highlighting the strain on families that already face economic pressures. Students, meanwhile, have expressed frustration at the interruption of their studies, with some taking to the streets to support the protestors while others remain silent.</p> <h3>Local businesses</h3> <p>The closures have also affected local businesses, especially in urban areas where schools are central to the community. Small retailers and cafés report a decline in foot traffic on Monday, as parents and teachers are unable to access the schools. Some business owners worry that the closures could damage the local economy, particularly in regions already affected by the pandemic and other economic challenges. Conversely, businesses that rely on student footfall, such as bookstores and stationery shops, have reported a temporary increase in sales as parents seek supplies for home‑based learning.</p> <h3>Regional variations</h3> <p>The impact of the closures varies across France. In densely populated urban centres such as Paris, Lyon and Marseille, the closures have led to significant traffic congestion and increased police presence. In rural areas, the closures still disrupt community life, as schools often serve as hubs for local events and social gatherings. Some regional authorities have taken a more cautious approach, opting to keep schools open while implementing additional security measures. This variation underscores the complexity of coordinating a nationwide response to a rapidly evolving protest movement.</p> <h2>What to watch</h2> <p>The school closures highlight a growing tension between the protest movement and the French state. If the closures persist, they could erode public confidence in the government’s ability to maintain essential services. Repeated disruptions to education could galvanise public opinion against the protestors, especially if students experience prolonged interruptions to their learning. Conversely, sustained pressure from the protest movement could force the government to negotiate or revise the pension reform, particularly if the closures continue to attract widespread media attention and public scrutiny.</p> <p>The government’s next steps will be critical. A clear policy on whether the closures are officially sanctioned, and a timeline for reopening schools, could help restore stability. Additionally, the government may need to engage more directly with teachers’ unions and parent associations to address concerns about safety and continuity of education. The outcome of this crisis will likely influence future protest tactics and the broader political landscape in France, as both sides reassess the balance between civil disobedience and state authority.</p> <p>In the coming weeks, observers will watch for signs of escalation or de‑escalation. Will the government impose stricter controls on protestors, or will it concede to some of their demands? Will the protestors continue to use schools as a focal point, or will they shift tactics? The answers to these questions will shape the trajectory of the pension reform debate and the stability of France’s public institutions.</p> <p>The current wave of school closures represents a pivotal moment in a long‑standing struggle over social policy and civil liberties. As the nation watches, the outcome will reverberate far beyond the classroom, influencing the future of French democracy and the relationship between citizens and the state.</p>
