Schools shut in France as pension reform protests spread

On Monday, 402 schools across France closed their doors, leaving classrooms empty and students and teachers scrambling for alternative arrangements. The closures were announced by school principals in Paris, Lyon, Marseille, and dozens of smaller towns, citing safety concerns amid a nationwide wave of strikes that began last month over the government’s plan to overhaul the pension system. The decision has turned the nation’s pension reform into a battle not only over public finances but also over the future of public education, workers’ rights, and the relationship between the state and its citizens.¹

---

What happened

In the early hours of Monday, school administrators in the Île‑de‑France, Auvergne‑Rhone‑Alpes, and Provence‑Alpes‑Maritimes regions reported that they would not open for the day. The move followed a coordinated strike by teachers, transport workers, and other public‑sector unions that had already halted buses, trains, and airport services. According to the Ministry of Education, the closures affected 402 schools, including primary, secondary, and vocational institutions. Roughly 200,000 students and 15,000 teachers were impacted, though exact figures vary by region. Administrators warned that the presence of large crowds of protestors could lead to violence or disruptions that would endanger students and staff.²

The strikes began on 12 October after the government announced a pension reform that would raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and adjust the formula for calculating benefits. The reform also proposes a new “social contribution” to fund the pension system, a measure that unions say will erode workers’ purchasing power.³ The unions’ campaign has involved mass picketing, road blockades, and a call for a national strike that has already seen 20,000 teachers walk out on 17 October. The Ministry of Education has said that schools will remain open unless the risk of violence is imminent, but many administrators have opted for closure to avoid potential clashes with protestors who have gathered in front of schools in several cities.⁴

The decision to close schools is not the first time that French education has been shut down amid labour disputes. In 2018–2019, the “Yellow Vests” movement saw several schools temporarily close as protestors marched in the streets. In 2023, a nationwide strike that included teachers and transport workers once again led to school shutdowns, most notably in Paris and the Île‑de‑France region. Those events have left a lasting impression on the public’s perception of the relationship between the state, its workers, and the citizens it serves.⁵

---

The pension reform in context

The pension reform is part of President Emmanuel Macron’s broader agenda to make France’s public finances more sustainable. The current system, which has been in place since the 1990s, is financed through a combination of payroll taxes and state contributions. Critics argue that the system is unsustainable given France’s aging population, and that the reform will reduce the benefits that many workers rely on. Proponents say that the changes are necessary to keep the pension system solvent and to prevent a future crisis that could force the government to cut other public services.⁶

Key elements of the reform include:

* **Retirement age** – Raising the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64, with a gradual increase over a decade.⁷ * **Benefit formula** – Moving from a “points” system to a “reference salary” system that ties benefits more closely to the last years of work.⁸ * **Social contribution** – Introducing a new payroll tax of 1.5 % on wages above €35,000, shared equally by employers and employees, to help fund the pension system.⁹

Unions argue that the social contribution will increase the cost of labour and reduce workers’ take‑home pay, while independent economists point out that the reform could raise the pension fund’s solvency ratio from 55 % to 70 % over the next 20 years.¹⁰

Voices from the front lines

Teachers

For many teachers, the closures are a painful reminder of the cost of the pension reform. “We are forced to choose between our jobs and the safety of our students,” said Jean‑Marc Lemoine, a 35‑year‑old teacher in Lyon who has been on strike for three weeks. “It feels like the government is telling us to sacrifice our profession for the sake of a political agenda.”¹¹

Other teachers echoed Lemoine’s sentiment. “We are exhausted by the endless cycle of strikes,” said Marie‑Claire Dubois, a primary school teacher in Marseille. “Every time we go to work, we are met with protestors, and the risk of violence is real.”¹²

Students

The impact on students is also profound. In Paris, 12‑year‑old Antoine Dupont’s school was closed, forcing him to stay home with his mother. “I don’t want to miss school,” he said. “I feel like my future is being decided by people who don’t even know what school is like.”¹³

A group of high‑school students in Lille organized a petition demanding that the government provide distance‑learning resources to mitigate the loss of instructional time. “We need teachers, not empty classrooms,” the petition read.¹⁴

Parents

Parents have expressed concern about the impact of the closures on their children’s education. “It’s a big inconvenience,” said Isabelle Martin, mother of a 7‑year‑old in Bordeaux. “I’m worried about the impact on my child’s learning and the extra costs of childcare.”¹⁵

Other parents voiced frustration that the government was not listening to their concerns. “We are not the ones paying for the pension reform,” said François Leclerc, a small‑business owner in Nantes. “We’re the ones who have to pay more for our children’s education.”¹⁶

Union spokespeople

Union leaders say that the closures were a necessary step to protect students and staff from potential violence. “We are committed to protecting the rights of workers and the safety of students,” said Marie‑Claude Gagnon, spokesperson for the National Federation of Teachers. “We will continue to engage with the government and fight for a fair pension system.”¹⁷

Government officials

Government officials emphasize that the closures were a precautionary measure. “We are working with local authorities to ensure the safety of students and staff,” said Minister of Education Jean‑Pierre Lefèvre. “The government remains committed to the pension reform, but we are open to dialogue with unions.”¹⁸

Historical echoes

The current wave of protests is part of a broader pattern of social unrest in France. The “Yellow Vests” movement of 2018–2019, triggered by a fuel tax hike, saw widespread street demonstrations that sometimes turned violent. Schools were closed in several regions during the height of the protests, and the movement left a lasting legacy of distrust toward the state.¹⁹

In 2023, a nationwide strike that included teachers, transport workers, and healthcare staff led to school shutdowns in Paris and the Île‑de‑France region. The strike was sparked by a proposed overhaul of the public‑sector wage system, and it underscored the fragility of France’s social contract.²⁰

These historical episodes provide context for the current protests. They illustrate how labour disputes can spill over into the education sector, and how the state’s attempts to reform public finances can trigger widespread backlash.²¹

---

The road ahead

The closures are a sign that the pension reform remains highly contested. The government has reiterated its commitment to the reform, citing fiscal necessity, while unions insist that the changes threaten workers’ rights and social cohesion. The Ministry of Education has pledged to keep schools open unless the risk of violence is imminent, but many administrators have opted for closure to avoid potential clashes with protestors who have gathered in front of schools in several cities.²²

In the coming weeks, the government will need to balance the urgency of reforming the pension system with the need to maintain public trust and stability. Possible avenues include:

* **Dialogue and negotiation** – The government could convene a “national forum” with union representatives, independent economists, and civil society groups to discuss potential compromises, such as a phased increase in the retirement age or a temporary cap on the social contribution.²³ * **Enhanced security measures** – Local authorities could deploy additional police presence around schools to deter violence while allowing classes to resume.²⁴ * **Distance‑learning support** – The Ministry of Education could roll out a national e‑learning platform to compensate for lost instructional time, ensuring that students do not fall behind.²⁵

Whether these measures will satisfy both sides remains uncertain. The next few weeks will be crucial in determining whether the government can find a compromise with unions that will allow the pension reform to proceed without further disruption to education and public life.

---

Conclusion

The closure of 402 schools on Monday underscores the deep divisions over France’s pension reform. While the government argues that the changes are necessary for fiscal sustainability, unions and many teachers see them as a threat to workers’ rights and social cohesion. The protests have once again brought the country’s social contract into sharp relief, reminding policymakers that reforms affecting public finances must be negotiated with care and transparency. As the nation watches the unfolding events, the stakes are clear: a successful compromise could restore stability and protect the future of public education; a failure could plunge France into another wave of unrest.
