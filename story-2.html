More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut down as teachers’ unions stage a nationwide protest against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension and labour reforms. The closures, announced by the Ministry of Education in coordination with the unions, represent the largest coordinated school shutdown since the Yellow‑Vests movement in 2016 and underscore a deepening rift between the state and the nation’s educators.<sup>1</sup> The action comes amid a wave of demonstrations that have already seen thousands of protesters block roads, occupy public spaces, and demand a halt to reforms many view as a threat to workers’ rights and social solidarity.<sup>1</sup>

The scale of the shutdown

A joint statement from the Ministry and the unions SNES (Syndicat National des Enseignants de l’Enseignement Secondaire) and CFDT (Confédération Française Démocratique du Travail) announced that 412 schools will close on Monday, the first day of the new academic year. The closures will affect 1.2 million students and 70 000 teachers nationwide, with the majority of schools in urban centres such as Paris, Lyon, Marseille, and Lille, and a smaller number in rural districts.<sup>2</sup> The Ministry urged parents to use the day for self‑study and to consult online resources, but many teachers and students expressed uncertainty about how to make the most of the lost instructional time.<sup>3</sup>

A historic tactic revisited

School closures as a protest tactic are not new in France. During the 2016 Yellow‑Vests protests, teachers’ unions and civil‑society groups closed over 300 schools to disrupt the economy and draw attention to grievances against the government.<sup>4</sup> The current closures are the largest since then, reflecting an escalation in the unions’ strategy to pressure the government into revisiting the pension reform. The decision followed a meeting between the Ministry of Education and union leaders on Sunday evening, with the unions citing the lack of progress in negotiations as the main reason for the action.<sup>5</sup>

The pension reform at the heart of the dispute

Macron’s pension reform, introduced in 2023, aims to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and merge the 42 existing pension schemes into a single points‑based system. Critics argue that the reform erodes workers’ rights and disproportionately affects lower‑income and public‑sector employees. The reform sparked widespread opposition, with protests swelling to millions across France in 2023.<sup>6</sup> The school closures are part of a broader pattern of civil‑society mobilization that has become a hallmark of French protest culture, where public institutions are used strategically to amplify dissent.<sup>7</sup>

European context and the use of public‑sector shutdowns

The unions’ strategy reflects a broader European trend of using public‑sector shutdowns to pressure governments over labour and social policy. In several European countries, teachers and public‑sector workers have staged strikes and school closures to demand reforms that protect workers’ rights and public services.<sup>8</sup> The French case is notable for its scale and the fact that it occurs at the start of a new academic year, thereby amplifying its symbolic impact on the nation’s education system.<sup>9</sup> The protests also continue the legacy of the Yellow‑Vests movement, which demonstrated the power of coordinated civil‑society action to disrupt normal life and force political concessions.<sup>10</sup>

Voices from the front lines

From the perspective of teachers, the closures are a necessary measure to protect their professional interests and the quality of education. Union spokespeople argue that the reforms threaten job security, working conditions, and the integrity of the public education system. “We cannot allow the state to impose a pension system that erodes the dignity of public servants,” said Jean‑Baptiste Drouet, president of CFDT. “The closures are a last resort to force the state to reconsider the reforms.”<sup>11</sup> Teachers also point to the broader impact on students, who face uncertainty about their learning trajectory and exam schedules.<sup>12</sup>

Parents and students are caught in the crossfire. Many parents express concern about the disruption to their children’s education and the potential delay of academic milestones. “My son was already struggling with his math homework; now we have no school and no teacher to guide him,” said Claire, a mother of two from Lyon.<sup>13</sup> Some students see the protests as a way to assert their agency and demand a fairer system, while others are anxious about the uncertainty and the possible long‑term effects on their future prospects.<sup>13</sup> The government’s position is that the reforms are necessary to ensure the long‑term sustainability of the pension system and to reduce the fiscal burden on the state. “We are committed to protecting the rights of workers, but we must also safeguard the country’s economic future,” said Interior Minister Gérald Darmanin.<sup>14</sup>

The impact on the education system

The closures will affect an estimated 1.2 million students and 70 000 teachers nationwide. In urban centres such as Paris, Lyon, and Marseille, schools will be shut for the entire day, while in rural areas some institutions will remain open under special arrangements. The Ministry of Education has urged parents to use the day for self‑study and to consult online resources, but many teachers and students are uncertain about how to make the most of the lost instructional time. “We are exploring alternative learning methods, but the disruption is undeniable,” said Jean‑Pierre, a high‑school principal in Bordeaux.<sup>15</sup>

A broader struggle over social policy

The protests are part of a broader struggle over social policy that has defined French politics in recent years. The pension reform is just one element of a series of changes that have aimed to modernise the French welfare state, including labour market reforms, tax adjustments, and public‑sector restructuring. Critics argue that these reforms disproportionately benefit the wealthy and undermine the social safety net that has been a cornerstone of French society. The unions’ use of school closures is a powerful reminder that the state’s reforms are not merely policy adjustments but a challenge to the collective values that underpin the nation.<sup>16</sup>

The future of the protest movement

As the protests continue, the unions have signalled that they will maintain pressure on the government until a comprehensive dialogue is established. “We will not stop until the state recognizes the legitimate concerns of teachers and workers,” said Jean‑Baptiste Drouet.<sup>17</sup> The government, meanwhile, has pledged to review the reforms in light of the public backlash, but it remains cautious about making substantive changes that could jeopardise the fiscal stability of the pension system.<sup>17</sup> The outcome of this clash will shape not only the future of French pensions but also the broader trajectory of social policy in the country.

Conclusion

The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday is a stark illustration of the deepening divide between the French state and its educators. It underscores the intensity of the protests against President Macron’s pension and labour reforms and highlights the willingness of teachers’ unions to employ unprecedented tactics to protect their interests. As the nation watches the impact of this unprecedented shutdown, the debate over the future of France’s social safety net and the role of public institutions in shaping that future will continue to dominate headlines.<sup>18</sup>
