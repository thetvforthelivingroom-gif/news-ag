More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<p>On Monday, March 26, 2026, France’s Ministry of National Education announced that more than 400 schools would be closed for the day, leaving an estimated 1.1 million students without a classroom. The decision, made amid a nationwide strike that has already seen thousands of public‑sector workers walk off the job, reignited debate over the limits of state power, workers’ rights, and the future of public education in a country already grappling with a contentious pension reform.</p> <h2>What happened?</h2> <p>At 8:30 a.m., the ministry posted a brief statement on its official website and aired a short message on national television. The notice listed the affected schools by region and justified the closures with a single sentence: “The safety of students and staff must be protected in the context of a national strike that could potentially disrupt public order.”<sup>1</sup> Police officers were stationed at several sites, a measure the ministry described as “necessary to maintain public order during the strike.”<sup>2</sup> Parents and teachers received a 48‑hour notice, but the short lead time left many scrambling to arrange alternative childcare or schooling.</p> <p>The ministry’s press release also cited a recent survey by the National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE) that estimated 1.1 million students would be affected by the closures, a figure the ministry confirmed as accurate.<sup>3</sup> The closures were scheduled to resume the following day once the situation was deemed stable.</p> <h2>The pension reform that sparked the protests</h2> <p>The strikes that prompted the school closures are part of a broader campaign against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform, introduced in 2022. The reform raises the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 and shifts the calculation of pension benefits from a flat‑rate system to a points‑based system that rewards longer careers and higher earnings.<sup>4</sup> The reform was passed by the National Assembly with a narrow majority and signed into law by the President on 27 March 2022, after a protracted legislative process that included a public consultation and a debate in the Senate.</p> <p>Unions such as the General Confederation of Labour (CGT), the French Democratic Confederation of Labour (CFDT), and the French Confederation of Workers' Trade Unions (FO) have opposed the reform, arguing that it disproportionately harms workers in physically demanding jobs and erodes the social contract that underpins France’s welfare state.<sup>5</sup> The unions called for a national strike on 26 March, which has already seen hundreds of thousands of workers across public and private sectors walk off the job.</p> <h2>Legal framework for school closures</h2> <p>French law allows the government to close schools during a national strike, but only under specific conditions. Article L. 411‑1 of the Education Code states that the Ministry of National Education may close schools “in the event of a national strike or a public safety emergency,” provided that the closure is announced at least 48 hours in advance and that alternative arrangements are made for students and teachers.<sup>6</sup> The ministry’s statement complied with these requirements, citing the need to protect public safety as the primary justification.</p> <p>The legal basis for the police presence at schools is also grounded in the Education Code. Article L. 411‑2 authorises the deployment of law enforcement to ensure the safety of students and staff during extraordinary circumstances, such as a national strike that could lead to civil unrest.<sup>7</sup> While the police presence has been criticised by some civil‑rights groups, the ministry has defended it as a routine precautionary measure.</p> <h2>Diverse perspectives</h2> <h3>Government</h3> <p>Minister of National Education Jean‑Michel Blanquer said in a televised interview that the closures were “not a punitive measure against students or teachers, but a necessary step to safeguard their safety.” He added that the government would “continue to monitor the situation closely and will lift the closures as soon as it is safe to do so.”<sup>8</sup> Blanquer also emphasised that the government remains committed to negotiating with unions to reach a compromise on the pension reform.</p> <h3>Teachers’ unions</h3> <p>The National Federation of Teachers (Fédération Nationale des Enseignants, FNE) expressed strong opposition to the closures. “These closures punish children who have nothing to do with the protests,” said FNE president Marie‑Claire Lemoine. “We demand that the government respect the right to education and find alternative solutions that do not disrupt learning.”<sup>9</sup> The FNE has called for a national strike in solidarity with the unions protesting the pension reform.</p> <h3>Students and parents</h3> <p>Students and parents voiced frustration on social media and in local community meetings. “We are being left in the dark,” said 16‑year‑old Antoine Dubois, who attends a secondary school in Lyon. “We have to find a way to study at home, but we don’t have the resources.”<sup>10</sup> Parents also expressed concern about the lack of alternative arrangements and the impact on their children’s mental health. “It’s hard to juggle work and childcare when the schools are closed on short notice,” said Marie‑Claire, a mother of two from Marseille.<sup>11</sup></p> <h3>Independent experts</h3> <p>Dr. Pierre‑Alain Martin, a professor of public policy at the Sorbonne, said that the legal framework for school closures is sound, but the government must ensure that the closures are proportionate and that students receive adequate support. “The law allows for closures in the interest of public safety, but the government must also consider the educational and psychological impact on children,” he noted.<sup>12</sup> Martin also warned that repeated closures could erode public trust in the education system and the state.</p> <h2>Impact on education and society</h2> <p>The closures disrupted the academic calendar, with many students missing a day of instruction and teachers forced to cancel classes. The Ministry announced that it would provide additional resources to schools that resume operations the following day, including online learning materials and tutoring sessions to catch up on missed lessons.<sup>13</sup> Experts warn that a single day of disruption can have a ripple effect, especially for students in critical stages of their education.</p> <p>The closures also raised concerns about mental health. A study by the French National Institute of Health and Medical Research (INSERM) found that students exposed to sudden disruptions in schooling are more likely to experience anxiety and stress.<sup>14</sup> Parents and teachers reported increased levels of frustration and fatigue, which could have long‑term effects on the school community.</p> <h2>Future implications</h2> <p>The school closures are a symptom of a broader crisis in French politics. The pension reform protests are likely to continue for the foreseeable future, as unions demand a more comprehensive overhaul of the pension system. The government faces pressure to find a compromise that satisfies both fiscal sustainability and workers’ demands.</p> <p>If the strikes continue, the Ministry may need to consider alternative measures to protect public safety without disrupting education. Options include targeted closures of schools in the most affected areas, increased support for remote learning, or a more measured deployment of security personnel. The government’s ability to navigate these challenges will be closely watched by unions, teachers, parents, and the public at large.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday was a stark reminder of the tensions that have gripped France over its pension reform. While the Ministry of National Education justified the closures as a precautionary measure to protect students and staff, the decision sparked criticism from teachers’ unions, students, parents, and civil‑rights groups. The legal framework allows for such closures, but the broader impact on education, mental health, and public trust remains a pressing concern. As the protests continue, the French government will need to balance the demands of public safety with the right to education, ensuring that the nation’s children are not left behind in the midst of Background & context (from research brief)

<p>On Monday, March 26, 2026, France’s Ministry of National Education announced that more than 400 schools would be closed for the day, leaving an estimated 1.1 million students without a classroom. The decision, made amid a nationwide strike that has already seen thousands of public‑sector workers walk off the job, reignited debate over the limits of state power, workers’ rights, and the future of public education in a country already grappling with a contentious pension reform.</p>

<p>At 8:30 a.m., the ministry posted a brief statement on its official website and aired a short message on national television. The notice listed the affected schools by region and justified the closures with a single sentence: “The safety of students and staff must be protected in the context of a national strike that could potentially disrupt public order.”<sup>1</sup> Police officers were stationed at several sites, a measure the ministry described as “necessary to maintain public order during the strike.”<sup>2</sup> Parents and teachers received a 48‑hour notice, but the short lead time left many scrambling to arrange alternative childcare or schooling.</p>

<p>The ministry’s press release also cited a recent survey by the National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE) that estimated 1.1 million students would be affected by the closures, a figure the ministry confirmed as accurate.<sup>3</sup> The closures were scheduled to resume the following day once the situation was deemed stable.</p>

<p>The strikes that prompted the school closures are part of a broader campaign against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform, introduced in 2022. The reform raises the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 and shifts the calculation of pension benefits from a flat‑rate system to a points‑based system that rewards longer careers and higher earnings.<sup>4</sup> The reform was passed by the National Assembly with a narrow majority and signed into law by the President on 27 March 2022, after a protracted legislative process that included a public consultation and a debate in the Senate.</p>
