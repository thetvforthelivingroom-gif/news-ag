More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
On Monday, more than 400 French schools shut their doors as protests over pension and education reforms erupted, sending shockwaves through classrooms and the nation’s political landscape. The closures, spread across both urban centres and rural regions, marked the most extensive school strike in France in over a decade and underscored the deepening divide between the government’s reform agenda and the teachers’ demand for better conditions and protections. The disruption affected roughly 1.2 million students nationwide, with teachers and support staff also walking out in solidarity. The scale of the action highlights how policy decisions on pensions and public spending can ripple through the education system, affecting everyday life for millions of families. [T2]

What happened

The strike began early on Monday morning, with teachers from the Syndicat National des Enseignants (SNE) and other federations refusing to work across 423 schools in 12 regions, from Île‑de‑France to Brittany. In Paris, the largest capital city, 70 schools were closed, while in the rural departments of Aveyron and Moselle, the closures were almost total. Student groups, including the Union Nationale des Étudiants de France (UNEF), joined the protest, marching in front of school gates and demanding that the government halt the pension reform that would raise the retirement age to 64. The Ministry of Education issued a statement urging teachers to return to the classroom, but the unions maintained that the reforms threatened the quality of education and the welfare of public servants. The day ended with a partial return in some schools, but the overall impact remained significant. [T2]

Background and context

The trigger for the strike is the government’s pension reform, which aims to unify France’s 42 pension schemes into a single points‑based system and raise the retirement age from 62 to 64. The reform was announced in 2022 and has faced fierce opposition from unions, who argue that it undermines workers’ rights and the social contract. In addition, the Ministry of Finance announced a 2 % cut in the education budget for 2024, citing fiscal consolidation measures. Teachers’ unions view the budget cuts as a direct attack on public education, citing a projected loss of 1.5 billion euros in funding for schools, which would affect class sizes, extracurricular programs, and support services. The protests are part of a broader wave of social unrest that began with the Yellow Vests movement in 2019 and the 2018 pension protests, both of which highlighted the public’s frustration with perceived neoliberal policies. The unions’ demands are multifaceted: they seek a halt to the pension reform, a restoration of the 2016 education budget, and the introduction of a 30‑hour work week for teachers. They also call for increased investment in digital infrastructure and a reduction in class sizes to improve learning outcomes. The government, led by President Emmanuel Macron, has repeatedly defended the reforms as necessary for France’s long‑term fiscal health, arguing that the pension system is unsustainable and that public spending must be re‑balanced. Macron’s stance has drawn criticism from left‑wing politicians and the European Parliament, who fear that the reforms could erode France’s welfare state. [T2]

The bigger picture

Stakeholder perspectives reveal a complex web of interests. Teachers, many of whom are over 50 and approaching retirement, fear that the new pension rules will delay their financial security. They also worry that the budget cuts will compromise the quality of education, citing anecdotal evidence of overcrowded classrooms and reduced support staff. Parents, meanwhile, are divided: some sympathise with the teachers’ plight, while others express concern over the interruption of schooling and the potential impact on their children’s academic progress. Local officials in affected regions have called for a negotiated settlement, noting that prolonged closures could strain municipal budgets and disrupt community services. Political analysts suggest that the protests could influence the upcoming municipal elections, as voters grapple with the trade‑off between fiscal responsibility and social welfare. [T3]

From an economic perspective, the strike’s impact on productivity is significant. The Ministry of Education estimates that the closures could cost the French economy up to 500 million euros in lost productivity for the year, while teachers’ unions argue that maintaining high standards in education is a long‑term investment that outweighs short‑term costs. The broader European context also matters: neighbouring countries such as Germany and Spain have faced similar tensions over pension reforms, and the European Union has urged member states to balance fiscal discipline with social protections. The French case, therefore, serves as a litmus test for the resilience of the European social model in the face of economic pressures. [T2]

What to watch

The immediate future hinges on the outcome of negotiations between the government and the teachers’ unions. If the unions succeed in securing a pause on the pension reform, the government may still face pressure to address the education budget cuts. A compromise could involve a phased implementation of the pension changes, coupled with a targeted increase in education funding for the next two years. Alternatively, a hard‑line stance from the government could trigger further strikes, potentially extending into the summer term and affecting university admissions. The political ramifications are also significant: a failure to resolve the dispute could erode public confidence in President Macron’s administration, affecting his prospects in the next presidential election. Analysts predict that the protests could also influence the European Parliament’s stance on fiscal policy, as France’s case becomes a focal point for debates on the future of the welfare state in the EU. In the long term, the strike may catalyse a broader conversation about the sustainability of France’s pension system and the adequacy of public spending on education. The government’s willingness to engage in constructive dialogue will be crucial in determining whether the reforms can be implemented without eroding public trust. For teachers and students, the immediate priority remains the restoration of normal school operations and the protection of their rights and welfare. The unfolding events will likely shape France’s social contract for years to come, making Monday’s closures a pivotal moment in the nation’s ongoing struggle to balance fiscal prudence with social equity.
