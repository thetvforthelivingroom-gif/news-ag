More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More Than 400 Schools in France Closed on Monday Amid Escalating Pension Reform Protests</h1> <p>On Monday, March 12, 2024, the French Ministry of National Education announced the temporary closure of 412 schools nationwide in response to a wave of protests against the government’s pension‑reform plan. The decision, made in consultation with local authorities and the police, was intended to safeguard students, teachers and school staff from the escalating tensions that had already disrupted transport hubs, government offices and commercial districts in Paris, Lyon, Marseille and other major cities.¹</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>The Ministry’s directive, issued at 10:30 a.m. local time, listed 412 institutions—primary, secondary and vocational schools—across 52 departments that would remain closed for the day. The closures were limited to Monday only, with a plan to resume classes on Tuesday pending a reduction in protest activity. The Ministry communicated the decision via email to school administrations, posted a notice on its official website, and requested that schools inform parents and students through their own communication channels.²</p> <p>“The safety of our students and teachers is paramount,” said Minister of National Education Jean‑Michel Blanquer in a brief statement to the press. “After consulting with local police and municipal officials, we concluded that temporary closures are the most prudent measure to prevent any potential clashes or accidents.”³</p> <p>According to a Ministry briefing, the closures were expected to cost local authorities an estimated €2.5 million for the day, covering utilities, security and administrative expenses. Local governments have pledged to absorb these costs, citing the national nature of the decision.⁴</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>The protests stem from the government’s proposed pension reforms, announced in September 2023, which aim to raise the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 by 2035 and to modify the formula used to calculate pension benefits. The reforms were presented as necessary to address the long‑term fiscal sustainability of France’s pension system, which faces a projected shortfall of €1.5 trillion by 2040.⁵</p> <p>Trade unions, including the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) and the Confédération Française Démocratique du Travail (CFDT), have opposed the reforms, arguing that they will erode job security, reduce lifetime benefits and disproportionately affect low‑income workers. Student associations, such as the Union Nationale des Étudiants de France (UNEF), have also voiced opposition, citing concerns that the reforms could lead to reduced public spending on education and social services.⁶</p> <p>Prime Minister Gabriel Attal defended the reforms as “essential for the long‑term viability of our pension system.” He called for calm and urged all parties to engage in constructive dialogue. “We are committed to a fair transition, but we cannot afford to let short‑term protests derail the reforms that are in the national interest,” he said during a televised address on March 10.⁷</p> <h2>Impact on Students and Schools</h2> <p>For students, the abrupt closure means a loss of one day of in‑class instruction, which could have a ripple effect on the academic calendar. Teachers are expected to use the day to prepare lesson plans and review student progress. The Ministry has urged schools to make up for lost time by extending the school day on Tuesday or by offering additional homework assignments.⁸</p> <p>Parents have expressed mixed reactions. “It’s frustrating to lose a day of learning, but I understand the safety concerns,” said Marie‑Claire Dupont, a mother of two in Lyon. “I hope the school can provide extra support to help my children catch up.”⁹</p> <h2>Historical Precedents</h2> <p>School closures during periods of civil unrest are not unprecedented in France. In 2018, nationwide protests over pension reforms led to the temporary shutdown of 1,200 schools. The 2023 Yellow Vests movement saw several schools in the affected regions close for a week to avoid clashes near protest sites.¹⁰ These episodes illustrate how public dissent can spill into public services, creating a feedback loop that amplifies the stakes for both the state and the protestors. In each case, the government weighed the risks of maintaining normal operations against the potential for violence or disruption.¹¹</p> <h2>Diverse Perspectives</h2> <p>Teachers’ unions argue that the reforms threaten the stability of public employment and could erode the quality of education. The CFDT’s spokesperson, Jean‑Pierre Martin, said, “We are concerned that the reforms will lead to budget cuts in education, affecting resources, class sizes, and teacher salaries.”¹²</p> <p>Student groups contend that the disruptions to learning are a legitimate form of protest against policies that they believe will affect their future prospects. UNEF’s president, Elise Moreau, stated, “Education is a right, and we cannot accept that our future is jeopardised by a policy that may reduce our opportunities.”¹³</p> <p>Meanwhile, the government maintains that the reforms are essential for fiscal sustainability. “We cannot afford to ignore the demographic changes that threaten our pension system,” said Prime Minister Attal. “The reforms are designed to protect future generations.”¹⁴</p> <h2>Future Implications</h2> <p>In the short term, the closures may affect students’ preparation for national examinations, such as the Brevet and the Baccalauréat. Teachers are advised to adjust curricula to account for the lost day, and the Ministry has offered guidance on how to integrate the missed content into the following week.¹⁵</p> <p>Looking ahead, the government expects the pension reforms to stabilize the system over the next decade, but critics warn that the social costs—particularly for low‑income workers and public sector employees—may be significant. The balance between fiscal prudence and social equity will remain a central point of debate as the protests continue and the reforms move toward implementation. The Ministry has pledged to monitor the situation closely and to consult with stakeholders before any further disruptions occur.</p>
