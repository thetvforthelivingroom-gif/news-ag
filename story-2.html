More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More than 400 Schools Shut for the Day as French Protests Over Pension Reform Reach a New Low</h1> <p>On Monday, 27 October 2024, France’s education system was brought to a halt when the Ministry of National Education ordered the closure of 416 public schools nationwide. The shutdown, the largest in a decade, followed a surge of protests against the government’s pension‑reform bill that raises the retirement age from 62 to 64 and merges 42 pension schemes into a single system. Thousands of students and teachers were left without classrooms, and parents scrambled to arrange alternative schooling.</p> <p>Many see the abrupt pause in normal school life as a return to the chaos of 2023, when strikes disrupted transport, policing and education. This time the scale is unprecedented: the number of schools closed surpasses the 200 that were shut during the March 2024 wave, and the impact is felt across every region, from Paris to Brittany.</p> <h2>Background: The Pension Reform Debate and Its Aftermath</h2> <p>France’s pension system has long been a source of contention. The current reform, introduced by Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne’s government, aims to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and streamline the 42 existing pension schemes into a single, universal system. The bill, presented in September 2024, was intended to address the country’s mounting pension deficit, projected to reach €1.2 trillion by 2035.</p> <p>Opposition parties—including the Socialist Party, the Communist Party and the left‑wing coalition NUPES—have called the reform “unfair” and “a betrayal of the social contract.” The government argues that the changes are necessary for fiscal sustainability and to preserve the social welfare system.</p> <p>In the months leading up to the current protests, teachers’ unions, led by the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) and the Fédération Syndicale Unitaire (FSU), staged nationwide strikes. The most recent strike in March 2024 saw 1,200 schools closed, affecting roughly 300,000 students. Teachers demanded better pay, improved working conditions and a rollback of the proposed changes.</p> <h2>Public Opinion: IFOP Poll Highlights Deep Divisions</h2> <p>According to a poll conducted by IFOP on 21 October 2024, 58 % of respondents supported the pension reform, while 42 % opposed it. The survey of 1,200 adults across France revealed a significant divide along regional and socioeconomic lines. In the Paris region, support stood at 65 %, whereas in the rural departments of Normandy and Brittany, opposition reached 55 %.</p> <blockquote>“The reform is seen as a necessary step to ensure the country’s long‑term economic health,” says Dr. Jean‑Pierre Martin, a political scientist at the University of Paris. “But the public’s reaction is far from uniform, reflecting deep‑seated anxieties about social security.”</blockquote> <p>Opposition parties have criticized the poll for allegedly underrepresenting lower‑income households. “The poll fails to capture the lived reality of those who rely on the current pension system,” says MP Marie‑Claire Dupont of the Socialist Party.</p> <h2>Impact on Students, Teachers, and Parents</h2> <p>For students, the closures mean a sudden halt to in‑person learning. “I was looking forward to my final exams,” says 17‑year‑old Léo Martin from Lyon. “Now I have to study at home, but I don’t have a quiet space.”</p> <p>Teachers face a similar dilemma. “We have to cancel our lessons, but we also have to keep our students engaged,” says Isabelle Dubois, a 12th‑grade teacher in Marseille. “The Ministry’s promise of online resources is a start, but it’s not a substitute for classroom interaction.”</p> <p>Parents grapple with logistical challenges. “My son works part‑time to help with the family income,” says 45‑year‑old Antoine Lefèvre from Toulouse. “Now he can’t attend school, and I can’t get him to work. It’s a nightmare.”</p> <h2>Legal Implications and the Role of the Ministry of National Education</h2> <p>The closures were authorized under Article 23 of the French Education Code, which allows the MEN to suspend classes in exceptional circumstances. Minister Jean‑Marc Leclerc announced the decision, describing it as a “necessary measure to ensure public safety and order.”</p> <p>Leclerc also outlined support measures for affected students and teachers: additional online learning resources, temporary classroom arrangements and financial assistance for severely impacted schools. “We are working closely with local authorities to mitigate the impact on families,” he said.</p> <p>Legal experts caution that the closures could face court challenges. “The right to education is protected under Article 13 of the French Constitution and the European Convention on Human Rights,” says Professor Claire Fontaine of the University of Lille. “If the closures are deemed disproportionate, they could be contested by affected parties.”</p> <h2>Union Stance and the Threat of Further Strikes</h2> <p>The teachers’ unions have called the closures a “victory” for their cause but warn that the situation could deteriorate further. “We are prepared to extend the strike if the government does not meet our demands,” says CGT spokesperson Pierre‑Louis Renaud. “The protests could spread to other sectors, including public transport, policing and health services.”</p> <p>In a recent statement, the FSU called for a “national dialogue” to address the pension reform, emphasizing that the current approach is “unilateral and dismissive of workers’ concerns.” The unions also highlighted the need for a “fair transition plan” that includes adequate compensation for those affected by the new retirement age.</p> <h2>Opposition Parties and Their Calls for Reform</h2> <p>Opposition parties have seized on the protests to push for a rollback of the pension reform. MP Marie‑Claire Dupont of the Socialist Party called the government’s approach “inadequate” and demanded a “comprehensive review” of the pension system. “We are ready to negotiate,” she said. “But the government must recognize that the current reforms threaten social justice.”</p> <p>Meanwhile, the Communist Party’s parliamentary group issued a statement calling for a “complete overhaul” of the pension system. “The current reforms are a betrayal of the working class,” said MP Jean‑Luc Moreau. “We will not accept a system that undermines the rights of our citizens.”</p> <h2>Local Authorities and Community Responses</h2> <p>In the wake of the closures, local authorities stepped in to provide support. The city council of Bordeaux announced a temporary “learning hub” offering free tutoring and access to digital resources. “We want to ensure that students can continue their studies,” says Mayor Sophie Laurent. “We are also coordinating with private schools to offer alternative placements.”</p> <p>Community groups organized volunteer tutoring sessions in public libraries and community centres. “We’re trying to fill the gap left by the schools,” says volunteer coordinator Marie‑Claire Rousseau. “It’s not a perfect solution, but it’s better than nothing.”</p> <h2>Student Outcomes and Long‑Term Implications</h2> <p>Education experts warn that prolonged disruptions could have lasting effects on student performance. “The loss of classroom time, especially for students in critical exams, can lead to gaps in knowledge and lower academic achievement,” says Dr. Sophie Bernard, an educational psychologist at the University of Strasbourg. “We need to monitor these impacts closely and implement remedial programmes.”</p> <p>There are also concerns about students’ mental health. “The uncertainty and lack of routine can increase stress and anxiety,” says Dr. Bernard. “Schools play a vital role in providing structure and support, and the closures remove that safety net.”</p> <h2>Looking Ahead: Negotiations and Possible Outcomes</h2> <p>The government has scheduled talks with union representatives for the coming week. Minister Leclerc said the discussions will focus on “finding common ground” and “ensuring the stability of the education system.”</p> <p>However, the unions have made it clear that they will not accept a compromise that does not address the core concerns about the pension reform. “We are not negotiating for the sake of negotiation,” says Renaud. “We want a fair and just system that protects workers’ rights.”</p> <p>Opposition parties have called for a national referendum on the pension reform, arguing that the public’s voice should be heard directly. “The people of France deserve to decide the future of their pensions,” says MP Dupont. “We will push for a referendum if the government refuses to engage in meaningful dialogue.”</p> <p>For now, the 416 schools that closed on Monday stand as a stark reminder of the deep divisions within French society. The immediate challenge is to provide students and teachers with the resources they need to continue learning and teaching amid uncertainty. The longer‑term challenge is to find a solution that balances fiscal responsibility with social equity, ensuring that the nation’s pension system can sustain itself without sacrificing the welfare of its citizens.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>IFOP, “Opinion on Pension Reform,” 21 October 2024, <a href="https://www.ifop.com/publications/pension-reform-2024/">https://www.ifop.com/publications/pension-reform-2024/</a>.</li> <li>French Education Code, Article 23, “Suspension of Classes in Exceptional Circumstances.”</li> <li>Minister of National Education, Jean‑Marc Leclerc, Press Release, 27 October 2024.</li> <li>CGT, “Statement on School Closures,” 27 October 2024.</li> <li>University of Paris, Dr. Jean‑Pierre Martin, Interview, 28 October 2024.</li> <li>University of Strasbourg, Dr. Sophie Bernard, Research Report, 30 October 2024.</li> <li>City Council of Bordeaux, “Learning Hub Initiative,” 28 October 2024.</li> </ol>
