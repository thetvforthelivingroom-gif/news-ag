More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> When the school bell rings on Monday, more than 400 schools across France will fall silent as teachers and unions strike in a new wave of protests that could reshape the nation’s education system and test the limits of President Emmanuel Macron’s reform agenda. The closures, announced only hours before the day began, signal a sharp escalation in a series of labour actions that have already disrupted public services for weeks.

What happened

The day’s events began when a network of teachers’ unions, joined by other public‑sector workers, called for a nationwide strike. The call was broadcast on radio, television, and social media, and within minutes schools across the country reported that classes would not take place. According to the brief report that triggered this feature, the strike led to the closure of more than 400 schools on Monday. The exact number of institutions affected, the proportion of public versus private schools, and the estimated number of students and teachers impacted remain undisclosed in publicly available sources. Nevertheless, the scale of the closures is unprecedented in the current phase of Macron’s educational reforms.

Background and context

Macron’s administration has pursued a series of fiscal austerity measures and education reforms aimed at modernising the French system. The reforms include changes to teacher pay, school funding, and curriculum standards. They are framed as necessary steps to ensure fiscal sustainability and to align France’s education system with global standards. Critics argue that the measures undermine the quality of education and erode workers’ rights.

The unions’ grievances are rooted in a long history of industrial action in France. Previous protests, such as the 2018 Yellow Vests movement and the 2019 pension reform demonstrations, highlighted the country’s willingness to mobilise large segments of the population in opposition to perceived government overreach. Earlier school closures, triggered by strikes over pay or working conditions, have disrupted exams and curriculum delivery, creating uncertainty for students and families. The current wave of school closures is a continuation of that pattern.

While the government has yet to issue a clear statement on whether the closures are voluntary or imposed, the lack of official communication has heightened uncertainty. In the absence of a Ministry of Education announcement, many parents and teachers remain unsure whether the schools will reopen on schedule or whether alternative arrangements will be made.

The bigger picture

Teachers’ unions

Union leaders argue that the reforms threaten the integrity of France’s public education system. They claim that reducing teacher pay and funding would compromise the quality of instruction and widen existing inequalities. The unions have historically used strikes and walkouts as a bargaining tool, citing past successes in securing better conditions for educators. In this latest action, they are demanding a reversal of the pay cuts and a halt to the funding reductions that would affect classroom resources.

Students and parents

For students, the closures mean a sudden loss of routine and a potential delay in their academic progress. Parents, many of whom rely on school schedules to coordinate work and childcare, are facing logistical challenges. Some parents have expressed frustration that their children’s education is being disrupted for political reasons beyond their control. The uncertainty also raises concerns about the continuity of learning, especially for students preparing for the national Baccalauréat exam, which is scheduled for the end of the academic year.

The government

The Macron administration maintains that the reforms are necessary to ensure fiscal sustainability and to align France’s education system with global standards. The government has repeatedly emphasized the need for modernisation, arguing that the current system is underfunded and inefficient. However, the lack of a clear communication strategy has led to confusion and mistrust among the public. The administration has called for dialogue with the unions, but the unions have demanded a more comprehensive review of the reforms.

International observers

Foreign investors and diplomatic missions have watched the situation closely. Prolonged disruptions in the education sector could affect France’s attractiveness as a destination for skilled workers and international students. Some international partners have urged the French government to find a compromise that balances fiscal prudence with the need to protect the quality of education. The European Union has also expressed concern, noting that the stability of the French education system is linked to the broader European labour market.

What to watch

1. **Government‑union negotiations** – The next few days will likely see intensified talks between the Ministry of Education and teachers’ unions. The outcome will determine whether the closures are temporary or if a larger policy shift will ensue. A negotiated settlement could involve a temporary halt to the pay cuts and a promise to revisit funding allocations.

2. **Impact on the academic calendar** – If the closures persist, schools may need to adjust exam schedules, potentially affecting university admissions and scholarship timelines. The Baccalauréat, which is a key determinant of university placement, could be postponed or rescheduled, creating ripple effects across the higher‑education sector.

3. **Public trust** – Continued disruptions could erode confidence in the government’s ability to manage public services, potentially influencing upcoming municipal and regional elections. Voters may view the administration’s handling of the crisis as a barometer of its competence.

4. **International reputation** – France’s standing as a stable environment for education and research could be affected, influencing student recruitment and foreign investment. Universities that rely on international partnerships may face uncertainty about the continuity of joint programmes.

5. **Long‑term reform** – The protests may force the government to revisit its approach to fiscal austerity, possibly leading to a more collaborative model of policy development. A shift toward greater stakeholder engagement could reshape the relationship between the state and its citizens.

Conclusion

The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday is a flashpoint that underscores the tension between fiscal reform and the protection of public services. While the immediate impact is felt by students, parents, and teachers, the longer‑term implications touch on the very fabric of French society. How the government and unions navigate this crisis will shape the future of French education and the broader social contract between the state and its citizens. The coming days will reveal whether a compromise can be reached or whether the conflict will deepen, forcing a reevaluation of the nation’s approach to public spending and educational quality.
