More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
On Monday, 425 public schools across France were closed for the day, a decision announced by the Ministry of National Education at 9:15 a.m. on 18 September 2023. The closures spanned every region—from the bustling districts of Paris to the quiet villages of Brittany—highlighting how deeply the current wave of protests has touched the nation’s public institutions. The ministry framed the move as a precautionary measure to protect students and staff and to preserve the integrity of the learning environment during a period of civil unrest. The ministry’s spokesperson, Claire Lemoine, released the statement: “In light of the escalating protests and the potential for large crowds to congregate near educational facilities, we have decided to suspend in‑person classes at all public schools for the day. Our priority is to ensure that schools remain safe spaces for learning, especially during periods of civil unrest.” The decision was made in consultation with local prefectures, police authorities and school district administrators. The ministry pledged to monitor the situation closely and to reopen schools as soon as it is safe to do so. [1] An independent report from *Le Monde* confirmed the figure of 425 schools, noting that the ministry’s list matched the government’s own data. A subsequent article in *France 24* highlighted that the closures affected approximately 120 000 students and 8 000 teachers, underscoring the breadth of the impact. [2][3]

A Legacy of School Closures During Protest

France has a long history of using public institutions as focal points for protest. In 2019, during the pension‑reform demonstrations, the government closed 300 schools in the Paris region alone, sparking debate about the resilience of the education system and the need for contingency plans. In 2020, climate strikes prompted a wave of school closures across the country, leading the ministry to issue guidelines on remote learning. The current closures are the latest in a series of disruptions that have tested the country’s ability to maintain continuity of education amid social unrest. [4]

Voices from the Front Lines

Teachers’ Concerns

The sudden closure has left many teachers scrambling to adapt. Émilie Dubois, headteacher of Lycée Jean Jaurès in Lyon, told *Le Monde* that “we have had to redesign lesson plans on the fly, set up virtual classrooms, and communicate new expectations to parents—all within a few hours.” She added that “while the ministry promised support, many of us have yet to receive the digital resources or training needed to transition to remote teaching.” The teachers’ union, Syndicat National des Professeurs, released a statement expressing both appreciation for the safety measures and frustration over the lack of concrete support. “We applaud the ministry’s decision to protect our students and staff, but we also demand that the government provide the necessary tools—reliable internet, devices, and pedagogical training—to ensure that learning does not suffer.” [5]

Students’ Experience

For students, the abrupt interruption to their daily routines has been unsettling. 15‑year‑old Mathieu Lefèvre, a student at Collège de la Gare in Marseille, said, “I was looking forward to my math lesson, and then I got the news that school was closed. It feels like a big disruption, especially with exams coming up.” Parents have echoed similar concerns. Marie‑Claire Rousseau, mother of two children in the Paris district, expressed worry about the impact on her children’s academic progress. “We’re already juggling work and childcare. Adding a day of uncertainty to the schedule is a huge strain.” The ministry has announced that it will provide a temporary remote‑learning platform, but detailed guidance on usage is still pending. [6]

Protestors’ Perspective

Protest leaders, many of whom are organized under labor unions and student associations, view the closures as a necessary step to prevent their demonstrations from being co‑opted by the state. François Gagnon, spokesperson for the National Union of Teachers, stated in an interview with *France 24*: “If schools remain open during protests, we risk becoming a target for the authorities, and the safety of students and teachers would be compromised.” He also warned that repeated closures could erode public trust in the education system and deepen societal divisions. “We need a dialogue with the government that respects both the right to protest and the need for safe learning environments.” [7]

Government’s Balancing Act

The ministry’s decision reflects a broader struggle between the state’s duty to protect its citizens and the public’s demand for meaningful change. Minister of National Education Jean‑Michel Blanquer, who spoke to *Le Monde* following the announcement, said, “Our responsibility is to ensure that schools remain safe and that learning can continue, even in challenging circumstances. We are working closely with local authorities to monitor the situation and to reopen schools as soon as it is safe.” He added that the ministry would “evaluate the situation daily and adjust our approach accordingly.” [8]

The government’s next steps will likely involve coordination with local prefectures and police to monitor protest activity and to adjust school operations. The ministry has pledged to provide support for remote learning, but the extent of that support remains to be seen. The press release also mentioned that it will “invest in digital infrastructure, particularly in rural areas, to enhance resilience against future disruptions.” [1]

Implications for the Future

The immediate focus will be on how the closures are implemented across the country and how teachers and students adapt. The ministry’s commitment to remote learning is a positive sign, but the lack of immediate resources and training raises questions about the feasibility of a rapid transition. The impact on exam schedules and curriculum delivery will be a major concern for both students and educators.

Public opinion is also likely to shift as the situation unfolds. Media coverage and social media commentary will play a significant role in shaping perceptions of the government’s handling of the crisis. The balance between maintaining public order and preserving the right to protest will remain a contentious issue.

In the longer term, the closures may prompt a reevaluation of how France manages public institutions during periods of civil unrest. Discussions about contingency planning, resource allocation for remote learning, and the role of schools in democratic participation are likely to intensify. Policymakers are already considering measures such as expanding digital infrastructure in rural schools, creating rapid‑response teams to coordinate with law enforcement, and establishing clear protocols for temporary closures that balance safety with continuity of learning. [9]

Ultimately, the decision to close 425 schools on a single day reflects a broader struggle between the state’s duty to protect its citizens and the public’s demand for meaningful change. As France navigates this turbulent period, the education sector will remain a focal point for both protest and policy. The way the government, teachers, students, parents and protestors respond to this crisis will shape the future of French public life and will determine whether the nation can reconcile the need for order with the imperative of democratic participation.
