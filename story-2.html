More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> On Monday, more than 400 schools across France shut their doors, not because of a pandemic but because protestors turned classrooms into a front line of dissent. The abrupt closures, announced by local school boards after a wave of demonstrations, cut short lessons, disrupted exam preparations, and sent shockwaves through students, teachers, parents, and the nation’s political establishment.

What happened

In the early hours of Monday, dozens of local education authorities decided to close over 400 schools nationwide. The decision followed a series of protests in which demonstrators targeted educational institutions as symbolic sites of state authority. The closures were not driven by health concerns; France’s COVID‑19 restrictions had already been eased. Instead, they were a direct response to the escalating protests over President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform, which seeks to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and adjust pension calculations.

School boards coordinated the closures with union representatives. Teachers’ unions, especially the CGT (Confédération Générale du Travail) and FO (Force Ouvrière), voiced concerns about the safety and well‑being of students and staff. They urged authorities to consider alternatives such as remote learning or staggered schedules, but many boards opted for a complete shutdown to avoid potential clashes on school premises. Parents expressed worry about the sudden disruption to their children’s routine and the impact on exam preparation, particularly for students facing the Baccalauréat. The government has yet to issue an official statement, but officials emphasize that safety and public order remain paramount.

Background and context

The school closures are part of a broader protest wave that began in 2023, driven by opposition to Macron’s pension reform agenda. The reforms have sparked fierce resistance from workers, unions, and civil society groups. Protesters have staged mass demonstrations, strikes, and targeted actions against state institutions, including schools, police stations, and public transport hubs.

French schools have been closed for protest before. In 1968, during the May protests, students occupied universities and secondary schools to demand educational reforms and greater political participation. The 2018 pension reform strikes saw teachers and other public servants walk out, leading to temporary school closures and disruptions in public services. More recently, the 2023‑24 protest waves have targeted schools as symbolic sites of state authority, with protestors using social media to coordinate actions and amplify their message. This strategy aims to disrupt daily life and draw attention to perceived injustices of the proposed reforms.

The unions involved, particularly the CGT and FO, have long influenced France’s labor and social policy. They have historically used school closures as a tactic to pressure the government and rally public support. In the current wave, the unions have called for a nationwide strike and urged schools to close as a form of protest against the pension reforms. They argue that the reforms threaten the social safety net and the future of workers, and that school closures are a necessary step to draw attention to the issue.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Students

For many students, the closures have caused confusion and anxiety. Those preparing for the Baccalauréat—a crucial exam that determines future educational and career opportunities—found their study schedules disrupted. Some students feel that their hard work has been undermined by political disputes, while others view the closures as an opportunity to reflect on broader social issues.

Teachers and administrators

Teachers and school administrators face a difficult dilemma. They are responsible for providing education and ensuring a safe learning environment, yet they must navigate political pressures and union demands. Many teachers worry about the safety of students and staff amid ongoing protests. While some advocate for remote learning or staggered schedules, local school boards have largely chosen a complete shutdown to avoid potential conflicts on campus.

Parents

Parents are caught between concerns about their children’s education and the broader political context. Many have voiced frustration on social media, demanding a solution that balances public order with the right to education. Others support the protestors’ cause, arguing that the pension reforms threaten the social safety net and the future of workers.

Government

From the government’s perspective, the closures present a significant challenge to maintaining public order and ensuring continuity of education. Officials emphasize that safety remains paramount and that they are working to find a solution that balances the needs of students, teachers, and the public. The government has called for dialogue with unions and protestors to address all parties’ concerns.

Protestors

The closures also draw attention to the broader social issues at stake, including pension reforms and the role of unions in shaping social policy.

However, if it fails to resolve the issue, protestors may continue to target schools and other state institutions, further disrupting daily life.

If they maintain influence and shape the outcome of the pension reforms, they could continue to play a significant role in France’s social policy. Conversely, if they lose influence or are forced to compromise, they may need to rethink their tactics and find new ways to advocate for their cause.
