More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<article> On Monday, more than 400 schools across France closed their doors, turning classrooms into silent witnesses of a protest that has rattled the nation for months.

What happened

In the early hours of Monday, a coordinated wave of school closures swept the country. Estimates range from 350 to 450 institutions that were either shut by local authorities or voluntarily closed by teachers and students in solidarity with the broader protest movement. The closures were not isolated incidents; they occurred alongside demonstrations in major cities and rural towns alike, as activists sought to amplify their demands and disrupt everyday life. While the Ministry of Education did not issue a nationwide directive, several prefectures announced that schools would remain closed for the day, citing safety concerns amid escalating tensions. Teachers and students who participated in the demonstrations said the decision was largely driven by the collective will of the education community, rather than a top‑down order.

The protests that prompted the closures began in late 2023, when the French government introduced a controversial pension reform aimed at raising the retirement age and altering the calculation of benefits. The reform sparked widespread opposition, with workers, unions, and citizens rallying against what they view as an erosion of social protections. Over the past months, the movement has evolved from street marches to a more organized campaign that targets public institutions, including schools, as a means of drawing attention to the issue. The decision to shut down schools represents a significant escalation, marking the first time in recent history that such a large number of educational facilities have been closed in a coordinated manner in response to a political dispute.

Background and context

Targeting schools is not new to French protest history. In 2018, during the Yellow Vests movement, demonstrators closed schools as a form of civil disobedience, hoping to disrupt the status quo and force the government to address their grievances. Those closures had a profound impact on students, teachers, and families, and were widely reported in national media. The current wave echoes that strategy, suggesting that protest organizers see education as a powerful lever for political pressure. The scale of the current closures—over 400 schools—surpasses the numbers seen in previous movements, indicating a heightened level of coordination and resolve among participants.

The pension reform that sparked the protests was introduced by the government to address France’s aging population and fiscal deficits. Critics argue that raising the retirement age from 62 to 64 and altering the points system will disproportionately affect lower‑income workers and those in physically demanding jobs. Major unions, including the teachers’ union, have called for widespread strikes and demonstrations. The Ministry of Education has repeatedly emphasized its commitment to ensuring continuity of learning, but the unprecedented scale of the closures has forced a reevaluation of how to balance safety with educational obligations.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Government and Ministry of Education

The Ministry of Education has issued statements stressing that it is not endorsing the closures, but acknowledges the safety concerns raised by local authorities. Officials have expressed a willingness to work with prefectures to mitigate disruptions, but have also warned that unplanned closures could undermine the nation’s educational standards. The ministry’s stance reflects a tension between its duty to protect students and its responsibility to maintain uninterrupted schooling. While the ministry has not formally sanctioned the closures, it has indicated that it will monitor the situation closely and provide support where necessary.

Teachers and unions

Teachers’ unions have been vocal proponents of the closures, arguing that participation in the protests is a legitimate expression of collective dissent. Union representatives have highlighted the moral imperative of standing with workers nationwide, especially given the pension reform’s impact on public sector employees. Many teachers have joined demonstrations, either by refusing to attend classes or by actively organizing strikes. The unions view the closures as a strategic move to draw attention to the broader social issues at stake, and have called for a broader dialogue with the government to address the concerns of public sector workers.

Protest organizers

Organizers of the protest movement have framed the school closures as a symbolic act of resistance. They argue that shutting down educational institutions sends a clear message that the government cannot ignore the demands of ordinary citizens. Protest organizers have coordinated with teachers and students across the country, using social media and local networks to synchronize the closures. Their strategy mirrors earlier protest tactics, such as the Yellow Vests’ use of school closures, but on a larger scale. The organizers emphasize that the closures are temporary and that they will resume normal operations once the political situation stabilises.

Parents and students

Parents have expressed a mixture of concern and frustration. While many appreciate the solidarity shown by teachers and students, they worry about the impact on their children’s learning and the logistical challenges of arranging alternative childcare. Some parents have reported anxiety over the abrupt loss of a structured environment for their children, especially for those with special educational needs. Students have voiced mixed feelings; some feel empowered by participating in a national movement, while others are concerned about missing lessons and exams. The closures have sparked a broader conversation about the role of education during times of civil unrest.

Local businesses and communities

The closures have had ripple effects on local economies. Small businesses that rely on school traffic—such as cafés, bookstores, and transport services—have reported a noticeable drop in customers. In some areas, the absence of schoolchildren has led to quieter streets and reduced foot traffic, affecting revenue for nearby shops. Community leaders have called for a balanced approach that considers both the political significance of the protests and the economic well‑being of local businesses. Some municipalities have offered to provide temporary learning spaces or transportation to mitigate the impact on students.

Security and safety

Law enforcement agencies have increased police presence around schools to prevent potential clashes between protestors and security forces. Prefectures have coordinated with the Ministry of Interior to monitor the situation and deploy resources where necessary. The government has warned that any escalation could result in the use of force to protect public institutions. While the police have not reported widespread violence, there have been isolated incidents of vandalism and confrontations near school premises. The authorities emphasize that their priority is to maintain safety while respecting the right to protest.

What to watch (future implications)

Government response

The government’s next move will likely involve a combination of negotiation and enforcement. Officials may seek to address some of the protestors’ demands, particularly regarding pension reforms, to quell unrest. Alternatively, the administration may adopt a stricter stance, reinforcing the importance of maintaining educational continuity and potentially imposing penalties for schools that close without official authorization. The balance between concession and control will shape the political climate in the coming weeks.

Impact on education

The closures raise questions about long‑term educational outcomes. If schools remain closed for an extended period, students may experience learning gaps that could affect their academic progress. The Ministry of Education may need to implement remedial programs or alternative learning modalities to compensate for lost instructional time. Teachers may also face increased pressure to adapt curricula and assessment methods to accommodate disruptions.

Political legitimacy

The protest movement’s use of school closures underscores the depth of public dissatisfaction with the pension reform. Whether the government responds with concessions or a hard‑line approach will influence the movement’s credibility and the public’s perception of the state’s willingness to listen. The outcome could set a precedent for how future protests target public institutions and how authorities balance civil disobedience with the need to maintain essential services.

In the days ahead, observers will watch how the government, schools, and protest organisers navigate the delicate balance between civic expression and the continuity of education. The decisions made now will shape not only the immediate future of France’s schools but also the broader relationship between citizens, the state, and the institutions that serve them.
