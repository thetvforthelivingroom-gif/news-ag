More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
When more than 400 French schools shut their doors on Monday, the country was forced to confront a deeper crisis: a government pushing reforms that unions and citizens see as eroding their rights, turning classrooms into silent witnesses of a clash over the very fabric of the social contract.

What happened

On Monday, a nationwide strike organized by teachers and other public‑sector unions led to the closure of over 400 schools across France. The shutdown was a direct response to protests against the government’s pension and labor reforms. Thousands of students missed a day of instruction, extracurricular activities were suspended, and teachers faced uncertainty over their pay for the day. Reuters reports that the closures were coordinated with the CGT and FO unions, which have called for a nationwide strike in solidarity with other public‑sector workers [T2]. The decision to shut schools was not taken lightly; it followed weeks of escalating demonstrations and a growing sense that the reforms threatened the protections that have long underpinned France’s social model.

Background and context

The protests stem from a set of pension and labor law changes introduced by President Emmanuel Macron’s administration in 2023. The reforms raise the retirement age from 62 to 64, alter the pension points system, and impose limits on collective bargaining. Critics argue that these measures weaken workers’ rights and reduce the generosity of France’s social safety net. The Guardian details how the reforms were intended to make the pension system more sustainable but have been met with fierce opposition from unions that view them as an erosion of the social contract [T2]. Historical echoes of the current unrest run deep: the 1968 student protests, the 2018 pension reform strikes, and the Yellow Vest movement all highlighted the French public’s willingness to mobilize against perceived governmental overreach. Le Monde contextualises these events, noting that each wave of protest has reshaped France’s political landscape and pushed the government to negotiate or modify its agenda [T3].

Stakeholders

The closures bring a wide array of stakeholders into focus. Teachers, represented by unions such as the CGT, FO, and the National Federation of Teachers, argue that the reforms threaten their job security and working conditions. Students and parents are caught in the middle; while many support the unions’ fight for fair treatment, others are frustrated by the loss of learning time and the uncertainty surrounding future schooling schedules. Local businesses, especially those in school districts, feel the economic impact of reduced foot traffic and the closure of school‑run services. Reuters reports that some parents have voiced concerns about the lack of alternative arrangements for children, while teachers’ unions have demanded clearer compensation mechanisms for the days lost to strikes [T2]. Le Monde highlights how the protests have also drawn in student groups and parent associations, all of whom are demanding a more transparent dialogue with the government.

The bigger picture

The state‑union conflict over pension reforms has turned public institutions into battlegrounds. The government’s push for reform is framed as a necessary step to ensure long‑term fiscal stability, while unions insist that the changes disproportionately harm workers and erode the social contract that has defined French society for decades. The Guardian notes that the tension is not simply a policy dispute but a clash over the very nature of public service and its role in protecting citizens’ rights [T2]. Students and parents, meanwhile, are caught between a desire for a stable educational environment and a broader concern about the future of French social welfare. Le Monde points out that the protests have revealed a generational divide, with younger students more sympathetic to the unions’ cause than some older parents who fear the long‑term economic implications of the reforms [T3]. This dynamic illustrates how historical continuity and modern governance intersect, as France’s legacy of protest culture confronts contemporary political decision‑making.

What to watch

Continued closures could erode public confidence in the education system and force the government to reconsider its approach to pension and labor reforms. Reuters warns that the strikes may intensify if the government does not engage in meaningful negotiations, potentially leading to further disruptions in schooling and public services [T2]. The Guardian highlights the political stakes, noting that the upcoming regional elections could be influenced by the public’s perception of how the government handles the protests and the reforms. Le Monde emphasizes that any concessions made by the government will likely be scrutinised by both unions and the electorate, shaping the future trajectory of France’s social policy [T3]. Observers will also watch how the government balances the need for fiscal reform with the imperative to maintain public trust in institutions that have historically been seen as pillars of the French welfare state.

Economic ripple

The economic impact of school closures extends beyond the classroom. Local businesses that rely on school‑related foot traffic—cafés, bookstores, and transport services—report a noticeable dip in sales on strike days. Reuters documents that some small enterprises have had to adjust staffing and inventory to accommodate the sudden drop in customers, while others have seen a decline in revenue that could affect their long‑term viability. Le Monde adds that the closures also affect the broader economy by reducing the productivity of the next generation; missed instructional time can translate into lower academic performance and, ultimately, a less skilled workforce. The economic ripple is further compounded by the uncertainty that teachers face regarding their compensation for strike days, which may influence their willingness to participate in future protests. These factors collectively underscore how policy disputes in the public sector can have cascading effects on the private economy and the social fabric.

Conclusion

The shutdown of more than 400 schools on Monday is a stark illustration of how deeply intertwined France’s social policy, public trust, and everyday life have become. While the government argues that pension and labor reforms are essential for fiscal sustainability, unions and many citizens see them as a threat to the protections that have long defined the French social contract. As the protests continue, the nation watches not only the immediate disruptions to education but also the broader implications for public confidence, economic stability, and the future of France’s welfare state. The outcome of these negotiations will likely shape the political landscape for years to come, determining whether the country can reconcile its commitment to social solidarity with the demands of a changing economic reality.
