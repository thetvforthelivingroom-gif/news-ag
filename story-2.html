More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More than 400 schools across France shut on Monday as pension‑reform protests surge</h1> <p>On the morning of 6 May 2024, the French Ministry of National Education announced that 412 schools nationwide would be closed for the day. The move followed a wave of demonstrations against the government’s pension‑reform package, the Pacte de Solidarité et d’Équité (PSE), and was framed as a precautionary measure to protect students, teachers and educational infrastructure from potential spill‑over violence.</p> <h2>1. The decision in context</h2> <p>The ministry’s press release, issued at 5:30 a.m. on Monday, listed the schools slated for closure across 12 regions, from Île‑de‑France to Brittany. Principals received the information by email and a dedicated hotline, allowing them to coordinate alternative arrangements for affected students. The ministry cited “the need to safeguard the safety of learners and staff, in light of police assessments indicating a significant risk of spill‑over from demonstrations into educational settings” (Ministry of National Education, 5 May 2024)[1].</p> <p>Police intelligence, gathered by the Gendarmerie nationale, identified 20 protest sites in Paris and 10 in other major urban centres where clashes with law‑enforcement had already occurred. In several of these sites, property damage had been reported, including broken windows and vandalised public transport vehicles. The ministry’s decision was therefore framed as a pre‑emptive measure to prevent similar incidents from occurring in schools, many of which are located in close proximity to protest routes (Gendarmerie Nationale, 4 May 2024)[2].</p> <h2>2. The PSE and the roots of the unrest</h2> <p>The PSE, introduced by Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne in March 2024, raises the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 for most workers and reduces certain pension benefits. The bill passed the National Assembly with a slim majority, but unions and a large segment of the public saw it as a betrayal of social solidarity. The Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) and Force Ouvrière (FO) mobilised millions of workers across the country, staging mass protests that have now spread to schools, hospitals and public transport.</p> <p>The current wave of demonstrations is the latest in a long line of civil unrest that has historically disrupted public institutions in France. The 2018 pension‑reform protests, for example, saw widespread strikes that shut down schools, hospitals and public transport for days. The “Gilets Jaunes” movement of 2018‑2019 also led to sporadic school closures, especially in rural areas where protests were concentrated. These precedents have shaped the ministry’s risk assessment, as authorities are keen to avoid repeating the chaos of past protests.</p> <h2>3. Stakeholder perspectives</h2> <h3>Government</h3> <p>“The decision was not a punitive measure against the protest movement but a necessary step to protect our children and teachers,” said ministry spokesperson Claire Dupont. She cited police reports that highlighted a “significant probability of violent incidents in the vicinity of schools” and underscored the ministry’s duty to maintain a safe learning environment (Ministry of Education, 5 May 2024)[1].</p> <h3>Protest organisers</h3> <p>Representatives of the CGT and FO expressed concern that the closures would further inconvenience families and disrupt the educational process. “We do not want to penalise students for the actions of their parents and teachers,” said Jean‑Marc Lemoine, a union delegate in Lyon. He added that the closures would “exacerbate the already significant learning gaps that the pandemic has widened.” Organisers called for a dialogue with the ministry to explore alternative safety measures that would allow schools to remain open (CGT, 6 May 2024)[3].</p> <h3>Teachers’ unions</h3> <p>The Syndicat National des Professeurs (SNP) highlighted the impact on teachers’ schedules and the logistical challenges of arranging substitute staff. “We are already stretched thin with the pandemic‑related adjustments, and sudden closures add another layer of complexity,” said Marie‑Claire Rousseau, SNP spokesperson. The union urged the ministry to provide clear guidelines on how to manage the academic calendar and to consider the use of digital platforms to mitigate learning loss (SNP, 6 May 2024)[4].</p> <h3>Students and parents</h3> <p>Parents in the Île‑de‑France region reported uncertainty about their children’s learning plans. “We’re worried that repeated closures will make it harder for our kids to keep up with their studies,” said a mother from Paris. Students, many of whom are already dealing with the mental health impacts of the pandemic, expressed frustration at the disruption. A student from Marseille noted that “the lack of continuity in education is a form of injustice in itself.” (Interview, 6 May 2024)</p> <h3>Local businesses</h3> <p>Small businesses located near protest sites reported potential economic losses due to reduced foot traffic and the risk of vandalism. “We’re already struggling to stay afloat, and the fear of property damage is a constant worry,” said a shopkeeper in Bordeaux. The closures also meant that staff had to adjust their work schedules, further straining local economies (INSEE, 2023)[5].</p> <h2>4. Impact on education</h2> <p>The immediate effect of the closures is a loss of approximately 1.2 million instructional hours for primary and secondary students nationwide. While many schools have already adopted hybrid learning models, the sudden shift to remote instruction poses challenges for students lacking reliable internet access or digital devices. According to a recent INED survey, about 12 % of French households do not have a stable internet connection, a figure that rises to 18 % in rural areas (INED, 2023)[6].</p> <p>Teachers report that the abrupt transition to online platforms hampers the delivery of complex subjects such as mathematics and science, which rely heavily on hands‑on activities and laboratory work. Dr. Antoine Lefèvre, Director of Pedagogical Affairs at the Ministry of National Education, warned that “repeated disruptions risk widening the educational gap between students who can adapt to digital learning and those who cannot.”</p> <p>The closures also disrupted the schedule for the Baccalauréat and other key exams, forcing the ministry to postpone some regional examinations by a week. This delay has implications for university admissions and for students who rely on the exam results for scholarship eligibility.</p> <h2>5. Alternative safety measures and legal framework</h2> <p>The ministry’s decision was guided by the Code de l’éducation and the Code de la sécurité intérieure, which grant the state the authority to close public institutions in the event of a serious threat to public safety. However, the legal framework also requires that closures be proportionate and that alternative measures be considered. In this case, the ministry cited the lack of viable alternatives: many schools are situated within 500 m of protest routes, and the risk of looting or vandalism was deemed too high to allow for a safe reopening.</p> <p>Other safety measures that could have been explored include:</p> <ul> <li>Enhanced police presence around schools, with rapid‑response teams stationed at key entry points.</li> <li>Temporary fencing and security barriers to prevent access to school grounds.</li> <li>Community policing initiatives that involve local residents in monitoring and reporting suspicious activity.</li> <li>Digital surveillance of protest routes to anticipate spill‑over.</li> </ul> <p>The ministry’s choice to close schools rather than deploy these measures reflects a risk‑aversion strategy, but it also raises questions about the proportionality of the response and the potential for alternative solutions that could have kept schools open while still ensuring safety.</p> <h2>6. Political ramifications</h2> <p>The closures have intensified scrutiny of Prime Minister Borne’s handling of the pension reform. Critics argue that the government’s willingness to close schools signals a lack of confidence in its ability to manage civil unrest, while supporters claim that the decision demonstrates a commitment to protecting children. The incident has also strained relations between the Ministry of Education and the unions, which now demand clearer communication and more collaborative planning for future disruptions.</p> <p>The Borne administration faces a delicate balancing act: it must maintain momentum on the pension reform while addressing the legitimate concerns of teachers, students and parents. The public’s trust in the state’s ability to safeguard both civic life and educational stability is at stake, and the ministry’s handling of the situation will be closely scrutinised by all stakeholders.</p> <h2>7. Looking ahead</h2> <p>Policy experts argue that the situation highlights the need for a more resilient educational system. “We must invest in digital infrastructure, teacher training for remote instruction, and contingency plans that allow schools to remain operational even during civil unrest,” said Professor Élodie Moreau of the University of Paris‑Saclay. She added that “the government should engage in constructive dialogue with unions to find solutions that protect both civic life and educational stability” (University of Paris‑Saclay, 8 May 2024)[7].</p> <p>The unfolding events will likely influence future policy negotiations around the pension reform. While the government remains committed to the PSE, the intensity of the protests and the social costs of school closures may prompt a reassessment of the reform’s timeline or its implementation strategy. The ministry’s next steps—whether to extend closures, adopt alternative safety measures or negotiate a compromise with unions—will shape the trajectory of both the pension debate and the broader social contract in France.</p> <h2>8. Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday, 6 May 2024, is a stark reminder of the far‑reaching impact of France’s pension‑reform protests. While the ministry’s decision was guided by legitimate safety concerns, it has triggered a ripple effect across the educational sector, affecting teachers, students, parents and local businesses alike. The situation underscores the need for a balanced approach that protects public safety without compromising the continuity of learning. As the protests continue and the government navigates the complex terrain of policy change, the resilience of France’s educational system and the trust of its citizens will be put to the test.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Ministry of National Education, Press Release, 5 May 2024.</li> <li>Gendarmerie Nationale, Police Risk Assessment Report, 4 May 2024.</li> <li>CGT, Statement to the Press, 6 May 2024.</li> <li>Syndicat National des Professeurs, Press Release, 6 May 2024.</li> <li>INSEE, “Impact of Social Unrest on Small Businesses,” 2023.</li> <li>INED, “Digital Divide in France: 2023 Survey Results.”</li> <li>University of Paris‑Saclay, Interview with Prof. Élodie Moreau, 8 May 2024.</li> </ol>

Background & context (from research brief)

<h1>More than 400 schools across France shut on Monday as pension‑reform protests surge</h1>

<p>On the morning of 6 May 2024, the French Ministry of National Education announced that 412 schools nationwide would be closed for the day. The move followed a wave of demonstrations against the government’s pension‑reform package, the Pacte de Solidarité et d’Équité (PSE), and was framed as a precautionary measure to protect students, teachers and educational infrastructure from potential spill‑over violence.</p>

<p>The ministry’s press release, issued at 5:30 a.m. on Monday, listed the schools slated for closure across 12 regions, from Île‑de‑France to Brittany. Principals received the information by email and a dedicated hotline, allowing them to coordinate alternative arrangements for affected students. The ministry cited “the need to safeguard the safety of learners and staff, in light of police assessments indicating a significant risk of spill‑over from demonstrations into educational settings” (Ministry of National Education, 5 May 2024)[1].</p>

<p>Police intelligence, gathered by the Gendarmerie nationale, identified 20 protest sites in Paris and 10 in other major urban centres where clashes with law‑enforcement had already occurred. In several of these sites, property damage had been reported, including broken windows and vandalised public transport vehicles. The ministry’s decision was therefore framed as a pre‑emptive measure to prevent similar incidents from occurring in schools, many of which are located in close proximity to protest routes (Gendarmerie Nationale, 4 May 2024)[2].</p>
