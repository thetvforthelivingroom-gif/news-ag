More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More Than 400 Schools in France to Close on Monday as Protests Intensify</h1> <p>On Monday, more than 400 public schools across France will shut their doors in a coordinated strike organized by teachers’ unions. The closures come amid a nationwide wave of protests that began with the controversial pension reform and now test President Emmanuel Macron’s ability to balance reform and dissent. By shutting schools just before the Bastille Day holiday, the demonstrators aim to maximise visibility and disrupt the national narrative of unity.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>Although the Ministry of Education has not yet released an official list, local authorities report that the closures will affect schools in both urban and rural areas. In some municipalities, every public school will remain closed for the day. The move is expected to force many students to miss a day of instruction, potentially impacting exam schedules and curriculum pacing. Teachers’ unions view the strategy as a legitimate form of protest, while government officials warn that such disruptions undermine the state’s responsibility to provide education to all children.</p> <p>According to a report from <em>Le Monde</em>, more than 400 schools are slated to close, a figure that underscores the scale of the action. The closures are part of a broader campaign of civil disobedience that has been unfolding since March, when President Macron’s government introduced a pension reform that raised the retirement age and altered benefit calculations. Protesters—including teachers’ unions, students, and civil‑society groups—have used school closures as a tactic to pressure the government and draw attention to their grievances <sup>[2]</sup>.</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>The pension overhaul that sparked the current protests was part of Macron’s broader agenda to modernise France’s social security system. The reform raised the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 and introduced a points‑based system, aiming to address the country’s growing pension debt and ensure the sustainability of public finances. However, the changes were widely criticised for eroding public‑sector protections and disproportionately affecting workers in physically demanding jobs. Teachers, who have historically enjoyed generous pension benefits, were among the most vocal opponents, arguing that the reform would undermine the stability of their profession and the quality of education <sup>[2]</sup>.</p> <p>The protests have unfolded against a backdrop of economic uncertainty, with inflation rates hovering above 6 % and unemployment remaining stubbornly high. The cost‑of‑living crisis has amplified public frustration, as many households struggle to afford basic necessities while also dealing with rising energy prices and stagnant wages. The pension reform, therefore, has come to symbolize a broader struggle over the distribution of public resources and the future of the welfare state.</p> <p>Historically, school closures have been a strategic lever in French dissent. The 1968 student uprisings saw widespread shutdowns of schools and universities that played a pivotal role in the broader protest movement. More recently, the 2019 pension protests included school closures as a means to disrupt the status quo and draw attention to the plight of public‑sector workers. These precedents underscore the symbolic power of education institutions as sites of resistance and the willingness of protestors to disrupt them in pursuit of systemic change.</p> <h2>Reactions from Key Stakeholders</h2> <p>Teachers’ unions, such as the National Federation of Teachers (Fédération Nationale des Enseignants), have defended the closures as a legitimate form of collective action, arguing that the government’s reforms threaten the very fabric of public education. The unions have called on the government to negotiate a fair compromise that protects teachers’ pensions and ensures the sustainability of the education system <sup>[2]</sup>.</p> <p>Students and parents have expressed mixed feelings. While some students view the closures as a powerful statement against an unjust pension reform, others are concerned about the impact on their studies, especially those preparing for national exams. Parents, meanwhile, worry about the logistical challenges of arranging childcare and the potential financial burden of missed school days. Local businesses have also felt the ripple effects, as school closures can reduce foot traffic and disrupt supply chains for nearby vendors.</p> <p>Political parties have split on the issue. Left‑leaning parties, including the Socialist Party and the Communist Party, have condemned the closures as an unnecessary disruption of public services and called for a negotiated settlement. Right‑leaning parties, such as the National Rally, have expressed support for the protestors’ demands, framing the pension reform as a betrayal of the working class. The government, led by President Macron, has warned that continued disruptions could undermine public confidence in the state’s ability to deliver essential services and has urged protestors to return to the negotiating table.</p> <p>The use of school closures as a protest tactic also raises questions about the balance between civil disobedience and the state’s duty to ensure uninterrupted education. While the protestors argue that the closures are a necessary pressure point, critics contend that they undermine the rights of children and the broader public interest. The debate reflects a broader tension between the right to protest and the obligation to maintain essential public services.</p> <h2>Implications for the Future</h2> <p>The immediate future will hinge on the government’s response to the school closures and the broader protest movement. The Ministry of Education has signalled that it will engage in dialogue with teachers’ unions and other stakeholders to address the concerns raised by the pension reform. However, the government’s willingness to make substantive concessions remains uncertain, as the reform was a cornerstone of Macron’s policy agenda.</p> <p>The upcoming Bastille Day weekend, a period of national celebration and public gatherings, may serve as a focal point for further protest activity. The timing of the school closures just before the holiday underscores the protestors’ strategy to maximise visibility and disrupt the national narrative of unity and celebration. If the protests intensify, the government may face mounting pressure to negotiate a compromise or risk further civil unrest.</p> <p>In the longer term, the outcome of the pension reform debate will shape France’s social security system and its relationship with public‑sector workers. A successful negotiation could lead to a more balanced pension system that protects teachers and other public employees while ensuring fiscal sustainability. Conversely, a failure to reach an agreement could prolong the protest cycle, erode public trust in the government, and potentially destabilise the broader political landscape.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>As the country watches the unfolding events, the balance between protest and public service will remain a critical point of contention. The school closures, emblematic of the broader struggle over pension reform and public‑sector protections, will continue to test the resilience of France’s social contract and the capacity of its institutions to adapt to a rapidly changing economic and political environment. Whether the government will concede to the demands of teachers and other public workers, or whether the protests will force a re‑evaluation of the pension system, remains to be seen. In the meantime, more than 400 schools will remain closed on Monday, sending a stark reminder of the power of collective action in shaping the nation’s future.</p>
