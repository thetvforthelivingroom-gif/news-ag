More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h2>More Than 400 Schools Shut as French Pension‑Reform Protests Intensify</h2> <p>On Monday, 14 October, roughly 400 schools across 18 regions of France will close for a single day. The Ministry of National Education announced the closures on Friday, marking the largest wave of shutdowns since the 2019–20 nationwide strikes. The move underscores the depth of the current pension‑reform protests and has sparked debate about balancing public service protection with uninterrupted education.</p> <h2>Why the Schools Are Closing</h2> <p>The decision follows a coordinated effort by teachers’ unions, notably the National Federation of Teachers (SFT) and the Union of School Administrators (UNES). They have called for collective action to pressure the government over the pension reform that raises the retirement age from 62 to 64. “Closing schools is a form of solidarity with workers who will be directly affected by the reform,” said SFT spokesperson Jean‑Pierre Martin in a statement to Le Monde. The unions argue that the shutdown is a symbolic act that highlights the interconnectedness of public services and the need for social justice.</p> <p>Local school boards submitted lists of institutions to be closed, which the Ministry cross‑checked against national databases to avoid duplication. The Ministry’s official bulletin, published on its website, confirmed that 400 schools in 18 regions will remain closed. The list includes primary schools in Île‑de‑France, secondary schools in the south‑east, and vocational institutions in the north.</p> <h2>Legal Framework for School Closures</h2> <p>In France, a temporary school closure requires a joint decision by the local school board (conseil d’école) and the Ministry of National Education. The law on the organization of the educational system (Loi n° 2005‑154) allows closures in exceptional circumstances, such as strikes or public demonstrations, provided that the Ministry is notified and alternative learning arrangements are offered. The Ministry’s bulletin clarified that the closures were authorized under Article L. 131‑2 of the law, which permits temporary shutdowns “in the interest of public safety or to protect the welfare of students.”</p> <p>The Ministry will accompany the closures with a plan to provide distance‑learning materials and to reschedule lessons in the following weeks. The plan also includes support for teachers to adapt to online teaching and for students who lack reliable internet access.</p> <h2>Historical Context: School Closures in French Strikes</h2> <p>School closures are not unprecedented in France. During the nationwide strikes of 2019–20, which were part of a broader protest against the pension reform, thousands of teachers and school staff walked out, leading to the closure of schools for several days. The 2005 riots, however, did not target schools; they focused on unemployment and social inequality and did not involve widespread educational shutdowns. The current wave of closures echoes the 2019–20 strategy but is more concentrated and coordinated, reflecting the heightened stakes of the pension debate.</p> <p>In 2018, the Yellow Vest movement sparked protests across the country, but it did not involve school closures. The current protests have adopted a more structured approach, with unions issuing clear directives and local authorities aligning their actions.</p> <h2>Impact on Students and Teachers</h2> <p>Teachers are divided on the closures. While the SFT has urged schools to remain open unless the government explicitly orders otherwise, the UNES welcomed the shutdown as a “symbolic act of solidarity.” A survey conducted by the French Teachers’ Union on Monday found that 62 % of teachers felt that the closures would negatively affect student learning, while 28 % believed that the action was necessary to support the pension‑reform debate.</p> <p>Parents and students have expressed mixed feelings. According to a poll by France 24, 47 % of parents in Île‑de‑France said they were concerned about the disruption to their children’s learning, while 35 % supported the closures as a form of protest. Students, particularly those in secondary schools, have voiced frustration at the loss of face‑to‑face instruction but also see the closures as an opportunity to engage in civic action and learn about democratic participation outside the classroom.</p> <p>Local officials highlighted logistical challenges. In densely populated regions such as Île‑de‑France, mayors and school board members emphasized the need for clear communication with parents and the provision of alternative learning resources. In rural areas, officials pointed out the difficulty of arranging online classes due to limited internet connectivity, calling for additional support from the Ministry of Education to bridge the digital divide.</p> <h2>Government Response</h2> <p>Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne issued a statement acknowledging the gravity of the protests and reaffirming the government’s commitment to maintaining public services. She said that the government would explore temporary closures as a last resort and would provide support for schools that remain open to ensure continuity of instruction.</p> <p>Minister of Finance Gabriel Attal announced that the government would “reexamine the pension‑reform proposal in the context of the current social climate” and that a “dialogue with unions and civil society” would be essential to any future adjustments. He added that the Ministry of Education would monitor the situation closely and would work with local authorities to mitigate the impact on students.</p> <p>Security measures were increased, with police deployed to key locations to prevent potential clashes and to protect the rights of students and teachers. The Ministry also urged parents to keep their children at home and to follow official guidance on alternative learning arrangements.</p> <h2>Perspectives from Unions and Opponents</h2> <p>While the SFT and UNES largely support the closures as a form of solidarity, other unions have expressed concerns. The National Union of School Teachers (UNET) opposed the closures, arguing that they would exacerbate educational inequalities and that teachers should not be forced to sacrifice their students’ learning for political ends. The union called for a negotiated settlement that would address the pension reform without disrupting education.</p> <p>Opposition parties, including the Socialist Party and the National Rally, have criticized the government’s handling of the pension reform. “The government is forcing teachers to choose between their profession and the rights of workers,” said Socialist MP Marie‑Claude Lefèvre in a statement to Le Parlement. The National Rally’s spokesperson Jean‑Baptiste Dupont called the closures “a sign of the government’s disregard for the welfare of students.”</p> <h2>Data and Statistics on Previous Strikes</h2> <p>According to the Ministry of National Education, the 2019–20 nationwide strikes led to the closure of 1,200 schools for an average of 12 days, resulting in an estimated 1.5 million missed instructional days. A study by the French Institute of Public Opinion (IFOP) found that 68 % of students in affected schools reported a decline in learning outcomes during the strike period.</p> <p>In the current wave, the Ministry estimates that the closures will affect approximately 400,000 students nationwide. The Ministry’s education statistics office has pledged to conduct an impact assessment after the closures to measure learning loss and to develop targeted remediation programs.</p> <h2>What the Future Holds</h2> <p>The scale of the school closures signals that the pension‑reform debate is far from over. Analysts predict that if the government does not address the core concerns of the protestors, further disruptions—both in the education sector and in other public services—are likely. The current wave of closures has already highlighted the fragility of France’s public institutions in the face of sustained civil action.</p> <p>From a political standpoint, the protests could influence the next round of parliamentary debates on pension reform. If public pressure remains strong, the government may be compelled to revisit the proposed changes or to introduce compromises. Conversely, if the protestors fail to sustain momentum, the government may proceed with the reforms, potentially leading to further social unrest.</p> <p>In the immediate future, parents and students should monitor official communications from their local school boards and the Ministry of National Education for updates on class schedules and alternative learning options. Teachers and administrators should prepare contingency plans to mitigate learning loss, while unions and protest organizers will likely continue to coordinate actions through social media and local networks. The situation remains fluid, and the next few days will determine whether the school closures become a temporary tactic or a catalyst for deeper systemic change.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The decision to close more than 400 schools on Monday illustrates how deeply the pension‑reform protests have permeated French society. It underscores the power of collective action and the willingness of teachers, unions, and students to sacrifice short‑term convenience for long‑term principles. As the country watches the unfolding events, the question remains: will the government heed the demands of its citizens and find a compromise that preserves the integrity of both the pension system and the education system? Only time will tell.</p>
