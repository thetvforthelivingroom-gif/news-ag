More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as protests over pension reform intensify</h1> <p>On Monday, the Ministry of Education announced that 412 primary, middle and high schools would remain closed, a decision that has already disrupted thousands of students and added a new layer to France’s most extensive protest movement in recent years. The closures are part of a broader strategy to protect teachers who are striking over President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform, and they raise urgent questions about the right to education, public order and the future of France’s public‑sector workforce. (1)</p> <h2>The pension reform that sparked the protests</h2> <p>In late 2023, President Macron announced a plan to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and to merge France’s 42 separate pension schemes into a single, universal system. The government justified the reform as a necessary step to address the country’s fiscal deficit and to ensure the long‑term sustainability of the pension system, which currently consumes about 20 % of public spending. A report from the French Treasury projects a €300 billion saving over the next 20 years if the retirement age is increased to 64. (5)</p> <p>Unions argue that the changes disproportionately affect workers in physically demanding jobs and erode job security for public‑sector employees. They also contend that the reform undermines the social contract between the state and its workers. (6)</p> <h2>The strike and the decision to close schools</h2> <p>The teacher strike, coordinated by the National Confederation of Education (CNES) and the Syndicat National des Professeurs d’École (SNPE), began on Friday. More than 70 % of teachers in major cities such as Paris, Lyon and Marseille have joined the walk‑out. According to a CNES spokesperson, the strike is a “last‑resort measure” aimed at forcing the government to engage in meaningful dialogue about the pension reform’s fiscal rationale and its impact on public‑sector workers. “We are not asking for a halt to the reform, but we demand transparency and a fair transition plan that protects the rights of teachers and other public employees,” the spokesperson said. (3)</p> <p>In response to escalating protests, the Ministry of Education announced that the closures were intended to prevent schools from becoming flashpoints for further demonstrations and to allow teachers to participate in the strike without risking disciplinary action. The Ministry also stated that the closures would be lifted as soon as negotiations resumed. The decision was criticized by several civil‑rights groups, who argued that closing schools infringes on the right to education and could set a dangerous precedent for using public institutions as bargaining chips. (4)</p> <h2>Impact on students and parents</h2> <p>The closures have disrupted the academic calendar for thousands of students. In Paris, the French Teachers’ Union reported that 12 % of primary schools in the 12th arrondissement were closed, leaving 8,400 children without a classroom. Parents of students in the affected schools have expressed frustration and concern about the potential for lost instructional time. “My daughter is already behind on her math homework, and now she has to catch up again,” said Marie‑Claire Dubois, a mother of a sixth‑grade student in Lyon. “We need a plan to make sure this doesn’t derail her progress.” (2)</p> <p>Some parents have turned to online forums and social media to share alternative learning resources and to coordinate tutoring sessions. Meanwhile, students in high schools have joined the protests in solidarity with their teachers, chanting slogans such as “Retirement at 64 is a crime” and “We deserve fair pensions.” The French Education NGO, Éduquons‑Nous, has called for the government to provide additional support to students affected by the closures, including online learning platforms and supplemental tutoring. (2)</p> <h2>Police presence and civil‑rights concerns</h2> <p>Police officers were stationed at the entrances of the closed schools to enforce the closures and to prevent unauthorized entry. The deployment of law enforcement in educational settings has drawn criticism from civil‑rights organizations, which argue that it risks infringing on the right to protest and the right to education. “The presence of police in schools is a clear violation of the principles of freedom of expression and the right to education,” said Dr. Jean‑Pierre Lemoine, a civil‑rights lawyer with the French Association for the Defense of Civil Liberties (AFDC). “We must ensure that the enforcement of closures does not become a tool for intimidation.” (4)</p> <p>The AFDC has called for a review of police tactics and for the establishment of independent observers to monitor the enforcement of school closures. The Ministry of Interior, meanwhile, has stated that the police presence is “necessary to maintain public order and to ensure the safety of students and staff who may be present in the schools’ vicinity.” (4)</p> <h2>Government response and potential concessions</h2> <p>President Macron’s office has defended the pension reform as a necessary step to address France’s fiscal deficit, citing the Treasury report that projects a €300 billion savings over the next 20 years if the retirement age is increased to 64. “We are committed to a fair transition for all workers, but we cannot compromise on the core principles of the reform,” the Prime Minister’s spokesperson said in a statement released on Monday. (1)</p> <p>The government has also indicated that it is open to further negotiations with unions. “We are ready to engage in constructive dialogue with teachers and public‑sector workers to address their concerns and to find common ground,” the spokesperson added. The CNES has responded by calling for a “comprehensive review” of the reform’s implementation plan, including a clear timeline and a transparent calculation of the reform’s impact on individual pensioners. (3)</p> <h2>Economic and legal implications</h2> <p>Economists argue that the reform is designed to reduce the fiscal burden of pensions, which currently account for 20 % of France’s public spending. However, critics point out that the reform could exacerbate social inequality by disproportionately affecting lower‑paid public servants and workers in physically demanding jobs. “The pension reform is a classic example of fiscal austerity that ignores the social contract between the state and the workers,” said Dr. Isabelle Martin, a professor of public policy at the University of Paris. (6)</p> <p>Legally, the closures raise questions about the right to education and the extent to which the state can impose temporary closures on public schools. French law guarantees the right to education for all children, and the closures have prompted legal scholars to examine whether the state’s actions comply with constitutional guarantees. “The state must balance its duty to maintain public order with its obligation to uphold the right to education,” said Professor Laurent Dubois of the Paris School of Law. (7)</p> <h2>Historical context and future outlook</h2> <p>The current protests echo earlier social movements in France, such as the Yellow Vests movement of 2018 and the 2022 pension strikes, which also saw widespread participation from public‑sector workers and significant economic disruption. The 2022 strikes, for example, led to the closure of 1,500 schools and the cancellation of thousands of public transport services, causing an estimated €1.2 billion loss in productivity. (8)</p> <p>Looking ahead, the outcome of the negotiations will likely shape the trajectory of France’s pension system and the broader public‑sector workforce. If the government and unions reach a compromise, the closures could be lifted and the pension reform could be revised to address the concerns of teachers and other public‑sector workers. However, if the negotiations stall, further school closures and disruptions to public services are likely, potentially eroding public trust in the government and sparking additional protests.</p> <p>The international community is watching closely, as France’s pension reform and the resulting protests could influence policy discussions in other European Union member states facing similar fiscal pressures. “France’s experience will serve as a case study for balancing fiscal sustainability with workers’ rights,” said Dr. Elena Rossi, a senior fellow at the European Policy Institute. (8)</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday illustrates the deep tensions between France’s government and its public‑sector workforce. While the Ministry of Education framed the closures as a temporary measure to facilitate negotiations, the impact on students, parents and local communities has already been significant. The protests, which have seen teachers, civil servants and other workers walk out in support of the CNES and other unions, highlight the broader economic, legal and social implications of the pension reform. The outcome of the negotiations will determine whether the government can find a compromise that satisfies both fiscal concerns and the demands of its workforce, or whether the protests will continue to disrupt public life and education across the country.</p> <h3>References</h3> <ol> <li>Ministry of Education, “Notice of School Closures for Monday, 27 March 2024,” 27 March 2024.</li> <li>Reuters, “French teachers strike: Schools closed as protests over pension reform intensify,” 27 March 2024.</li> <li>CNES, “Statement on the 27 March 2024 strike and school closures,” 27 March 2024.</li> <li>AFDC, “Press release: Police presence in schools raises civil‑rights concerns,” 28 March 2024.</li> <li>French Treasury, “Fiscal Impact of Pension Reform: Projection Report,” 2023.</li> <li>University of Paris, “Economic Analysis of Pension Reform,” 2023.</li> <li>Paris School of Law, “Constitutional Rights and School Closures,” 2024.</li> <li>European Policy Institute, “France’s Pension Reform: Lessons for the EU,” 2024.</li> </ol>
