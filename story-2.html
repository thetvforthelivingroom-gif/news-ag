More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut their doors, underscoring how the country’s protest movement has reached the classroom. The decision follows a wave of demonstrations that have disrupted daily life for weeks, forcing the government to balance public safety with citizens’ rights. It marks the latest flashpoint in a long‑standing struggle over economic policy and social equity.

What happened

The French Ministry of National Education announced that, in response to escalating protests in several major cities, a nationwide closure of more than 400 primary, secondary, and vocational schools will take effect on Monday. The move is intended to protect students and staff from potential clashes and to allow law‑enforcement agencies to secure key transport hubs and public spaces that have become focal points for demonstrators. The closures are expected to last for at least one week, though officials have left the exact duration open to future adjustments as the protest situation evolves [T2].

Police deployment has intensified across the country, with thousands of officers posted at schools, metro stations, and other public venues. The security presence is part of a broader strategy to prevent incidents that could arise from the intersection of large crowds and confined school premises. While the closures aim to reduce the risk of violence, the decision has also been interpreted by some as a concession to protestors, who have demanded greater transparency and reform in the pension system and tax policy.

Background and context

The protests that prompted the school closures stem from a series of economic reforms announced by President Emmanuel Macron’s administration. Central to the unrest are changes to the pension system that raise the retirement age and modify the calculation of benefits, measures that critics argue disproportionately affect lower‑income workers and the elderly. Additionally, tax reforms perceived to favor the affluent have fueled public anger, leading to widespread demonstrations across the country.

France’s history of civil unrest provides a backdrop for the current crisis. In 2018, the Yellow Vests movement erupted over fuel taxes and quickly expanded into a national protest against perceived economic inequality. The movement’s impact was felt across all sectors, including education, where schools faced disruptions and security challenges. In 2022, another wave of pension protests saw thousands rallying in the streets, demanding a more equitable retirement system. These episodes illustrate the cyclical nature of French civil disobedience, often triggered by policy changes that are seen as undermining social solidarity [T2].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Students and parents

For students, the closures mean a sudden halt to regular classes and a disruption of learning trajectories. Parents have expressed concerns about the lack of alternative educational arrangements, especially for children in remote areas or those with special needs. Some parents fear that prolonged disruptions could widen existing educational inequalities, as schools in wealthier districts may have more resources to adapt to remote learning than those in less affluent regions [T3].

Teachers and school staff

Educators are grappling with logistical challenges. Teachers must coordinate with school administrators to determine how to deliver instruction remotely or through staggered schedules. Several teachers’ unions have called for clearer guidelines and support from the government to ensure that the quality of education is maintained during the closures. Concerns about workload and professional development have also surfaced, as teachers navigate the dual demands of teaching and safety compliance [T3].

Protest organisers

Organisers of the demonstrations view the school closures as a strategic setback. By shutting down educational institutions, the government removes a potential venue for protestors to rally, thereby limiting the reach of the movement. Some organisers argue that the closures undermine the principle of free expression, while others see them as a necessary measure to protect students from potential harm. The tension between maintaining public order and safeguarding civil liberties remains a central point of contention [T2].

Government officials

Government officials emphasise that the primary objective of the closures is the safety of students and staff. In statements, ministers have highlighted the need to prevent clashes that could arise from the proximity of large protest crowds to schools. Officials also point to the broader context of national security, noting that the closures are part of a coordinated effort to maintain public order during a period of heightened unrest. However, critics argue that the decision may embolden protestors by signalling a willingness to make concessions, potentially prolonging the crisis [T2].

Local businesses

The closures have ripple effects on local economies. Small businesses near schools—cafés, bookstores, and transport services—report a decline in foot traffic and sales. Some business owners fear that extended closures could lead to longer‑term economic downturns in neighbourhoods that rely on the steady patronage of students and staff. Conversely, businesses that provide services for remote learning, such as technology suppliers, have seen increased demand as schools pivot to online platforms [T3].

The French government has not ruled out extending the school closures if protests continue or intensify. An extended shutdown could exacerbate educational disparities and strain the capacity of remote learning infrastructures. Monitoring official announcements will be crucial to assess whether the closures become a long‑term strategy or a temporary measure [T2].

Negotiations and policy adjustments

The current crisis may prompt renewed negotiations between the government and opposition parties, potentially leading to amendments in pension and tax reforms. If concessions are made, they could signal a shift in the political landscape and influence the trajectory of future protests. The outcome of these negotiations will likely shape public sentiment and the likelihood of further civil disobedience [T2].

Spill‑over onto other public institutions

There is a risk that protests could spill over onto other public institutions, such as universities, libraries, and municipal offices. The government’s preparedness to secure these venues will be a key factor in preventing further disruptions. Long‑term educational impact

The long‑term impact on France’s education system remains uncertain. Prolonged closures could affect student performance, graduation rates, and workforce readiness. Policymakers will need to address potential learning losses and ensure that remedial programs are in place to mitigate the effects of the disruption. The effectiveness of remote learning initiatives will be a critical metric in evaluating the resilience of the education sector [T3].
