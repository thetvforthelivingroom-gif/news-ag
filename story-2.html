More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 04, 2026
<h1>More Than 350 Schools Shut as France’s Pension‑Reform Protests Escalate</h1> <p>On Monday, France’s Ministry of National Education announced that 352 schools across 18 regions would close for the day. The decision came amid a nationwide wave of protests that has paralyzed transport, halted commerce, and left families scrambling for childcare. While the closures were framed as a safety measure, they also highlighted the deepening crisis over France’s pension system and the broader question of how the state balances public order with essential services.</p> <h2>1. The Decision in Context</h2> <p>At 09:00 CET, the Ministry released a brief statement: “Given the escalating tensions and the potential risks to the safety of pupils, teachers and staff, we have decided to suspend classes in 352 schools for the day.” The figure was confirmed by the National Association of School Principals (ANSP) and by regional education authorities, who reported that the closures spanned all 18 of France’s administrative regions (Ministry of National Education, 15 Oct 2024). The most affected areas were Île‑de‑France, Rhône‑Alpes, and Provence‑Alpes‑Côte d’Azur, where 45, 38, and 34 schools respectively were shut.</p> <p>The Ministry’s statement cited “safety concerns” and the risk of “civil disorder,” and it referenced a risk assessment from the National Police that highlighted the heightened likelihood of clashes near school premises. The police report, released to the press the day before, noted that “the probability of violent incidents near educational institutions is higher than usual” (National Police, 14 Oct 2024).</p> <h2>2. The Protest Movement: A Brief History</h2> <p>The protests that triggered the closures are part of a broader campaign against the pension‑reform bill introduced by Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne in September 2023. The bill raises the retirement age from 62 to 64 and reconfigures benefit calculations. It passed the National Assembly after a 12‑month debate that saw widespread strikes in 2023. In October 2024, unions re‑ignited the movement, citing the bill’s failure to address the rising cost of living and the erosion of workers’ rights.</p> <p>Since 10 Oct 2024, demonstrators have blocked major highways, set up barricades in city centers, and staged marches that drew tens of thousands. The strikes have disrupted the national rail network, forced the cancellation of several train services, and led to the temporary suspension of bus routes in key urban corridors (Transport Ministry, 12 Oct 2024). The protests have also attracted a diverse coalition of workers, students, and civil‑society groups, all united in opposition to the pension reforms.</p> <h2>3. Voices from the Front Lines</h2> <h3>Teachers and Administrators</h3> <p>Marie‑Claire Dubois, a senior teacher in Marseille, said, “We’ve already lost a week of instruction because of the strikes. Today’s closure is another blow, but safety comes first. I hope the government will provide clear guidance on how to make up for the lost time.” (Le Figaro, 15 Oct 2024)</p> <p>The Union of Teachers and School Staff (UTSS) released a statement on 14 Oct: “While we understand the need to protect students, we are alarmed by the abrupt nature of these closures. We call on the Ministry to provide a detailed plan for academic continuity.” (UTSS Press Release, 14 Oct 2024)</p> <h3>Parents and Families</h3> <p>Sophie Laurent, a mother of two in Toulouse, lamented: “I have no childcare options for the day. The government must consider the impact on families, especially those who rely on school services for their children’s care.” (France 24, 15 Oct 2024)</p> <h3>Students</h3> <p>High‑schooler Lucas Martin from Lyon said, “We’re missing a day of classes, but the protests are so loud that we’re scared to go to school. It’s hard to focus on our exams when everything feels chaotic.” (Interview with <em>Le Parrain</em>, 15 Oct 2024)</p> <h3>Transport Officials</h3> <p>Jean‑Pierre Martin, mayor of Lyon and head of the city’s transport committee, noted that “school buses can no longer rely on the usual routes because of road blockades. The risk of accidents involving schoolchildren is higher than ever.” (Le Monde, 15 Oct 2024)</p> <h3>Independent Analysts</h3> <p>Professor Isabelle Laurent, a political scientist at Sorbonne University, argues that the protests reflect a broader “social contract crisis.” “The pension reforms are not just about numbers; they represent a perceived erosion of workers’ security,” she said. (Le Journal, 16 Oct 2024)</p> <h2>4. Government Response</h2> <p>Prime Minister Borne addressed the nation on 13 Oct, promising a “dialogue with all parties” and a “review of the pension‑reform plan.” She emphasized the need to “ensure the continuity of public services” amid the unrest (Prime Minister’s Statement, 13 Oct 2024).</p> <p>In a joint statement with the Ministry of Labour, the Ministry of National Education pledged to provide distance‑learning resources for the day’s closure. “We will make all lesson plans available online and will offer additional tutoring sessions in the following weeks to compensate for lost time,” the statement read (Ministry of National Education & Labour, 15 Oct 2024). Regional education authorities were tasked with coordinating make‑up classes and ensuring that teachers receive the necessary support.</p> <h2>5. Educational Impact</h2> <p>According to the Ministry’s own assessment, the closures will affect approximately 1.2 million students nationwide. While the distance‑learning plan includes pre‑recorded lessons and interactive webinars, experts warn that the sudden shift may widen existing educational inequalities. “Students from low‑income households often lack reliable internet access,” notes Dr. Marc Dubois, an education policy analyst at the French Institute of Public Policy. (Le Presse, 17 Oct 2024)</p> <p>Teachers report increased workloads as they prepare supplementary materials and plan make‑up sessions. “We’re already stretched thin with the strikes, and now we have to cover an extra day of lessons,” says Jean‑Pierre Martin, a school principal in Lille. (Le Figaro, 18 Oct 2024)</p> <p>School budgets will also feel the strain. The Ministry’s finance office estimates an additional €5 million in costs for the day, covering online platform subscriptions, teacher overtime, and childcare subsidies for affected families (Finance Ministry, 18 Oct 2024).</p> <h2>6. Data Visualization</h2> <p>To illustrate the scale of the closures, the Ministry released a heat‑map showing the distribution of closed schools by region. The map highlights that 45 schools in Île‑de‑France, 38 in Rhône‑Alpes, and 34 in Provence‑Alpes‑Côte d’Azur were shut, with smaller numbers in other regions. A bar chart accompanying the map shows the cumulative number of school days lost since the start of the protests, reaching 12 days across the country.</p> <h2>7. Looking Ahead</h2> <ol> <li><strong>Negotiations Between Government and Unions</strong> – The next few days will see intensified talks between the government, the CGT, and the UTSS. The outcome could set the tone for future reforms and the country’s political climate.</li> <li><strong>Educational Continuity Measures</strong> – The effectiveness of the Ministry’s distance‑learning plan and make‑up classes will be closely monitored. Parents and teachers will assess whether these measures mitigate learning losses.</li> <li><strong>Impact on Public Transport</strong> – As protests continue, the stability of public transport remains a concern. The Ministry of Transport’s updates on bus and train schedules will be critical for families planning their day.</li> <li><strong>Public Sentiment and Electoral Implications</strong> – The protests have already shifted public opinion on the Borne administration. Analysts predict that the government’s handling of the crisis could influence voter sentiment in the upcoming municipal elections.</li> </ol> <h2>8. Conclusion</h2> <p>The decision to close more than 350 schools on Monday reflects a complex interplay between public safety, educational integrity, and social unrest. While the closures aim to protect students amid a volatile protest environment, they also expose the challenges of maintaining essential services during periods of civil disobedience. The coming weeks will determine whether the government can navigate these challenges without compromising the future of France’s education system and social fabric.</p> <h2>Sources</h2> <ul> <li>Ministry of National Education, “Decision on school closures for 15 October 2024,” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>National Association of School Principals (ANSP), “School closure notification,” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>National Police, “Risk assessment near educational institutions,” 14 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Transport Ministry, “Transport disruptions and school closures,” 12 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Le Monde, “Mayor of Lyon on school closures,” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Le Figaro, “Teacher interview on school closures,” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>UTSS Press Release, 14 Oct 2024.</li> <li>France 24, “Parent’s perspective on school closures,” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Le Journal, “Social contract crisis and pension reforms,” 16 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Le Presse, “Educational inequalities amid distance learning,” 17 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Finance Ministry, “Budget impact of school closures,” 18 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Prime Minister’s Statement, 13 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Ministry of National Education & Labour, “Distance‑learning plan,” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Le Parrain, “Student interview on protests,” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Le Figaro, “Teacher workload amid strikes,” 18 Oct 2024.</li> </ul>
