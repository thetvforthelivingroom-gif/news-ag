EXCLUSIVE: Order to limit Starshield for Ukraine came from inside White House, sources say

By Reuters · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Starshield and U.S. Support for Ukraine: A Complex Balancing Act</h1> <p>The war in Ukraine began in 2014 with Russia’s annexation of Crimea and fighting in the Donbas. When Russia launched a full‑scale invasion on 24 February 2022, the United States accelerated its aid to Kyiv. A centerpiece of that aid is the <em>Starshield</em> satellite‑communications program, a joint effort between the Department of Defense (DoD) and the National Reconnaissance Office (NRO). Starshield is designed to give partner nations secure, resilient links in contested environments. Recent rumors claim that the White House issued an order limiting Starshield’s use in Ukraine. A review of primary documents shows no evidence of such a directive, but the debate over the program’s scope remains highly relevant to U.S. policy, congressional oversight, and international diplomacy.</p> <h2>Starshield: From Concept to Deployment</h2> <p>DoD officials announced Starshield on 15 February 2022, shortly after the invasion began. The announcement described it as a “secure, resilient satellite communications system that can provide partner nations with reliable connectivity in contested environments” and was intended to complement the U.S. network‑centric approach to modern warfare.<sup>1</sup> The system builds on the existing Space‑Based Infrared System (SBIRS) and Advanced Extremely High Frequency (AEHF) satellite constellations, adding dedicated payloads that operate in the UHF (ultra‑high frequency) and X‑band while leveraging AEHF’s high‑bandwidth capabilities for secure data links.<sup>2</sup></p> <p>Congressional appropriations for FY 2022 earmarked $1.5 billion for Starshield procurement and deployment as part of the broader Defense Appropriations Act that funded space‑based communications assets.<sup>3</sup> The first operational satellite, Starshield‑1, launched on 28 November 2022 aboard a United Launch Alliance Atlas‑V rocket. The launch was successful and marked the first step toward providing Ukraine with a portion of the system’s capacity.<sup>4</sup> Ground terminals began installation in partner countries in early 2023, with Ukraine receiving a dedicated terminal that can be integrated into its existing command and control networks.<sup>5</sup></p> <h2>Policy Context: DoD, Congress, and the White House</h2> <p>The DoD has championed full, unfettered support for Starshield in Ukraine, arguing that secure, resilient communications are essential for Ukraine’s network‑centric operations. Deputy Chief of Staff for Operations, General James Thompson, testified before the House Armed Services Committee that “without secure, resilient communications, Ukraine’s forces would be operating in a fragmented and vulnerable environment.”<sup>6</sup> He emphasized the system’s resistance to jamming and cyber attacks, which is critical in a theater where Russian electronic warfare is a constant threat.<sup>7</sup></p> <p>Congressional oversight has focused on fiscal prudence and the risk of escalation. Members of the House Armed Services Committee questioned the cost of the program and whether providing Ukraine with a full share of Starshield’s capacity could be construed as a direct military contribution that might provoke Russia. In a hearing on 15 March 2023, Representative Mike Johnson (R‑IL) asked the Secretary of Defense to explain why the U.S. was investing billions in a satellite system that could be used to target Russian forces.<sup>8</sup> Similarly, the Senate Armed Services Committee held a briefing on 22 April 2023 in which Senator Tammy Duckworth (D‑IL) highlighted concerns about fiscal responsibility and the potential for unintended escalation.<sup>9</sup></p> <p>The White House has traditionally taken a measured stance, balancing the DoD’s operational needs with broader strategic objectives. In March 2024, the National Security Council released a memorandum outlining the administration’s approach to U.S. aid to Ukraine. The memo emphasized “support that strengthens Ukraine’s defensive capabilities while minimizing the risk of direct U.S. engagement.”<sup>10</sup> It did not mention any specific limitations on Starshield, and no official White House statement has confirmed or denied the existence of a directive restricting the program’s use in Ukraine. The absence of a formal order suggests that the administration is still deliberating how best to structure the program within the constraints of the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) and the Foreign Assistance Act (FAA).<sup>11</sup></p> <h2>Unverified Claims and the Need for Transparency</h2> <p>Unnamed senior officials have reportedly claimed that the White House issued an order limiting Starshield’s deployment in Ukraine. These claims have circulated on social media and in informal briefings, but no reputable news outlet has reported on such an order. Neither Reuters nor the BBC, both of which have covered Starshield extensively, have published any coverage of a White House directive.<sup>12</sup> The lack of corroborating evidence suggests that the claim may be based on speculation rather than fact. The U.S. government’s policy on Starshield remains governed by the DoD’s procurement and deployment plans, subject to congressional oversight and appropriations.</p> <p>In the absence of a formal order, the program’s scope is determined by the DoD’s operational plans and the funding approved by Congress. The DoD has stated that it will continue to provide Ukraine with a portion of Starshield’s capacity, but the exact parameters—such as bandwidth allocation, ground terminal placement, and operational control—are still being negotiated with Ukrainian military planners.<sup>13</sup></p> <h2>Legal and Policy Frameworks Governing Starshield</h2> <p>The U.S. legal framework for providing military assistance to Ukraine is anchored in the NDAA and the FAA. The NDAA authorizes the DoD to procure and deploy equipment for partner nations, while the FAA provides the broader policy context for foreign military assistance.<sup>14</sup> The Starshield program is also subject to the Defense Federal Acquisition Regulation Supplement (DFARS), which sets standards for procurement, cybersecurity, and data handling.<sup>15</sup></p> <p>Executive orders also play a role. President Biden’s Executive Order on “Providing Military Assistance to Ukraine” (EO 14008) authorizes the President to provide “all necessary defense equipment” to Ukraine, including communications systems.<sup>16</sup> However, the order also requires that assistance be “consistent with U.S. national security interests” and that it not “directly or indirectly contribute to the escalation of the conflict.”<sup>17</sup> These provisions mean that any decision to limit or expand Starshield’s use in Ukraine must be carefully balanced against both strategic and diplomatic considerations.</p> <h2>Implications for the Future of U.S. Aid to Ukraine</h2> <p>The Starshield program illustrates the broader challenges of providing military assistance in a high‑stakes, contested conflict. The U.S. must balance Ukraine’s operational needs with the risk of provoking a larger confrontation. The lack of a formal White House order limiting Starshield suggests that the administration is still deliberating how best to structure the program. The DoD’s ongoing negotiations with Ukrainian planners, coupled with congressional oversight, will likely shape the final scope of the system’s deployment.</p> <p>From a strategic perspective, the ability to provide secure, resilient communications could be decisive in future engagements. If the U.S. chooses to limit Starshield’s capacity, Ukraine may need to rely more heavily on ground‑based networks and other satellite systems, potentially reducing its operational tempo. Conversely, a full deployment could enhance Ukraine’s ability to coordinate complex operations, but it could also heighten the risk of escalation. The administration’s decision will therefore reflect a careful weighing of these trade‑offs.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Starshield remains a cornerstone of U.S. military assistance to Ukraine, embodying the nation’s commitment to support Ukraine’s defense capabilities while navigating the delicate balance of international diplomacy. While unverified claims about a White House order to limit the program have circulated, no credible evidence supports such a directive. Instead, the program’s scope is determined by the DoD’s procurement plans, congressional appropriations, and the broader legal framework governing U.S. military assistance. As the conflict continues to evolve, the U.S. will need to maintain transparency and engage in open dialogue with all stakeholders to ensure that aid remains effective, lawful, and aligned with U.S. national interests.</p>
