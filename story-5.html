Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

By Mastodon #science · Sep 24, 2026
<h2>Lede</h2> <p>During a series of observations with the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) and the Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) network, astronomers reported a narrow‑band radio signal that may originate from the temperate super‑Earth TOI‑700 c. Although the finding still awaits independent confirmation, it has sparked debate about whether we are witnessing the first direct detection of exoplanetary radio emission.</p> <h2>Background</h2> <p>The discovery of the first exoplanet orbiting a Sun‑like star in 1995 opened a new era of indirect planet detection through transits and radial‑velocity measurements. For decades, radio astronomers have sought a complementary, direct probe of planetary magnetospheres. The first attempts to detect exoplanetary radio emission date back to the early 2000s, when the Low‑Frequency Array (LOFAR) and the UTR‑2 telescope searched for the low‑frequency auroral bursts predicted by Zarka (2007). Those campaigns set upper limits of a few hundred millijanskys for nearby hot Jupiters (Lazio et al. 2014). More recent pathfinder observations with the Murchison Widefield Array (MWA) and the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT) have pushed the limits further, but no convincing signal has yet been reported (Ravi et al. 2021).</p> <p>Theoretical models suggest that the radio power of an exoplanet scales with its magnetic moment and the stellar wind pressure (Zarka 2008; Tilley et al. 2019). Hot Jupiters and close‑in super‑Earths are therefore prime targets, yet the expected flux densities at Earth are typically below 1 mJy at frequencies of 10–100 MHz. The SKA, with its unprecedented sensitivity and angular resolution, promises to change that landscape.</p> <h2>Observations and Data Processing</h2> <p>Between March and May 2025, the SKA mid‑frequency array (Band 2, 0.95–1.75 GHz) and a global VLBI network comprising the MeerKAT, ATCA, and the Very Long Baseline Array (VLBA) collected 120 hours of data on TOI‑700. The observations were scheduled to cover the planet’s full orbital phase, allowing the team to search for periodic modulation.</p> <p>The raw visibilities were pre‑processed with the standard SKA pipeline, including RFI flagging, bandpass calibration, and fringe fitting. The resulting time–frequency data cubes were then fed into a convolutional neural network (CNN) trained on a hybrid dataset of simulated auroral bursts (generated with the Auroral Emission Simulation Toolkit) and real pulsar and solar flare signals from the Pulsar Catalogue and the Solar Radio Burst Archive. The CNN architecture, described in detail by Klein et al. (2024), consists of three convolutional layers followed by a fully connected layer that outputs a probability of planetary origin. Cross‑validation on a held‑out set of 10,000 simulated signals yielded a false‑positive rate of 0.8 % and a true‑positive rate of 92 % for signals with S/N > 5.</p> <h2>Signal Characteristics</h2> <p>The algorithm flagged a narrow‑band feature at 1.42 GHz with a bandwidth of 1.2 kHz and a peak flux density of 0.9 ± 0.1 mJy. The signal appeared in three separate observing blocks spaced by 12 hours, suggesting a repeatable origin. Its dispersion measure (DM = 12.3 pc cm⁻³) matches the expected interstellar contribution to the line of sight, ruling out terrestrial sources. The VLBI imaging localized the emission to within 0.5 mas of the host star, consistent with the planet’s projected position at the time of detection.</p> <p>Despite these promising attributes, the team stresses that the signal’s S/N is only 5.3, and the narrow bandwidth could be produced by instrumental artifacts or residual RFI. The authors have released the calibrated visibilities, the CNN code, and a detailed log of the flagging steps in an open‑access repository (doi:10.5281/zenodo.1234567) to invite independent scrutiny.</p> <h2>Natural Versus Artificial Origins</h2> <p>Several experts argue that the most plausible explanation is auroral emission driven by the planet’s magnetic field interacting with the stellar wind (Gillon et al. 2017). The frequency of 1.4 GHz is higher than expected for a super‑Earth, but recent magnetohydrodynamic simulations (Tilley et al. 2020) suggest that strong magnetic fields can produce higher‑frequency cyclotron maser emission under certain conditions.</p> <p>Conversely, a minority view, championed by the Breakthrough Listen Initiative, points to the signal’s narrow bandwidth and persistence as hallmarks of a technosignature (Sullivan et al. 2020). However, natural solar system aurorae, such as Jupiter’s decametric bursts, can exhibit similar spectral features (Bastian et al. 2000). The lack of accompanying optical or infrared signatures further weakens the technosignature hypothesis.</p> <p>Potential systematic errors must also be considered. The SKA’s Band 2 receivers are susceptible to harmonic leakage from the 50 Hz power grid, and the VLBI correlator can introduce spurious narrow‑band features if the delay model is imperfect. The team performed a series of null tests using off‑source fields and found no comparable features, but the possibility of a rare instrumental artifact cannot be ruled out.</p> <h2>Community Response</h2> <p>Within weeks of the preprint’s release, independent groups at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory (NRAO) and the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy (MPIfR) began re‑analyzing archival data from the MWA and LOFAR. Early reports indicate no detection of a similar signal, but the sensitivity of those instruments at 1.4 GHz is limited. A joint working group, chaired by Dr. Maria Lopez (University of Cambridge) and Dr. Kenji Nakamura (National Astronomical Observatory of Japan), has been formed to coordinate follow‑up observations with the upgraded MeerKAT and the Australian Long Baseline Array (LBA).</p> <p>Funding agencies have taken notice. The National Science Foundation (NSF) and the European Research Council (ERC) have earmarked additional support for SKA Phase 2 upgrades that will improve low‑frequency sensitivity. The Australian Research Council and the South African National Research Foundation have pledged to enhance data‑sharing infrastructure, ensuring that raw visibilities are available to the global community within 48 hours of observation.</p> <h2>Policy and Ethical Considerations</h2> <p>The open‑access release of the data and analysis pipeline reflects a broader trend toward transparency in radio astronomy. However, the potential dual‑use nature of high‑resolution radio imaging—used both for scientific discovery and for national security—has prompted discussions about data governance. The consortium has adopted a policy that requires all participants to sign a data‑use agreement that prohibits the exploitation of the data for non‑scientific purposes.</p> <p>Ethically, the team emphasizes that the detection of a technosignature, even if tentative, would have profound societal implications. The Breakthrough Listen Initiative has drafted a set of guidelines for the responsible communication of such findings, including a staged disclosure protocol that involves national science agencies and the International Astronomical Union (IAU).</p> <h2>Future Work</h2> <p>Confirming the signal will require repeated observations with multiple facilities. The upcoming James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) will provide high‑resolution infrared spectra of TOI‑700 c, potentially revealing atmospheric signatures that correlate with magnetic activity. Meanwhile, the Extremely Large Telescope (ELT) will be able to search for Zeeman splitting in the planet’s sodium lines, offering an independent measure of the magnetic field strength.</p> <p>On the radio front, the SKA’s full‑sensitivity operation (planned for 2027) will enable routine monitoring of dozens of nearby exoplanets. A systematic survey of the nearest 200 systems, combined with machine‑learning pipelines trained on a broader range of simulated signals, could reveal a population of magnetized worlds and refine the empirical scaling laws that link radio power to planetary properties.</p> <p>From a methodological perspective, the team plans to expand the training set to include simulated signals from a wider array of planetary masses, orbital distances, and stellar activity levels. Cross‑validation with independent datasets from LOFAR and the MWA will help quantify the robustness of the CNN.</p>
