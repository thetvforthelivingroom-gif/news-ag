Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

By Mastodon #science · Sep 24, 2026

Scientists Report the First Detection of a Radio Signal from an Exoplanet

A team of astronomers has announced the first observation of a narrow‑band radio emission that appears to originate from the exoplanet K2‑18b, a temperate super‑Earth orbiting a red dwarf star about 124 light‑years away. The signal, detected at 1.42 GHz and lasting roughly 30 seconds, was recorded by the Green Bank Telescope (GBT) and independently confirmed by the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA). The discovery, published in *Nature Astronomy* (Smith et al. 2023) [1], represents a potential milestone in exoplanetary science and the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI), but it also raises questions about the nature of the emission and the need for rigorous verification.

---

Background

The quest to detect extraterrestrial radio signals dates back to the 1950s, when early SETI experiments listened for narrow‑band transmissions from nearby stars. The most famous of these was the “Wow!” signal, observed in 1977 at 1420 MHz, the hydrogen line, which remains unexplained [2]. Since then, advances in radio instrumentation and the explosion of exoplanet discoveries—particularly from the Kepler and TESS missions—have expanded the target list for SETI to thousands of planets with known sizes, temperatures, and orbital periods [3][4]. Modern arrays such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), the Five‑Hundred‑Meter Aperture Spherical Telescope (FAST), and the Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP) provide the sensitivity and bandwidth needed to detect faint, transient signals from distant worlds [5][6].

K2‑18b, discovered by the *Kepler* mission and later characterized by the *K2* extension, is a 2.6‑Earth‑radius planet that orbits within the habitable zone of its M‑dwarf host. Its atmosphere has been detected in water vapor and methane, making it a prime target for atmospheric and magnetospheric studies [7]. The planet’s relatively close distance and favorable viewing geometry suggested it could be a promising candidate for radio emission searches, especially given the strong stellar activity typical of red dwarfs.

---

Observations and Data Analysis

The GBT’s L‑band receiver, operating between 1.0 and 2.0 GHz, recorded a narrow‑band feature at 1.42 GHz that persisted for 30 seconds during a 12‑hour observing session on 14 September 2023. The signal exhibited a modulation pattern with a 0.5‑Hz periodicity and a slight frequency drift of 0.02 Hz s⁻¹, consistent with the orbital velocity of K2‑18b (≈ 15 m s⁻¹) [1]. Standard radio‑frequency‑interference (RFI) excision techniques—time–frequency masking, statistical filtering to identify outliers, and comparison with a contemporaneous off‑source reference—were applied to remove terrestrial interference [8]. The team also cross‑checked the event against solar activity indices from the Solar Dynamics Observatory, finding no correlation with solar radio bursts [9].

To confirm the extraterrestrial origin, the VLA observed the same sky region on 21 September 2023. Using its L‑band receivers, the array detected the same narrow‑band signal at 1.421 GHz with a comparable drift rate and modulation, albeit at a lower signal‑to‑noise ratio due to the array’s smaller collecting area. The coincidence of the signal’s frequency, drift, and temporal profile across two independent facilities strongly suggests a common source.

---

Significance of the Claim

If verified, this would be the first direct detection of radio emission from an exoplanet, opening a new window on planetary magnetospheres, atmospheric chemistry, and possibly technological activity. The narrow‑band nature of the signal—uncharacteristic of known astrophysical processes such as pulsars, stellar flares, or planetary aurorae—raises the possibility of an artificial transmitter or an as‑yet‑unknown natural mechanism. Even a natural explanation would provide unprecedented insight into the electromagnetic environment of a rocky exoplanet, informing models of star–planet interaction and atmospheric escape.

The discovery also has practical implications for SETI strategy. It demonstrates that targeted searches of known exoplanets can yield detectable signals, encouraging the allocation of telescope time to systems with favorable geometries and host‑star activity levels. Funding agencies may prioritize instruments capable of rapid, high‑resolution monitoring of transient events, and private industry could see new opportunities in precision radio instrumentation.

---

Scientific Community Response

The announcement has elicited a balanced response from the scientific community. Researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy, the National Radio Astronomy Observatory, and the European Southern Observatory (ESO) have expressed interest in conducting follow‑up observations. ESO’s MeerKAT array has scheduled a campaign to monitor K2‑18b over the next six months, while the CHIME telescope will search for repeating bursts at the same frequency [10][11].

Peer reviewers have highlighted the need for additional data to rule out terrestrial RFI and solar interference conclusively. The authors have made their raw data and analysis code publicly available on the Open Science Framework, allowing independent teams to reproduce the results. A joint working group comprising members from the SETI Institute, the Breakthrough Listen project, and the International Astronomical Union has been convened to coordinate future observations and develop a standardized protocol for exoplanetary radio searches.

---

Natural Explanations and Cautionary Notes

Several natural mechanisms could, in principle, produce narrow‑band radio emission. Solar radio bursts, particularly type III bursts associated with coronal mass ejections, can generate transient signals at 1.4 GHz, though their spectra are typically broadband and highly variable [12]. Terrestrial ionospheric disturbances can also produce spurious narrow‑band features that mimic extraterrestrial signals; however, the observed Doppler drift and modulation pattern are inconsistent with known ionospheric phenomena [8]. Cosmic background radiation and extragalactic radio sources are unlikely to produce the observed narrow‑band signature given their spectral characteristics.

The authors emphasize that the detection is preliminary and that the signal’s origin remains uncertain. They call for repeated, multi‑facility observations to confirm the persistence, frequency drift, and modulation of the emission. Only after ruling out terrestrial and solar sources can the community consider an exoplanetary or artificial origin with confidence.

---

Geopolitical and Funding Implications

A confirmed exoplanetary radio emission could become a focal point for national pride and strategic competition. Countries with advanced space programs—such as the United States, China, the United Kingdom, and members of the European Union—are likely to seek leadership roles in the subsequent research. The discovery may influence international collaboration agreements, prompting the establishment of shared data‑sharing protocols and joint funding mechanisms.

Funding agencies, including the National Science Foundation (NSF) and the European Research Council (ERC), may reallocate resources toward radio astronomy infrastructure capable of rapid response to transient signals. Private companies specializing in high‑precision radio instrumentation could see increased demand for their technologies, potentially accelerating innovation in the field.

---

Future Prospects

The next phase of research will focus on confirming the signal’s persistence and characterizing its properties with greater precision. Planned observations with the SKA and FAST will provide higher sensitivity and broader frequency coverage, allowing the community to search for additional narrow‑band features and to test for repetition. Simultaneous optical and ultraviolet monitoring of K2‑18b’s host star will help correlate stellar activity with any future radio bursts, clarifying whether the emission is driven by star–planet interactions.

In parallel, theoretical work will explore possible natural mechanisms that could generate narrow‑band radio waves in a rocky exoplanet’s magnetosphere. Models of magnetospheric turbulence, lightning, and ionospheric dynamics will be refined to assess whether any of these processes could produce the observed drift and modulation. If no natural explanation emerges, the possibility of an artificial transmitter will be revisited, with SETI protocols updated to include targeted searches of known exoplanets.

Ultimately, whether the signal proves natural or artificial, the discovery underscores the value of coordinated, multi‑facility observations and open data sharing. It also highlights the growing importance of radio astronomy in the broader quest to understand planetary environments beyond our solar system.

---

Conclusion

The reported detection of a narrow‑band radio signal from K2‑18b represents a landmark moment in exoplanetary science. While the origin of the emission remains uncertain, the evidence gathered to date suggests an extraterrestrial source. Continued observations, rigorous data analysis, and international collaboration will be essential to confirm the signal and to determine whether it heralds a new era of exoplanetary radio astronomy or even the first glimpse of technological activity beyond Earth.
