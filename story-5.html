Poland warned on 14 April that Russian forces might deploy drones or short‑range rockets to strike NATO territory, a message that has sharpened concerns across the alliance about the growing volatility on its eastern flank. The Polish Ministry of Foreign Affairs cautioned that unmanned aerial vehicles or low‑altitude rocket systems could be used against NATO installations in the region, a claim that follows a surge of Russian military activity near Poland’s borders since the 2022 invasion of Ukraine. The ministry’s statement urged NATO allies to “remain vigilant” and to “strengthen air‑defence measures” in the face of possible low‑altitude attacks [28]. It also called for a rapid NATO response plan, citing the need to protect critical infrastructure and military bases that could be vulnerable to such strikes [16]. Warsaw’s alert is not the first time the country has raised concerns about Russian aggression toward NATO territory. Earlier in the year, the Polish Defence Minister announced that the country would increase its air‑defence budget and request additional NATO assets, including Patriot batteries, to counter the growing threat of missile and drone attacks [15]. The ministry also referenced Russia’s recent use of drones in the Ukraine conflict, noting that similar tactics could be employed against Polish targets if the situation escalated further [23]. Poland’s position as a NATO member bordering Russia places it at the front line of any potential escalation. Since the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Warsaw has consistently highlighted the strategic importance of its airspace and the necessity of maintaining robust defence capabilities. In a broader context, the Polish government has been actively lobbying for increased NATO presence, arguing that the alliance’s collective defence commitments must adapt to new forms of warfare that include unmanned systems and precision rockets [18]. The country’s defence spending has risen sharply, with a focus on modernising radar networks and deploying counter‑drone technologies, reflecting a shift from conventional deterrence to a more nuanced security posture. The warning also reflects a broader trend of Russian military exercises that test the limits of NATO’s rapid reaction forces. In recent months, Russian forces have conducted large‑scale drills near the Polish border, simulating scenarios that involve the use of unmanned aerial vehicles and short‑range ballistic missiles against NATO targets. These exercises are designed to probe the alliance’s readiness and to signal Russia’s willingness to employ asymmetric tactics if conventional deterrence fails [16]. Poland’s statement, therefore, can be seen as both a deterrent signal to Moscow and a plea for stronger collective defence measures. From the perspective of NATO allies, Poland’s warning has prompted a reassessment of the alliance’s air‑defence architecture. The United States, for example, has reiterated its commitment to the “collective defence” principle, emphasizing that any attack on a member state would trigger Article 5 obligations. However, the U.S. has also acknowledged the challenges posed by low‑altitude drone swarms, which can evade traditional radar systems and require rapid, coordinated counter‑measures [18]. European allies have expressed concern that the Polish alert could lead to an arms race in the region, prompting calls for diplomatic engagement with Russia to de‑escalate tensions while simultaneously enhancing defensive capabilities. Poland’s own defence strategy has evolved in response to these developments. The country has invested in the development of the “Polish Air Defence System” (PADS), a network of radar stations, surface‑to‑air missile batteries, and electronic warfare units designed to detect and neutralise low‑flying threats. In addition, Warsaw has accelerated the procurement of the Patriot PAC‑2 and PAC‑3 batteries, which are capable of intercepting both ballistic missiles and unmanned aerial vehicles. These measures are part of a broader effort to create a layered defence that can respond to a range of threats, from conventional aircraft to swarms of small drones. The Russian drills near the Polish border have also highlighted the importance of intelligence sharing within NATO. Warsaw has called for the expansion of the alliance’s real‑time surveillance network, including the integration of satellite imagery, unmanned aerial reconnaissance, and signals‑intelligence feeds. By improving situational awareness, NATO can better anticipate and counter potential attacks, reducing the likelihood that Russian forces can exploit gaps in the alliance’s defensive perimeter. The Polish Ministry of Foreign Affairs has urged that NATO’s rapid reaction forces be positioned closer to the eastern flank, citing the need for a swift response to any incursion. The broader implications of Poland’s warning extend beyond the immediate security environment. It underscores the need for NATO to adapt its doctrine to the realities of hybrid warfare, where state and non‑state actors can employ a mix of conventional and unconventional tactics. The alliance must therefore balance deterrence with resilience, ensuring that its members can withstand attacks while maintaining the ability to respond decisively. This dual approach is essential in a landscape where low‑cost, high‑volume drone swarms can overwhelm traditional air‑defence systems, and where precision rockets can target critical infrastructure with minimal collateral damage. In conclusion, Poland’s alert about potential Russian drone and rocket strikes on NATO territory is a clear signal that the security environment in Eastern Europe has become more complex and dangerous. The warning has prompted NATO to reassess its air‑defence architecture, to strengthen intelligence sharing, and to accelerate the deployment of advanced missile‑defence systems. While diplomatic engagement with Russia remains essential to prevent further escalation, the alliance must also invest in the technologies and capabilities needed to counter asymmetric threats. The situation serves as a reminder that deterrence in the 21st century requires a blend of conventional force, advanced technology, and robust collective defence commitments. By addressing these challenges head‑on, NATO can preserve the security of its members and uphold the principles of collective defence that have guided the alliance since its founding.
