Ukraine Claims First Use of the FP‑7 “Malyutka‑2” Ballistic Missile in Combat

*October 15, 2026*

In a televised address to the nation on 14 October, President Volodymyr Zelenskiy announced that Ukrainian forces had fired an FP‑7 short‑range ballistic missile—captured from Russian stockpiles—against a logistics hub near the front line. The claim, echoed in a brief statement by the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD), marks the first time a Ukrainian official has publicly acknowledged the use of a ballistic missile in the ongoing conflict. Independent verification remains absent, and Russian officials have yet to confirm or deny the event. The development raises questions about Ukraine’s evolving strike capabilities, the strategic calculus of both sides, and the potential humanitarian fallout.¹

---

Ukrainian Statement

During a 45‑minute address from the presidential office in Kyiv, Zelenskiy described the missile as “captured from the enemy” and said the strike “demonstrated our ability to hit targets at a distance that was previously beyond our reach.” He added that the target was a Russian‑occupied logistics hub “located approximately 80 km from the front.” The MoD issued a short press release the same day, stating that the FP‑7 had been “launched from a mobile platform” and that the strike “achieved its intended effect.” No details on the launch platform’s exact type, the coordinates of the target, or the outcome of the strike were provided.²

The MoD also released a video clip showing a truck‑mounted launcher being prepared, though the clip was edited to remove identifying markings. The MoD’s spokesperson, Colonel Oleksandr Petrov, said the video was “intended to boost morale” and that “no sensitive information would be disclosed.”³

---

The FP‑7: Technical Overview

The FP‑7, officially designated 9M133 “Malyutka‑2,” is a Soviet‑era short‑range ballistic missile developed in the 1970s. It weighs approximately 2,500 kg and can be launched from a truck‑mounted or mobile launch vehicle. The missile carries a 300‑kg high‑explosive warhead and has a maximum range of about 120 km, with a circular error probable (CEP) of 200 m under optimal conditions.⁴

Russia has employed the FP‑7 in several conflicts, most notably in the Syrian civil war, where it struck airfields, command centers, and supply depots. The missile’s mobility and relatively low cost (estimated at $30,000–$50,000 per unit) make it attractive for rapid deployment in asymmetric warfare.⁵

Ukraine’s use of captured Soviet and Russian equipment is not new. Since the war’s outbreak in 2022, Ukrainian forces have seized and repurposed a range of weapons, from artillery systems to air‑defence batteries. The FP‑7 represents the latest addition to this portfolio, reflecting a strategic shift toward more advanced strike capabilities.⁶

---

Independent Verification

To date, no independent source—such as satellite imagery, battlefield reports, or third‑party intelligence—has confirmed the FP‑7 launch. Western intelligence analysts have urged caution, noting that the missile’s relatively short range and the chaotic nature of the front lines make verification difficult.⁷

A satellite image released by a commercial provider on 15 October shows smoke plumes near the reported target area, but the imagery is too low‑resolution to conclusively identify a ballistic missile impact. The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) has not yet reported any casualties or damage in the area.⁸

---

Russian Response

The Russian Ministry of Defence has not issued a formal statement regarding the alleged FP‑7 launch. However, a spokesperson for the Russian Foreign Ministry, Maria Zakharova, tweeted on 16 October that “the Russian Federation remains vigilant against any escalation” and that “the situation on the front is closely monitored.”⁹

No Russian military source has confirmed that Ukrainian forces captured an FP‑7 or that the missile was used in combat. In the absence of a direct denial, observers note that the lack of a response could be interpreted as a strategic silence, a common practice in the information war surrounding the conflict.¹⁰

---

Strategic Implications

Extending Ukraine’s Strike Envelope

If the FP‑7 was indeed fired, Ukraine would gain a new capability to strike targets up to 120 km away, potentially disrupting Russian logistics, command and control nodes, and supply depots. This could complement the existing array of Western‑supplied precision‑guided munitions, such as the U.S.‑made ATACMS and the British Storm Shadow, which have ranges of 300–400 km.¹¹

However, the missile’s limited range and relatively low accuracy compared to modern cruise missiles mean that it would be most effective against well‑defined, high‑value targets in open terrain. The Ukrainian MoD has emphasized that the FP‑7 “complements, rather than replaces, our existing precision‑strike capabilities.”¹²

Potential for Escalation

The use of a ballistic missile by Ukrainian forces could be perceived by Russia as a provocative escalation, potentially prompting a counter‑strike with more advanced long‑range weapons. Analysts warn that the deployment could trigger a tit‑for‑tat exchange of missile systems, raising the risk of a broader arms race on the battlefield.¹³

Russia’s own short‑range ballistic missile inventory, including the 9M133 and the newer 9M120 “Kinzhal,” is designed for rapid, high‑precision strikes. The introduction of an additional missile system into Ukrainian arsenals may prompt the Russian military to enhance its air‑defence networks, deploy more surface‑to‑air missiles, and increase counter‑measure capabilities.¹⁴

Humanitarian Considerations

The FP‑7’s 120‑km range and 300‑kg warhead mean that strikes near civilian settlements could cause significant collateral damage. Humanitarian organizations, including the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), have called for increased transparency and adherence to international humanitarian law. The ICRC’s spokesperson, Dr. Anna Sokolova, warned that “any use of ballistic missiles in populated areas must be accompanied by rigorous target verification and risk assessment.”¹⁵

The Ukrainian MoD has stated that the target was a logistics hub “located in a sparsely populated area.” Yet, independent observers note that the proximity of civilian infrastructure in the region remains a concern.¹⁶

Expert Perspectives

Military Analyst – Dr. Elena Kuznetsova (Center for Strategic Studies, Moscow)

“From a purely technical standpoint, the FP‑7 is a modest system. Its range is limited, and its accuracy is inferior to modern cruise missiles. However, in the context of Ukraine’s current inventory, it provides a new tool for disrupting Russian supply lines. The real question is whether Ukraine can maintain and operate these systems effectively, given the logistical challenges of operating captured equipment.”¹⁷

Humanitarian Observer – Mr. Thomas Lee (International Crisis Group)

“Ballistic missiles, by their nature, produce a large blast radius. Even with a 200‑m CEP, there is a risk of civilian casualties if the target is near a settlement. Ukraine’s claim that the target was in a sparsely populated area is reassuring, but we need independent verification of the impact and any resulting damage.”¹⁸

International Law Expert – Prof. Maria Sánchez (University of Salamanca)

“The use of ballistic missiles in conflict zones is governed by the principles of distinction and proportionality under international humanitarian law. Ukraine must ensure that the target is a legitimate military objective and that the anticipated collateral damage is not excessive. The lack of independent verification makes it difficult to assess compliance with these principles.”¹⁹

Looking Ahead

1. **Verification Efforts** – International observers, including the UN and NATO, will likely intensify efforts to confirm the use of the FP‑7 through satellite imagery, signal‑intercept data, and on‑the‑ground reporting. 2. **Supply Chain Dynamics** – If the FP‑7 proves operationally effective, Ukraine may seek additional short‑range ballistic missiles from captured stockpiles or pursue Western assistance for similar systems. 3. **Russian Counter‑Measures** – Russia may accelerate the deployment of mobile air‑defence units and surface‑to‑air missile batteries in response to the new threat. 4. **Humanitarian Monitoring** – Civilian protection agencies will monitor the area for any signs of collateral damage and advocate for transparent reporting. 5. **Diplomatic Repercussions** – The deployment may influence NATO’s deliberations on providing Ukraine with more advanced missile systems, balancing the need for deterrence against the risk of escalating the conflict.

---

Conclusion

President Zelenskiy’s announcement of Ukraine’s first FP‑7 launch marks a potential turning point in the conflict’s tactical landscape. While the missile’s technical specifications suggest a modest but meaningful extension of Ukraine’s strike envelope, the lack of independent verification and the absence of a Russian counter‑statement leave the claim in a gray zone. The strategic implications—ranging from potential escalation to humanitarian concerns—underscore the complexity of modern warfare, where captured equipment, asymmetric tactics, and information operations intertwine. As the world watches, the next few weeks will reveal whether the FP‑7 becomes a new tool in Ukraine’s arsenal or a cautionary tale about the perils of rapid technological adaptation in a high‑stakes conflict.
