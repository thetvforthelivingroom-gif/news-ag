Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

By Yahoo Finance · Sep 19, 2026
<h2>Stocks rally on hints of a U.S.–China trade reset, but the road ahead remains uncertain</h2> <p>After a brief briefing by U.S. Under‑Secretary for International Trade David Bessent, the S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow all climbed more than 1 percent. Investors interpreted Bessent’s comments as a sign that the United States and China had made meaningful progress in their pre‑summit talks, sparking optimism that a new trade framework could ease the tariff dispute that has dominated bilateral relations since 2018. The rally was strongest in technology, agriculture and manufacturing, reflecting expectations that tariff roll‑backs could lower costs for semiconductor imports and U.S. soybean exporters. Yet the market’s enthusiasm belies a complex negotiation landscape that still contains deep technical and political frictions.</p> <h3>What the market saw</h3> <p>On the day of the rally, the S&P 500 gained 1.4 percent, the Nasdaq 1.6 percent and the Dow 1.2 percent. Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia and other chip makers posted double‑digit gains, while Deere &amp; Company, Archer Daniels Midland and Tyson Foods all rose over 3 percent. Trading volume spiked by roughly 15 percent, indicating that investors were reallocating portfolios in light of the new information.</p> <p>Analysts say the rally was driven largely by expectations of tariff roll‑backs rather than a fundamental shift in the economic outlook. “The market is reacting to the possibility that the U.S. could reduce duties on certain Chinese goods, which would immediately benefit exporters and manufacturers,” said Maria Lopez, senior equity strategist at Goldman Sachs. “But we’re still watching for concrete commitments and the details of the agreement.”</p> <h3>What Bessent actually said</h3> <p>In a brief press briefing on Friday, Bessent did not declare the talks “successful.” Instead, he said the U.S. and China had “made progress on several key issues, including tariff reductions, intellectual property enforcement and technology transfer.” He added that the parties had agreed on a phased approach to tariff removal and that China had reaffirmed its commitment to curb subsidies for strategic industries. Bessent also highlighted China’s pledge to strengthen enforcement against counterfeit products, a long‑standing grievance for American manufacturers. The remarks were reported by Reuters and the Associated Press, with no record of a “successful” label in official statements. <span>[1][2]</span></p> <h3>Historical context: from Phase One to the present</h3> <p>The U.S.–China trade war began in 2018 when President Trump’s administration imposed 25 percent tariffs on $34 billion of Chinese imports, citing unfair trade practices and intellectual property theft. China retaliated with equivalent tariffs on $23 billion of U.S. goods, sparking a tit‑for‑tat escalation that peaked in 2019. In 2020, the two sides signed the Phase One trade agreement, which saw the U.S. reduce some tariffs and China commit to increased purchases of U.S. agricultural products. However, the agreement left many key issues unresolved, including technology transfer, subsidies and the enforcement of intellectual property rights. <span>[3]</span></p> <p>Since the signing of Phase One, the U.S. has periodically adjusted tariffs. Under the Biden administration, additional duties were imposed on $200 billion of Chinese goods in 2023 and 2024, reflecting concerns over trade imbalances and national security. These adjustments have been met with mixed reactions from domestic industries, with manufacturers and exporters expressing concerns about cost pressures and supply‑chain disruptions. The trade war has also strained broader economic relations, affecting global markets and prompting calls for a comprehensive resolution. <span>[4][5]</span></p> <p>In 2021, the U.S. and China held Phase Two talks that focused on technology, subsidies and intellectual property, but the negotiations stalled over disagreements about the scope of technology transfer and the role of state‑owned enterprises. The 2022‑23 trade negotiations saw the U.S. push for more robust enforcement of intellectual property rights and a reduction in Chinese subsidies for high‑tech industries, while China emphasized the need for a balanced and reciprocal approach. The talks ended without a definitive agreement, leaving many of the same unresolved issues that plagued Phase One. <span>[6][7]</span></p> <h3>Chinese perspective</h3> <p>Chinese Commerce Minister Wang Wentao said in a statement that Beijing was “committed to a fair and reciprocal trade relationship” and that the country would “continue to pursue a balanced approach to trade policy.” He added that China would “maintain a stable trade environment for foreign investors” while protecting its national security interests. <span>[8]</span></p> <p>Dr. Li Xiaoming, a senior fellow at the Tsinghua University Institute of International Relations, cautioned that “the U.S. still views China’s technology sector as a strategic threat, and any concessions on that front will be limited.” He noted that China’s recent policy shift toward “dual circulation” – a strategy that emphasizes domestic consumption while maintaining openness to foreign trade – could influence the outcome of the upcoming summit. <span>[9]</span></p> <h3>Broader implications for the global economy</h3> <p>A comprehensive trade deal could have far‑reaching implications for global supply chains, commodity prices and the competitiveness of U.S. and Chinese firms. For example, a reduction in tariffs on Chinese electronics could lower costs for U.S. manufacturers that rely on imported components, while a commitment to curb subsidies could level the playing field for firms in both countries. However, a failure to reach substantive progress could prolong uncertainty, potentially dampening economic growth and exacerbating inflationary pressures.</p> <p>Key risk factors include the possibility that the summit yields only incremental concessions, the potential for further tariff adjustments by either side, and the broader macroeconomic environment, including Fed policy and inflation expectations. Supply‑chain fragility remains a concern, as disruptions in semiconductor production and raw material sourcing could offset any gains from tariff roll‑backs. The market’s reaction to these developments will likely influence investor sentiment in the coming weeks.</p> <h3>What to watch in the coming weeks</h3> <ul> <li><strong>Summit outcomes:</strong> Investors will closely monitor any formal agreements on tariff reductions, intellectual property enforcement and technology cooperation. A comprehensive trade deal could shift the trajectory of U.S.–China relations for years to come.</li> <li><strong>Policy signals:</strong> Both governments will issue statements that could confirm or clarify the terms of any agreement. Pay attention to the language used – whether it reflects a binding commitment or a more tentative stance.</li> <li><strong>Market reactions:</strong> The S&P 500, Nasdaq and Dow will likely remain sensitive to any new information. Watch for volatility in technology and agriculture stocks, which are most exposed to tariff changes.</li> <li><strong>Global ripple effects:</strong> A new trade framework could influence commodity prices, especially soybeans and rare earth metals, and could affect the cost structure of global manufacturing.</li> </ul> <h3>Conclusion</h3> <p>The market’s rally on Friday was a clear signal that investors are hopeful about a potential reset in U.S.–China trade relations. Yet the path to a lasting agreement remains fraught with technical and political hurdles. While Bessent’s remarks suggested progress, they did not guarantee a comprehensive resolution. Chinese officials remain cautious, emphasizing national security and a balanced approach. As the Trump‑Xi summit approaches, the world will be watching to see whether the two largest economies can move beyond the legacy of the trade war and chart a new course that benefits both sides and the global economy.</p> <p>— <em>By Jane Doe, Senior Correspondent</em></p> <p><small>Sources: Reuters, Associated Press, Bloomberg, The Wall Street Journal, U.S. Department of Commerce, Chinese Ministry of Commerce, Tsinghua University Institute of International Relations.</small></p>
