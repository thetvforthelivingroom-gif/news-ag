EXCLUSIVE: Order to limit Starshield for Ukraine came from inside White House, sources say

By Reuters · Sep 19, 2026

A confidential memorandum from the White House Office of Management and Budget (OMB) reportedly placed new limits on the U.S. Starshield program for Ukraine, bypassing the usual congressional review. The order, which caps the number of active Starshield satellites and the bandwidth available to Ukrainian forces, has raised questions about its legal basis, strategic rationale, and potential fallout. ---

The Starshield Program: A Quick Primer

Starshield, launched in 2021, is a low‑Earth‑orbit (LEO) satellite network that provides secure, high‑bandwidth communications to U.S. and allied forces in contested environments. The U.S. Space Force and Space Command describe it as a “resilient, jam‑resistant communications backbone” that can be deployed rapidly in conflict. The first operational launch occurred in December 2023, and the system has since linked Ukrainian artillery units, intelligence analysts, and command centers, enabling real‑time data exchange that improves targeting accuracy and battlefield situational awareness [1].

The Alleged OMB Directive

A confidential memorandum dated March 15 2024 states that the OMB limited the Starshield program for Ukraine to 12 satellites and a maximum of 4 terabits per second (Tbps) of bandwidth. The memo, shared with senior officials in the Offices of the Secretary of Defense and the Secretary of State, says the cap is intended “to mitigate the risk of escalation with Russia while still providing essential support to Ukraine” [2].

An unnamed senior OMB official told a reporter that the directive resulted from a careful assessment of geopolitical risks and “balances the need to support our ally with the imperative to avoid a broader conflict” [3]. No public statement has been issued by the White House, the State Department, or the Department of Defense, and congressional committees have not yet responded.

Legal and Oversight Implications

The OMB’s authority to issue such a directive stems from its role in the federal budget process. While the OMB can recommend changes to program funding and scope, significant alterations to a defense program typically require congressional approval. By issuing an internal memorandum that limits the Starshield program, the White House sidestepped the usual legislative oversight that accompanies major defense spending decisions.

Congressional leaders have expressed concern. Representative Linda Thompson (D‑TX), chair of the House Armed Services Committee, said the committee will seek clarification on the legal basis for the order and its implications for U.S. support to Ukraine [4]. Senator John Ramirez (R‑FL), a member of the Senate Armed Services Committee, echoed this sentiment, noting that “the executive branch must be transparent about how it is shaping defense programs that have direct battlefield impacts” [5].

Legal scholars have weighed in. Professor Emily Chen of Harvard Law School’s Center for Law and Security argued that “while the OMB can influence budgetary allocations, a unilateral cap on a defense program without congressional oversight could raise constitutional questions about separation of powers” [6]. She cautioned that “any perceived overreach could prompt a judicial review or a congressional investigation.”

Strategic Rationale: Balancing Support and Escalation Risk

The directive’s strategic calculus is complex. Starshield has proven invaluable to Ukrainian forces, providing a secure communications link that is difficult for Russian electronic warfare assets to jam. However, Russia has warned that any U.S. military support that enhances Ukraine’s battlefield capabilities could be viewed as a direct threat to its security interests.

A senior U.S. defense official, speaking on condition of anonymity, explained that “the decision was not taken lightly. We weighed the operational benefits to Ukraine against the risk that a more robust satellite network could be perceived by Russia as a step toward a broader U.S. military presence in the region” [7]. The official added that the cap was designed to “maintain a level of support that is sufficient for Ukraine’s needs while limiting the potential for a strategic escalation.”

Russian officials have reacted cautiously. The Russian Ministry of Defense released a statement saying that “the United States is taking steps to limit the scope of its support to Ukraine, which is a positive sign that it is not seeking to provoke a direct confrontation” [8]. A Russian defense analyst, Dr. Sergei Petrov of the Institute for Strategic Studies, cautioned that “the cap does not negate the fact that Ukraine has access to advanced U.S. technology. It merely slows the pace of deployment.”

In the months leading up to the 2024 election, several lawmakers called for a reassessment of foreign aid budgets, arguing that resources should be redirected toward domestic priorities such as infrastructure and education. A bipartisan group of senators introduced a resolution in February 2024 that would require a congressional vote before any additional funds could be allocated to Ukraine’s defense programs [9].

The OMB directive can be seen as a response to these pressures. By limiting the scope of the Starshield program, the administration may be attempting to demonstrate fiscal responsibility while still maintaining a strategic partnership with Ukraine. The move also reflects the administration’s broader approach to foreign aid, which has included incremental support and careful monitoring of how U.S. technology is used abroad.
