Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

By Mastodon #science · Sep 24, 2026
<article> <h1>Candidate Auroral Radio Emission from the Exoplanet Gliese 876 b: A First‑Look Analysis</h1> A team of astronomers has reported a tentative detection of auroral radio emission from the hot Jupiter Gliese 876 b, a gas giant that orbits the nearby M‑dwarf Gliese 876. The signal, observed at a central frequency of about 30 MHz with the LOw Frequency ARray (LOFAR) in the Netherlands and confirmed by the Murchison Widefield Array (MWA) in Australia, shows properties that could be produced by a cyclotron‑maser mechanism powered by the planet’s magnetic field. However, the evidence remains unconfirmed, and other explanations—including stellar activity and terrestrial radio frequency interference (RFI)—cannot yet be ruled out. The study, published in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* (2026), calls for independent verification and highlights the difficulties of detecting exoplanetary radio emission at low frequencies. ---

Observational Campaign and Data Processing

The researchers carried out a series of targeted observations over a 12‑month period, focusing on the 10–80 MHz band where cyclotron‑maser emission from magnetized planets is expected to peak. LOFAR’s international stations delivered sub‑arcminute angular resolution and a time resolution of 0.1 s, which allowed the team to identify transient bursts. The MWA contributed complementary data with a larger field of view and a lower system temperature in the southern hemisphere.

To isolate potential planetary signals, the authors applied a multi‑stage pipeline:

1. **RFI excision** using median filtering and the AOFlagger algorithm to suppress narrowband terrestrial interference. 2. **Polarization calibration** to recover Stokes V (circular polarization) and Stokes I (total intensity). 3. **Spectral‑time cross‑correlation** against a model of the expected Doppler shift for Gliese 876 b, accounting for its 2.64‑day orbital period and known radial‑velocity amplitude. 4. **Statistical significance testing** via a matched‑filter approach, yielding a signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) of about 4.5 for the strongest burst.

The most prominent burst, recorded on 12 April 2025, lasted roughly 3 s, had a peak flux density of 20 mJy, and displayed right‑hand circular polarization exceeding 70 % of the total intensity. The emission was confined to a 5 MHz bandwidth centered at 30 MHz. According to cyclotron‑maser theory, this frequency corresponds to a magnetic field strength of roughly 10 G at the source region (B ≈ 2 π m f / e, where f is the frequency and m, e are the electron mass and charge). This field strength aligns with dynamo models for hot Jupiters (e.g., Grießmeier et al. 2007).

---

Historical Context and Theoretical Background

The search for exoplanetary radio emission began in the 1970s, when Zarka (2007) suggested that magnetized planets should generate decametric bursts similar to Jupiter’s powerful 10–40 MHz emissions. Early attempts with single‑dish radio telescopes, such as the Green Bank Telescope and the VLA, failed to detect such signals because the expected fluxes are faint and the ionosphere blocks frequencies below about 10 MHz (Farrell et al. 1999). The development of large low‑frequency arrays—LOFAR, MWA, and the forthcoming Square Kilometre Array (SKA)—has dramatically improved sensitivity. These instruments achieve system equivalent flux densities (SEFD) of roughly 10 Jy at 30 MHz, enabling the detection of flux densities as low as a few mJy with reasonable integration times.

Theoretical models predict that close‑in gas giants (orbital periods < 10 days) can produce detectable auroral radio emission if their magnetic fields are strong enough to interact with the stellar wind. The power of the emission scales with the stellar wind ram pressure and the planet’s magnetic moment (Zarka 1998). For Gliese 876 b, the predicted radio power is on the order of 10^15 W, which translates to a flux density of about 10 mJy at Earth, assuming isotropic emission and a distance of 4.7 pc.

---

Alternative Explanations and Independent Verification

While the burst’s properties are compatible with cyclotron‑maser emission, several alternative scenarios remain plausible:

- **Stellar activity.** M‑dwarfs can produce coherent radio bursts with high circular polarization and narrow bandwidths (Hallinan et al. 2013). The timing of the burst aligns with the orbital phase of Gliese 876 b, reducing the likelihood of a stellar origin, but the star’s magnetic cycle and flaring behavior are not fully characterized. - **Terrestrial RFI.** Despite aggressive excision, narrowband RFI can mimic transient bursts, especially at low frequencies. The MWA data, taken from a remote site, provide an independent check, but the burst was only marginally detected in the MWA dataset, raising concerns about residual interference. - **Instrumental artifacts.** Beamforming errors or calibration drifts can produce spurious signals. The authors performed null tests using off‑source fields and found no comparable bursts, but the statistical significance remains low.

To address these uncertainties, the team released the raw visibilities, calibration tables, and analysis scripts under an open‑source license. Independent groups—including the Australian National University (ANU) Low‑Frequency Array team and the University of Cambridge’s LOFAR data analysis group—are re‑examining the archival data. Preliminary reports from the ANU group suggest that a similar burst appears in a separate May 2025 observation, albeit at a lower SNR (~3.2). No other group has yet reproduced the 30 MHz burst with comparable significance.

---

Quantitative Estimates and Implications

Assuming the burst originates from Gliese 876 b, the inferred magnetic field strength (~10 G) implies a magnetic moment of about 10^27 A m², comparable to Jupiter’s (~1.6 × 10^27 A m²). This field strength is consistent with dynamo models that incorporate rapid rotation and deep convective envelopes (Reiners & Christensen 2010). The narrow bandwidth (~5 MHz) suggests a localized source region, possibly the planet’s auroral oval.

If confirmed, the detection would provide a direct probe of the planet’s magnetosphere, offering insights into atmospheric retention and potential habitability. Magnetic shielding can mitigate atmospheric escape driven by stellar winds and high‑energy particles (Lammer et al. 2007). For Gliese 876 b, a robust magnetic field could preserve a substantial atmosphere, influencing its internal heat transport and the possibility of hosting moons with atmospheres.

Beyond Gliese 876 b, the study underscores the feasibility of low‑frequency radio surveys for exoplanetary magnetism. The SKA, with its unprecedented sensitivity and wide frequency coverage (50–350 MHz), is projected to detect auroral radio emission from dozens of hot Jupiters and potentially from Neptune‑mass planets around nearby M‑dwarfs (Lazio et al. 2009). Such a census would illuminate the distribution of magnetic field strengths across planetary systems, informing models of planetary dynamo generation and evolution.

---

Community Response and Future Directions

The exoplanetary community has responded with cautious enthusiasm. Several researchers applaud the methodological rigor and the potential scientific payoff, while others emphasize the need for stringent confirmation. The European Southern Observatory (ESO) has announced a dedicated program to monitor Gliese 876 with the MeerKAT array, aiming to obtain simultaneous optical and radio observations to correlate stellar activity with radio bursts.
