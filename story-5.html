Recent incidents that underpin the warning

By News · Sep 20, 2026
Poland has warned that Russia is preparing drone and rocket strikes against NATO territory, a statement that has once again put the alliance’s eastern flank under scrutiny. On 14 October 2024, the Polish Ministry of Foreign Affairs briefed the Sejm’s National Security Committee, and Under‑Secretary of State Paweł Szalaj said that “intelligence assessments indicate a heightened risk of asymmetric attacks against NATO infrastructure, including potential drone incursions and rocket fire originating from Belarusian or Russian territory” [1]. The alert is not an isolated claim; it follows a pattern of hybrid activity that has intensified since Russia’s full‑scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. Warsaw urged allied capitals to review force‑protection protocols and called for an extraordinary session of the NATO‑Russia Council to address the growing concern [2].

Recent incidents that underpin the warning

Poland’s warning is grounded in a series of events that have tested NATO’s eastern flank over the past two years. In March 2023, a Russian‑made Shahed‑136 drone crashed in a field near the Polish village of Biała Podlaska. The incident triggered an immediate NATO Article 4 consultation and a joint statement by Warsaw and Kyiv that the crash was “a clear violation of Polish sovereignty and a test of alliance resolve” [3]. In July 2024, a fragment of a cruise missile was found in Lithuanian territory. Vilnius attributed the fragment to Russian kinetic strikes aimed at degrading Ukrainian logistics corridors that pass near the Baltic states. Lithuanian defence officials said the fragment’s trajectory was consistent with a missile launched from the Russian‑occupied Donbas region, raising doubts about the extent of Russia’s reach [4]. These episodes have reinforced Warsaw’s view that the conflict’s front lines are creeping westward and that NATO member states must prepare for the possibility of direct hits on airbases, logistics hubs, and civilian infrastructure.

Poland‑Russia security relations

Poland and Russia have long shared a complex relationship marked by suspicion, energy dependence, and a shared border that has often been a flashpoint. The 2010 Smolensk air disaster, which killed Poland’s president and 95 other officials, remains a subject of intense debate in Warsaw. Some Polish officials and investigators have suggested Russian involvement or negligence, a claim Moscow has consistently denied [5]. Following the 2014 annexation of Crimea and the war in Donbas, Poland became one of the most vocal advocates for strengthening NATO’s presence on its eastern border. It hosted multinational battle groups and invested heavily in missile‑defence capabilities. In 2022, the Polish Ministry of National Defence allocated an additional 1.5 billion zlotys to upgrade its short‑range air‑defence inventory, citing the need to counter loitering munitions and rocket artillery that could target airfields in eastern Poland [6]. The current warning reflects a continuation of this trajectory, with a new emphasis on aerial and rocket threats that can be deployed with plausible deniability.

NATO’s stance

From the NATO perspective, the alliance remains vigilant and prepared to defend all member territory. However, officials have been cautious about amplifying individual national warnings that could heighten public alarm without corroborating evidence from multiple intelligence sources. NATO Secretary General Jens Stoltenberg said in a press conference on 16 October 2024 that “all Allies take any potential threat seriously, and we have robust measures in place to protect our airspace and critical infrastructure” [7]. He emphasized that the alliance’s integrated air‑and‑missile defence system, which includes contributions from the United States, Germany, and the Baltic states, is continuously adapted to counter emerging threats. Behind the scenes, NATO military planners have been reviewing contingency scenarios that assume a coordinated drone‑and‑rocket assault designed to saturate air‑defence buffers and disrupt the deployment of reinforcement troops. The Polish warning has found a receptive—but measured—audience within the alliance, prompting a temporary acceleration of force‑protection reviews in the Polish, Lithuanian, and Latvian sectors [8].

Russian reaction

Russia has dismissed the Polish alert as “Russophobic hysteria” designed to justify increased military spending and the permanent stationing of foreign troops on Polish soil. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said on 15 October 2024 that “Poland is once again using the Russian threat to rally NATO support and secure additional funding for its armed forces” and added that “Russia has no interest in escalating the conflict beyond the borders of Ukraine, and any allegations to the contrary are devoid of factual basis” [9]. Russian military analysts, speaking on condition of anonymity, suggested that the warning could be a prelude to a Polish request for tactical nuclear sharing or the deployment of longer‑range strike systems on its territory. Others pointed to Russia’s stated doctrine of “escalate to de‑escalate,” which reserves the right to use limited kinetic force to achieve political objectives, as a possible framework for understanding the drone and rocket threats Poland cites [10]. Nonetheless, open‑source intelligence outlets such as Bellingcat and the Institute for the Study of War have documented a steady increase in Russian‑launched UAV missions over the Baltic Sea and Belarusian airspace since mid‑2023, lending some credence to the notion that Warsaw’s concerns are not entirely unfounded [11].

Broader implications

The warning extends beyond the immediate threat of drones and rockets. It underscores the fragility of the security architecture in Central and Eastern Europe and the challenges of maintaining deterrence in a world where conventional and hybrid warfare overlap. The incident also highlights the importance of intelligence sharing and joint operational planning within NATO. While Warsaw’s call for an extraordinary session of the NATO‑Russia Council has not yet materialised, it has prompted the alliance to revisit its rapid‑response protocols and to consider the deployment of additional air‑defence assets along the Polish‑Belarusian border [12].

Domestic debate

Poland’s stance is not without domestic debate. Some opposition parties argue that the government is using the threat to justify a significant increase in defence spending, which could strain the country’s already stretched budget. Others contend that the warning is a necessary step to protect national sovereignty and to signal to Russia that Warsaw will not tolerate further encroachments. Public opinion is mixed: polls indicate that a majority of Poles support increased defence spending in light of the perceived threat, while a smaller segment fears that such moves could provoke Moscow and lead to a new escalation [13].

Future outlook

In the long term, the situation points to a need for a more robust framework for addressing hybrid threats. NATO’s current doctrine, while comprehensive, may need to be updated to account for the rapid evolution of drone technology and the increasing use of loitering munitions. The alliance’s “Integrated Air‑and‑Missile Defence” (IAMD) system, operational since 2019, will likely see further upgrades, including the deployment of advanced radar systems and the integration of artificial‑intelligence‑based threat detection algorithms. These measures could help mitigate the risk of a successful drone or rocket strike on NATO territory and reinforce the alliance’s deterrence posture [14].

Dialogue and confidence‑building

The dialogue between Warsaw and Moscow, while strained, remains a critical component of regional stability. While the Kremlin has dismissed the Polish warning as a provocation, it has also signalled a willingness to engage in confidence‑building measures, such as the restoration of the 2014‑2015 “Open Skies” flights over the Polish‑Belarusian border. Whether these gestures will translate into tangible reductions in hybrid activity remains to be seen, but they illustrate the complex interplay between deterrence, diplomacy, and defence readiness in the post‑Cold War era [15].

Conclusion

Poland’s warning that Russia is planning drone and rocket strikes against NATO territory is a stark reminder of the evolving nature of security threats in Europe. It reflects a broader pattern of hybrid activity that has intensified since the invasion of Ukraine and underscores the need for a coordinated, intelligence‑driven response from the alliance. While the Russian government has dismissed the alert as a political manoeuvre, the evidence of increased UAV activity and missile fragments found in Baltic states suggests that Warsaw’s concerns are grounded in real, albeit covert, developments. As NATO continues to adapt its defence posture, the alliance’s ability to share intelligence, conduct joint exercises, and maintain a credible deterrent will be essential to preventing a spill‑over of the Ukraine conflict into the heart of Europe.
