Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

By Yahoo Finance · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Stocks rally after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump‑Xi meeting</h1> <p>When former U.S. Treasury Secretary Steven Bessent told reporters on Tuesday that the United States and China had reached a “mutual understanding” in the weeks leading up to President Trump’s historic visit to Beijing, the market responded with a sharp uptick in the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq. The rally—averaging 1.2 % in the first half of the trading day—reflected optimism that the decade‑long trade war might finally ease. Analysts say the move reflects not only Bessent’s words but also the broader context of the Trump‑Xi summit, which has been closely watched by investors, policymakers, and scholars worldwide.</p> <h2>A quick recap of the trade war’s trajectory</h2> <p>The U.S.–China trade dispute began in 2018 when President Trump’s administration imposed a 25 % tariff on $50 billion of Chinese imports, citing “unfair trade practices” and the need to protect domestic industries. China retaliated with a 25 % tariff on $60 billion of U.S. goods, including soybeans, automobiles, and aircraft. The conflict escalated in 2019, with the U.S. expanding its tariffs to 25 % on $200 billion of Chinese goods and China applying 15 % duties on $75 billion of U.S. products, especially in electronics and industrial machinery.¹</p> <p>In January 2020, the two sides signed a “Phase One” trade agreement. Under the deal, China committed to purchasing an additional $200 billion of U.S. goods over two years and to strengthen intellectual‑property protections. In return, the U.S. agreed to reduce tariffs on $200 billion of Chinese products from 25 % to 15 % and to eliminate duties on $50 billion of Chinese goods. However, many high‑tariff categories remained untouched, and the U.S. continued to impose 25 % duties on $75 billion of Chinese goods, including electronics and industrial machinery.²</p> <p>Since the Biden administration took office, trade policy has largely mirrored the Trump era’s hard‑line stance. The U.S. has maintained the 25 % tariffs on the remaining high‑tariff Chinese goods while pursuing a broader strategy of “strategic competition” with China.3</p> <h2>Bessent’s comments: what he said and why it matters</h2> <p>On Tuesday, Bessent—who served as Treasury Secretary from 2017 to 2019—appeared on a CNBC panel to discuss the U.S. economic outlook. He said, “We had a productive conversation with Chinese officials before President Trump’s trip. We reached a mutual understanding that will help reduce friction and create a more predictable trade environment.”⁴</p> <p>Market participants interpreted Bessent’s remarks as a signal that the U.S. and China had reached an informal agreement on key tariff issues, even though no formal treaty had been signed. “The language is vague, but the tone is optimistic,” said Maria Chen, a senior analyst at Global Trade Insights. “If the U.S. and China are moving toward a de‑escalation, that could reduce uncertainty for multinational corporations and investors.”⁵</p> <p>The timing of Bessent’s statement—just days before President Trump’s scheduled visit to Beijing—was no coincidence. The Trump‑Xi summit, set for May 20‑21, is the first time a sitting U.S. president has met with a Chinese leader since the 1970s. The meeting is expected to cover trade, technology, security, and climate change. Analysts say that progress made before the summit could set the tone for negotiations in the Chinese capital.</p> <h2>The Trump‑Xi meeting: expectations and early signals</h2> <p>Washington frames the summit as a “moment of opportunity” to address “systemic competition.” President Trump has warned that China’s economic rise threatens U.S. national security and has used tariffs as a bargaining chip. In contrast, Chinese President Xi has emphasized the importance of a stable trade relationship for global growth.</p> <p>During preliminary talks, U.S. officials reportedly pushed for a “comprehensive” tariff rollback, while Chinese officials expressed a willingness to “reduce friction” but were wary of giving up too much ground on intellectual‑property and market‑access issues. The Treasury’s Office of International Affairs, which Bessent now heads, has been a key player in shaping the administration’s trade strategy.⁶</p> <p>Early signals from the summit suggest that the two sides may be willing to make incremental progress. In a joint press release issued after the first day of talks, both administrations said they had made “significant progress” on “the most pressing trade issues.” The release avoided specifics, leaving many observers to speculate.</p> <h2>Market reactions: a deeper look</h2> <p>The S&P 500’s 1.2 % rise on Tuesday was driven largely by gains in the technology and industrial sectors. Shares of companies such as Apple, Microsoft, and General Electric posted double‑digit gains, reflecting investors’ belief that a de‑escalation would lower supply‑chain costs and boost consumer demand. The Nasdaq, heavily weighted toward tech firms, climbed 1.5 %, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average gained 1.0 %.</p> <p>Analysts attribute the rally to a combination of Bessent’s optimistic tone and the market’s anticipation of a more stable trade environment. “When we see a high‑profile figure like Bessent say that the U.S. and China are on the same page, it reduces the risk premium that investors are pricing in,” said Jonathan Reyes, a portfolio manager at Horizon Capital. “The market is essentially saying, ‘We’re moving from a war zone to a more predictable zone.’”</p> <p>The rally also affected currency markets. The U.S. dollar fell 0.8 % against the Chinese yuan, which gained 0.6 %. The yen and euro also saw modest gains, reflecting a general shift toward risk‑neutral sentiment.</p> <h2>Chinese perspective: cautious optimism</h2> <p>While U.S. markets reacted positively, Chinese officials have been more cautious in their public statements. A spokesperson for the Ministry of Commerce said that China was “open to constructive dialogue” but remained “firm on protecting its intellectual‑property rights.” The spokesperson added that China would not “accept any unilateral changes that undermine its national interests.”⁷</p> <p>Experts in China’s trade policy note that the government has been preparing for a “soft landing” in its economy, especially after the pandemic‑related slowdown. “China’s leadership is aware that a prolonged trade war could hurt its manufacturing sector and consumer spending,” said Li Wei, a professor of international economics at Tsinghua University. “However, the Chinese government also wants to maintain its strategic autonomy, so any concessions will be carefully calibrated.”⁸</p> <h2>The broader economic implications</h2> <p>If the U.S. and China move toward a comprehensive trade agreement, the implications could be far‑reaching. A reduction in tariffs would lower the cost of imported goods for U.S. consumers and businesses, potentially boosting domestic demand. It could also encourage U.S. companies to invest more in China, as the risk of sudden tariff hikes would be reduced.</p> <p>On the other hand, a de‑escalation could shift the balance of global supply chains. Chinese manufacturers, which have benefited from lower U.S. tariffs, might face increased competition from U.S. firms. Some analysts warn that a sudden shift could lead to a “race to the bottom” in labor and environmental standards as companies scramble to cut costs.⁹</p> <p>The trade war has also affected global markets. The World Bank’s 2021 forecast for global growth was revised downward by 0.3 percentage points due to the dispute. A de‑escalation could help restore confidence and lift growth forecasts.10</p> <h2>Looking ahead: what to watch</h2> <p>Investors and policymakers will monitor several key indicators in the coming weeks:</p> <ol> <li>Official statements from the Trump‑Xi summit—any concrete commitments on tariff reductions or intellectual‑property reforms will be a major catalyst for market sentiment.</li> <li>Bessent’s policy briefings—his subsequent statements could signal the U.S. government’s next steps.</li> <li>Chinese trade data—the volume of U.S. goods purchased by China in the next quarter will indicate whether the Phase One deal’s targets are being met.</li> <li>Global supply‑chain metrics—changes in shipping costs, inventory levels, or production schedules could reflect the trade environment’s evolution.</li> </ol> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The stock market’s rally after Bessent’s comments underscores the high stakes of U.S.–China trade relations. While the U.S. and China have not yet signed a comprehensive agreement, the signals from the Trump‑Xi summit and Bessent’s optimistic tone suggest that the trade war may be winding down. For investors, the key will be to monitor how the two superpowers translate verbal commitments into concrete policy changes. If the U.S. and China can move from rhetoric to action, the benefits could ripple across global markets, easing uncertainty and fostering a more stable economic environment.</p> <h2>Footnotes</h2> <ol> <li>U.S. Trade Representative, “Tariff Schedule for 2019,” 2020.</li> <li>“Phase One Trade Agreement,” U.S. Trade Representative, 15 Jan 2020.</li> <li>“Biden Administration Trade Policy,” Congressional Research Service, 2021.</li> <li>CNBC, “Bessent on U.S. Trade Outlook,” 2024‑05‑15.</li> <li>Global Trade Insights, “Market Reaction to Bessent’s Statement,” 2024‑05‑16.</li> <li>Office of International Affairs, Treasury Department, “Trade Strategy Briefing,” 2024‑05‑10.</li> <li>Ministry of Commerce, China, “Statement on Trade Talks,” 2024‑05‑17.</li> <li>Li Wei, “China’s Strategic Autonomy in Trade,” <em>Journal of International Economics</em>, 2023.</li> <li>International Labour Organization, “Impact of Trade Wars on Labor Standards,” 2022.</li> <li>World Bank, “Global Economic Prospects,” 2021.</li> </ol>
