Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

By Yahoo Finance · Sep 19, 2026
When Treasury official Matthew Bessent suggested that talks with China had been “successful” just days before the Trump‑Xi summit, the S&P 500 and Nasdaq jumped, and technology and manufacturing stocks gained noticeably. The rally came without any formal confirmation from Washington or Beijing, showing how quickly markets can turn diplomatic chatter into equity moves even when the underlying story remains unverified. The episode raises questions about how investors weigh hope against hype amid a trade relationship that has evolved over the past decade.

What happened

On the day Bessent’s remark surfaced, U.S. equity markets opened higher. The S&P 500 advanced, and the Nasdaq followed suit. Trading volume spiked, especially in sectors tied to U.S.–China trade, such as technology and manufacturing. Analysts noted that the market’s reaction seemed driven more by the tone of the statement than by any concrete policy change. The comment was reported by several major outlets—Reuters, Bloomberg, AP News, The New York Times, and the Wall Street Journal—all of which described Bessent as a Treasury official who had been involved in the trade negotiations leading up to the phase‑one agreement [2].

Bessent’s comment appeared amid a week of heightened speculation about the upcoming Trump‑Xi summit, scheduled for the end of the month. Although no official confirmation had been issued, the markets were primed for any signal that might indicate a thaw in the trade dispute. The rally was short‑lived; later in the session the S&P 500 moderated and closed within a few points of its pre‑announcement level, underscoring the speculative nature of the move.

Background and context

The U.S.–China trade war began in 2018 under the Trump administration, when the United States imposed 25 % tariffs on $34 bn of Chinese goods and China retaliated with similar measures. Over the next two years, the U.S. imposed tariffs on an additional $300 bn of Chinese imports, covering a broad range of products from electronics to agricultural goods. In 2020, the parties signed a “phase‑one” trade agreement that lifted some tariffs and set a framework for future negotiations, but many key issues—technology transfer, intellectual property, and market access—remained unresolved.

The current tariff landscape remains uneven. While the U.S. has reduced or eliminated tariffs on certain categories, it still levies significant duties on high‑tech components and agricultural products, and it has imposed sanctions on Chinese technology firms such as Huawei and ZTE. China, in turn, has maintained tariffs on a range of U.S. goods, including soybeans, dairy, and automobiles. These ongoing disputes over technology, agriculture, and services continue to shape the trade relationship and influence market expectations.

The upcoming Trump‑Xi summit is scheduled for the last week of the month, with a focus on trade, technology, and geopolitical issues. Analysts note that the meeting could either signal a significant shift toward cooperation or reinforce the status quo, depending on the outcomes of the negotiations. The markets have been watching for any indication that the U.S. might ease tariffs or that China might open its markets further, both of which could have immediate implications for corporate earnings and investor sentiment.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Bessent’s role as a Treasury official—responsible for overseeing U.S. economic policy—grants him a degree of credibility, yet his statement remains unverified by any official source. The lack of corroboration has led some market participants to treat the claim as a rumor, while others have interpreted it as a sign of progress. The divergent reactions highlight the tension between risk‑averse investors, who prefer clear policy signals, and those who are willing to bet on speculative developments.

From a policy standpoint, the statement underscores the delicate balance between diplomatic messaging and market expectations. The U.S. government has historically been cautious about releasing trade policy information prematurely, as such disclosures can influence market behavior and potentially affect negotiation dynamics. Meanwhile, Chinese officials have not publicly responded to the claim, leaving the veracity of Bessent’s assertion in doubt.

Historically, U.S. equity markets have reacted strongly to trade news. In 2019 and 2020, for example, the announcement of the phase‑one agreement triggered a rally of several percentage points across major indices, as investors anticipated a reduction in tariff costs and a slowdown in trade tensions. The current rally differs in that it is based on an unverified statement rather than a formal policy change, raising questions about the sustainability of such gains.

The market’s willingness to price in speculation also reflects broader trends in investor behavior. With low interest rates and a search for yield, investors have increasingly turned to equities for returns, making them more sensitive to any news that could alter the cost of doing business. This dynamic amplifies the impact of even minor signals, as seen in the rapid advance of the S&P 500 following Bessent’s comment.

What to watch (future implications)

The outcome of the Trump‑Xi summit will be a key factor in determining whether the market rally can be sustained. If the leaders reach a substantive agreement that reduces tariffs or expands market access, the positive sentiment could translate into a longer‑term rally. Conversely, if the talks stall or result in a limited concession, the market may correct, as the rally was largely speculative.

Investors should monitor the U.S. Treasury’s subsequent communications for any clarification or confirmation of Bessent’s statement. A formal announcement confirming progress could validate the market’s expectations, while a lack of confirmation could reinforce uncertainty. Additionally, any policy changes announced by the Biden administration—such as adjustments to tariff rates or new trade initiatives—will likely influence market dynamics, given the ongoing sensitivity to trade policy.

From a broader perspective, the episode highlights the importance of transparency in trade negotiations. The rapid market reaction to an unverified claim underscores how easily speculation can shape financial outcomes. Policymakers may need to consider the implications of public statements by officials, especially when those statements can influence market expectations and corporate valuations.

In the long term, the U.S.–China trade relationship will continue to evolve as both countries grapple with issues such as technology competition, supply‑chain resilience, and geopolitical tensions. The markets will remain attuned to any signals that suggest a shift toward cooperation or further confrontation. For now, the rally sparked by Bessent’s remark serves as a reminder of the delicate interplay between diplomacy, policy, and market sentiment in a complex global trade environment.
