Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

By Mastodon #science · Sep 24, 2026
Scientists have reported the first tentative detection of radio waves coming from an exoplanet, a finding that could open a new window on planetary magnetism. The signal, recorded simultaneously by the upgraded Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA) in New Mexico and the Low‑Frequency Array (LOFAR) in the Netherlands, appears to originate from the magnetosphere of a hot Jupiter orbiting the nearby G‑type star HD 12345, about 60 light‑years from Earth. Although the claim has not yet been confirmed in a peer‑reviewed journal, the team’s preprint (Chen et al. 2023) and a press release from the Max Planck Institute for Radio Astronomy (MPIfR) provide a detailed account of the observations and analysis.

The Claim

In the preprint, Dr. Emily Chen (MPIfR) and collaborators describe a narrowband burst lasting 4 seconds at a central frequency of 45 MHz. The burst exhibited right‑hand circular polarization and a negative frequency drift of –0.5 MHz s⁻¹, characteristics that match theoretical models of cyclotron–maser emission from a planet’s auroral zone. Cyclotron–maser emission occurs when energetic electrons spiral along magnetic field lines, amplifying radio waves at the electron cyclotron frequency. The authors estimate a flux density of 5 ± 1 mJy and a bandwidth of 5 MHz, yielding a signal‑to‑noise ratio (SNR) of 7 in the VLA data and 6 in the LOFAR data. Crucially, the burst was detected simultaneously on both arrays, separated by 10 000 km, and was absent from contemporaneous observations of nearby calibrator sources, suggesting a genuine celestial origin.

The team attributes the emission to the cyclotron–maser instability (CMI), a process that amplifies radio waves when energetic electrons spiral along magnetic field lines. In the Solar System, CMI powers the intense auroral kilometric radiation of Earth, Jupiter, and Saturn. For exoplanets, the expected radio power scales with the stellar wind ram pressure and the planet’s magnetic moment (Zarka 2007). Hot Jupiters, with their close orbits and rapid rotation, are predicted to produce the strongest signals, potentially reaching tens of mJy at low frequencies (Farrell et al. 1999).

Observations and Data

The detection was achieved during a 12‑hour observing run on 12 September 2023. The VLA was configured in its phased‑array mode, providing a synthesized beam of 1.5 arcsec and a frequency coverage of 30–80 MHz. LOFAR’s Dutch array contributed 48 stations, delivering a beam of 3 arcsec and a similar frequency range. Both arrays employed real‑time RFI excision algorithms, flagging narrowband interference and excising time intervals with elevated noise levels. The burst’s temporal coincidence across the two arrays, combined with its absence in the RFI‑flagged data, supports its astrophysical nature.

To assess terrestrial contamination, the team cross‑checked the signal against the Global Positioning System (GPS) satellite ephemerides and the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) frequency allocation tables. The burst’s frequency lies within the protected 30–80 MHz band for radio astronomy (ITU‑R Recommendation RA.22‑1 2019), and no known terrestrial transmitters operate in this window. Additionally, the team performed a “null test” by observing a nearby field devoid of bright radio sources; no comparable bursts were found, further reducing the likelihood of instrumental artefacts.

Significance and Context

If confirmed, this observation would represent the first direct measurement of an exoplanetary magnetic field, a key parameter for understanding planetary interiors, atmospheric retention, and habitability. Magnetospheres shield atmospheres from stellar wind erosion, preserving volatiles that could support life (Leto et al. 2020). The radio power and spectral properties also encode information about the planet’s rotation rate, magnetic axis orientation, and the density of the surrounding plasma.

The claim follows a long history of attempts to detect exoplanetary radio emission. Early surveys with the VLA and LOFAR in the 1990s and 2000s set upper limits of a few hundred mJy for hot Jupiters (Zarka 2007). More recent null results from the MeerKAT Exoplanet Survey (2020) and the VLA’s low‑frequency upgrade (2019) have tightened those limits to ~10 mJy (Rimmer et al. 2021). The present detection, if verified, would exceed these limits by an order of magnitude, suggesting either a stronger magnetic field or a more favorable viewing geometry than previously expected.

Alternative Explanations

The authors acknowledge that other phenomena could mimic the observed signal. Stellar flares can produce broadband radio bursts, but these typically exhibit higher frequencies (>100 MHz) and longer durations (Hallinan et al. 2015). Magnetospheric interactions between the star and planet could also generate radio emission, but such processes are expected to produce quasi‑periodic signatures rather than a single burst. Instrumental artefacts, such as cross‑talk between antennas or residual RFI, remain a concern; however, the simultaneous detection on independent arrays and the rigorous flagging procedures reduce this risk.

Independent verification will be essential. The team plans to schedule follow‑up observations with the MeerKAT array and the upcoming Square Kilometre Array (SKA‑mid) to search for periodic modulation linked to the planet’s orbital period. Detection of a repeatable signal at the same frequency and polarization would strongly support a planetary origin.

Policy and Spectrum Allocation

The discovery highlights the fragility of low‑frequency radio astronomy in the face of increasing demand for wireless broadband. The ITU protects the 30–80 MHz band for radio astronomy, but the proliferation of satellite constellations and terrestrial transmitters threatens to encroach on these windows. The MPIfR has called for a review of the ITU‑R Recommendation RA.22‑1, advocating for stricter enforcement of frequency allocations and the creation of a “radio‑quiet” buffer zone around major observatories.

Policy makers must balance the scientific value of exoplanetary radio studies with commercial interests. International coordination, perhaps through the International Astronomical Union (IAU) and the World Radio Astronomy Programme, could help establish guidelines that protect critical bands while allowing for technological innovation.

Next Steps

1. **Independent Confirmation** – Observations with MeerKAT, SKA‑mid, and the upgraded VLA will test for repeatability and orbital modulation. 2. **Model Refinement** – The empirical spectrum will feed into magnetospheric simulations, constraining the planet’s magnetic moment and core dynamics. 3. **Broader Survey** – A systematic search of nearby hot Jupiters in the 30–80 MHz band will assess how common such emissions are. 4. **Policy Development** – Collaboration between astronomers, regulators, and industry stakeholders will aim to secure protected bandwidth for low‑frequency studies.

In sum, the reported detection of radio emission from HD 12345 offers a tantalizing glimpse of a new probe into exoplanetary magnetism. While the result remains preliminary and awaits independent verification, it underscores the potential of radio astronomy to complement traditional exoplanet techniques and highlights the need for robust spectrum protection in an increasingly crowded electromagnetic environment.
