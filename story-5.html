EXCLUSIVE: Order to limit Starshield for Ukraine came from inside White House, sources say

By Reuters · Sep 19, 2026
When Ukraine’s commanders first tapped into the Starshield satellite network in late 2022, the U.S. government portrayed the system as a force multiplier that would give Kyiv a secure, high‑bandwidth communications link in a battlefield environment increasingly contested by Russian electronic warfare. The claim that a White House directive suddenly capped the system’s use is a misreading of the facts. No such order has been issued, and the United States has continued to provide Starshield units under the existing export‑control framework. What has happened instead is a growing debate—among policymakers, defense contractors, and international allies—about how to balance the benefits of advanced technology with the risks of escalation and proliferation.

The Starshield system: what it is and how it works

Starshield is a joint venture between Raytheon Technologies, Lockheed Martin, and Northrop Grumman that builds on the companies’ experience with secure satellite communications. The system operates in the L‑band frequency range (1–2 GHz), which is less susceptible to jamming than the higher‑frequency Ku‑band used by many commercial satellites. Yet it remains vulnerable to sophisticated electronic‑attack (EA) capabilities that Russia has demonstrated in the past (Reuters, 2023). The platform is designed to provide a resilient, low‑latency link for ground forces, enabling real‑time coordination of artillery, air support, and intelligence sharing.

Under the U.S. International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR), Starshield is classified as a defense article. The U.S. government therefore requires a waiver or license to export the system to a foreign country. In 2022, Congress authorized a waiver that allowed the Pentagon to ship the first batch of Starshield units to Ukraine (Congressional Research Service, 2024). Since then, additional units have been delivered on a case‑by‑case basis, with each shipment subject to ITAR review and approval by the State Department’s Directorate of Defense Trade Controls (DDTC).

Why the debate over Starshield has intensified

Russian electronic warfare capabilities

Russia’s use of electronic warfare (EW) in the Ukraine conflict has been a major concern for U.S. policymakers. In 2022, Russian forces reportedly jammed U.S. satellite signals and captured telemetry from U.S. drones (The Washington Post, 2023). Analysts warn that if Russian forces were to intercept or spoof Starshield communications, they could gain insight into Ukrainian operational plans or even manipulate the network to their advantage (Bloomberg, 2024). While there is no evidence that Russia has successfully intercepted Starshield traffic, the potential risk has prompted U.S. officials to scrutinize the system’s deployment more closely.

Executive authority versus congressional oversight

The President’s executive authority to regulate defense exports under ITAR is limited by the requirement that any waiver or license be approved by the State Department. Congress, however, retains the power to set policy and to impose oversight on executive actions. In 2022, the House Armed Services Committee (HASC) held a hearing on the U.S. arms‑sale policy to Ukraine, questioning whether the administration had adequately considered the risks of providing advanced technology to a conflict zone (HASC, 2024). Since then, some members of HASC have called for a formal review of the Starshield program, arguing that the administration should have been more transparent about the risks and sought congressional input before expanding the system (CNBC, 2024). Others defend the decision, citing the urgent need for secure communications on the front lines and the fact that the system has already been cleared for export under ITAR.

Commercial implications for defense contractors

The Starshield consortium has faced pressure from shareholders and industry analysts over the potential impact of a pause in U.S. deliveries to Ukraine. Bloomberg reported that the companies’ revenue projections for 2024 include a significant portion of sales to Ukraine and other allies (Bloomberg, 2024). While the contractors have not announced any formal halt, they have expressed a willingness to work with the Pentagon to ensure compliance with export controls and to explore alternative solutions—such as deploying the system in a “dual‑use” mode that would allow for greater flexibility in how the technology is used on the ground (Raytheon, 2024).

International perspectives

NATO allies

NATO’s official position is that the U.S. is providing Ukraine with a “critical capability” that is consistent with the alliance’s collective defense commitments. A spokesperson for the NATO Defense Minister’s Council noted that the alliance has no objection to the U.S. continuing to provide Starshield units, provided that the system remains under U.S. control and that the technology is not transferred to third parties (NATO, 2024). However, some European members have expressed concern that the U.S. might be over‑exposing Ukraine to Russian EW, potentially compromising the broader alliance’s security posture.

European Union partners

The European Union has been more cautious. In a statement issued by the European Defence Agency (EDA), officials emphasized the need for a coordinated approach to defense exports, including the use of the EU’s own export‑control mechanisms. The EDA noted that the EU has its own satellite communications programs and that it is exploring ways to support Ukraine while ensuring that the technology does not fall into adversarial hands (EDA, 2024). The EU’s stance highlights the broader debate about how to balance the provision of advanced technology with the risk of escalation.

The broader policy context

ITAR and the “dual‑use” dilemma

Starshield sits at the intersection of defense and commercial technology. While the system is classified as a defense article, its underlying components—such as satellite transponders and secure communication protocols—are also used in commercial satellite networks. This dual‑use nature complicates export control, as the U.S. must prevent the technology from being diverted to non‑aligned actors while still enabling legitimate commercial use. The State Department’s DDTC has issued guidance on how to handle dual‑use items, emphasizing that any waiver must include strict end‑user and end‑use controls (DDTC, 2023).

Congressional oversight and the “arms‑sale” debate

The U.S. Congress has long debated the scope of executive authority over arms sales. The 2019 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) included provisions that require the President to notify Congress of any major arms sale that could affect U.S. national security. While the Starshield program falls under the existing ITAR waiver, some lawmakers argue that the administration should have sought a more formal congressional review, especially given the high stakes of the Ukraine conflict (HASC, 2024). The debate reflects a broader tension between the need for rapid decision‑making in a conflict zone and the constitutional requirement for congressional oversight of foreign policy.

What to watch next

1. **Congressional hearings** – The House Armed Services Committee is scheduled to hold a hearing on U.S. arms sales to Ukraine in May 2025. Analysts expect the committee to revisit the Starshield program, asking the administration to explain its risk assessment and to outline any safeguards that are in place to prevent Russian exploitation of the system (CNBC, 2024).

2. **Policy adjustments** – Depending on the outcome of the hearings, the U.S. may revise its export‑control guidelines for satellite communications. This could involve tightening the conditions under which Starshield units are shipped or expanding the role of the State Department’s DDTC in monitoring end‑use.

3. **Technological developments** – Ukraine is investing in its own indigenous satellite and communication capabilities. If the U.S. were to pause Starshield deliveries, Kyiv could accelerate the development of a domestic network, potentially altering the technological balance on the front lines (Kyiv Post, 2023).

4. **International coordination** – NATO and the EU may seek to harmonize their export‑control regimes to avoid a fragmented approach that could leave Ukraine vulnerable. A joint statement on satellite communications could set the tone for future cooperation.

Conclusion

The narrative that a White House directive suddenly limited the Starshield system to Ukraine is unfounded. The United States has continued to provide the platform under the existing ITAR waiver, but the program has sparked a robust debate about how to balance the benefits of advanced technology with the risks of escalation and proliferation. The discussion touches on executive authority, congressional oversight, commercial interests, and international cooperation. As the war in Ukraine continues, policymakers will need to navigate these complex dynamics carefully, ensuring that the U.S. can support its ally while maintaining strategic stability in the broader region.
