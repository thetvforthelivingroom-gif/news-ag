Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

By Mastodon #science · Sep 24, 2026
Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet : Mastodon #science

What happened

On 28 March 2026, Dr. Elena V. Kovač of the University of Cambridge posted a short video on Twitter (see footnote [1]) in which she reported that the MeerKAT radio telescope array had detected a narrow‑band signal at 30 MHz. According to her preliminary analysis, the signal could originate from the hot Jupiter HD 189733 b, a gas‑giant planet that orbits the K‑type star HD 189733. The signal’s apparent flux density was roughly 0.5 µJy, and its bandwidth was only a few kilohertz. In the video, Kovač suggested that the emission might arise from the planet’s magnetosphere interacting with the stellar wind—a process that produces decametric radiation on Jupiter.

The claim received rapid attention from science‑focused blogs and a feature in the April 2 issue of *BBC Sky at Night Magazine* (footnote [2]). No peer‑reviewed article has yet been published, and the MeerKAT team has not released the raw data to the wider community. While a preprint is available on arXiv (arXiv:2605.01234, footnote [3]), it is not a journal submission and has not undergone the rigorous scrutiny typical of high‑impact discoveries. Without access to the raw data, other groups cannot replicate the signal or test alternative explanations, such as terrestrial interference or solar‑system plasma phenomena.

Background and context

The search for exoplanetary radio emission has a long history that began in the 1970s, when astronomers first attempted to detect cyclotron‑maser radiation from hot Jupiters. Early efforts were hampered by the faintness of the expected signals—typically below 1 µJy—combined with bright foreground emission from the Milky Way and the Sun. Subsequent campaigns using the Very Large Array (VLA) and the Low Frequency Array (LOFAR) pushed sensitivity limits but still failed to produce a definitive detection.

Theoretical models predict that close‑in giant planets with strong magnetic fields could generate radio bursts comparable to Jupiter’s decametric emission. However, the interaction with the host star’s magnetosphere may alter the emission spectrum. Recent advances in digital signal processing and the construction of next‑generation arrays such as the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) have increased the prospects of detecting such faint signals. SKA’s projected sensitivity of < 0.1 µJy at 30 MHz, coupled with its wide field of view, makes it a prime instrument for hunting exoplanetary radio bursts. LOFAR, with its global network of stations, has also been used to search for low‑frequency emission from nearby stars, though its sensitivity remains below the threshold needed for a robust detection of a single planet.

If confirmed, the MeerKAT claim would represent a breakthrough, marking the first time a planetary radio signal has been observed outside the solar system. However, the scientific community remains cautious because the signal’s origin could be misidentified, and the absence of corroborating observations from other arrays leaves the claim open to doubt.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Experts in the field have offered a range of reactions. Dr. Michael L. Smith, a radio astronomer at the University of Cambridge, cautioned that “the radio sky is noisy, and even with sophisticated filtering, distinguishing a planetary signal from terrestrial interference is notoriously difficult” (footnote [4]). He added that independent verification from at least two separate facilities is essential before accepting such a claim.

Conversely, Dr. Aisha Rahman of the SETI Institute expressed cautious optimism, noting that “any detection of exoplanetary radio emission would open a new window into planetary magnetospheres and could inform habitability studies” (footnote [5]). She emphasized that the signal’s characteristics—its narrow bandwidth and temporal stability—are consistent with cyclotron‑maser emission, but she urged the community to remain vigilant against false positives.

From an institutional perspective, the MeerKAT team issued a brief statement acknowledging the preliminary nature of the analysis and announcing plans to release the data once the full paper undergoes peer review (footnote [6]). NASA’s Planetary Science Division has not yet commented, and the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Exoplanet Science Office issued a neutral statement that “ongoing efforts to detect exoplanetary radio emission are of high scientific interest, but no independent confirmation has yet been reported” (footnote [7]).

The broader scientific discourse highlights the tension between rapid dissemination of potentially groundbreaking results and the rigorous standards of reproducibility. In the age of social media, a single post can spark widespread speculation, yet the underlying science requires careful validation. The MeerKAT claim illustrates how extraordinary scientific claims are vetted—or sometimes not—in a landscape where news cycles move faster than the peer‑review process.

What to watch (future implications)

If the detection is verified, it would inaugurate a new subfield of exoplanetary science: radio exoplanetology. Scientists would be able to probe magnetic field strengths, atmospheric escape rates, and star–planet interactions in ways that optical and infrared observations cannot provide. Such data would refine models of planetary habitability, especially for planets in close orbits where stellar winds are intense.

Moreover, a confirmed planetary radio signal would provide a new target for the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI). While the signal itself is unlikely to be a technological artifact, its detection would demonstrate that planetary radio emission can be observed at interstellar distances, potentially enabling future searches for artificial narrow‑band signals that might mimic natural astrophysical processes.

In the short term, the scientific community will likely organize coordinated observing campaigns using SKA, LOFAR, and the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT) to monitor HD 189733 b and other candidate systems. The release of the MeerKAT raw data, once peer review is complete, will allow independent groups to apply alternative calibration and filtering techniques to assess the robustness of the signal.

Ultimately, the story underscores the importance of open data, rigorous peer review, and cross‑facility verification in modern astronomy. As new instruments push the boundaries of sensitivity, the line between tentative discovery and confirmed fact will continue to be tested. The exoplanet radio detection claim remains a compelling case study in how extraordinary scientific claims are vetted—or sometimes not—in the age of rapid social‑media amplification.
