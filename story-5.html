Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

By Mastodon #science · Sep 24, 2026
<article> The hunt for radio signals from planets beyond our solar system has long captivated astronomers. Theoretical work by Zarka and colleagues [1] suggested that magnetised exoplanets should emit auroral radio bursts at low frequencies, similar to Jupiter’s powerful decametric radiation. Yet, despite decades of effort, no definitive detection has appeared in a peer‑reviewed journal. In March 2026, a preprint by Martinez et al. [4] and a press release from the University of Cambridge’s Institute of Astronomy [5] announced the first possible detection of a radio signal that could originate from the hot Jupiter Kappa Pegasi b. Although the claim has not yet undergone formal peer review, it has sparked excitement and debate across the exoplanetary and radio‑astronomy communities. Kappa Pegasi b orbits a G‑type star (Kappa Pegasi A) at a distance of only 25 light‑years. The planet completes a 4.3‑day orbit at a semi‑major axis of 0.05 AU and has a mass of roughly 1.5 M_J. Its close proximity to its host star makes it a prime candidate for strong star‑planet interactions and, potentially, a powerful magnetosphere. Martinez et al. [4] report that a 12‑hour observation with the Low‑Frequency Array (LOFAR) core, comprising 48 stations, revealed a narrowband feature between 30 and 80 MHz. The signal exhibited a peak flux density of 2.3 mJy, a circular polarisation fraction of 10 %, and a periodicity matching the planet’s orbital phase to within 0.02 cycles. The authors estimate a signal‑to‑noise ratio of 6.5 and a statistical significance of 3.2 σ after accounting for the number of independent trials in the frequency–time–polarisation space. The data reduction followed the standard LOFAR pipeline described in the 2025 Technical Handbook [6]. Calibration used the bright calibrator 3C48, and the final image cube was produced with a 10‑second time resolution and 0.5‑MHz frequency resolution. The team performed a rigorous search for terrestrial interference by cross‑matching the timestamps with the Global Positioning System (GPS) time‑stamped RFI database, finding no coincident spikes. Nonetheless, the authors caution that stellar activity, such as flares from Kappa Pegasi A, could produce low‑frequency bursts that mimic planetary signatures, and they note that the signal’s narrow bandwidth is also a characteristic of some ionospheric artefacts. To enable independent verification, Martinez et al. [4] have deposited the calibrated visibilities and the derived image cube in the LOFAR Long‑Term Archive (LTA) under project ID LTA‑2026‑KappaPeg. The raw data are publicly available at https://www.lofar.org/lta, and the preprint includes a DOI link to the LTA dataset. The Cambridge press release [5] highlighted this open‑data policy, emphasizing the importance of transparency in a field where confirmation is notoriously difficult. Since the announcement, several groups have attempted to replicate the detection. The Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA) observed Kappa Pegasi b in its L‑band (1–2 GHz) configuration for 8 hours, achieving a 5‑µJy sensitivity [8]. No emission was detected above a 3‑σ threshold, consistent with the expectation that any planetary auroral signal would be strongest at low frequencies. The Murchison Widefield Array (MWA) carried out a 24‑hour observation at 80–300 MHz, reaching a 10‑mJy sensitivity [9], but likewise found no significant signal. These null results do not rule out the LOFAR detection, given the different frequency ranges, integration times, and instrumental sensitivities, but they underscore the need for coordinated, multi‑epoch campaigns. The forthcoming Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will provide an unprecedented combination of sensitivity and frequency coverage. The SKA‑Low component, slated to begin operations in the early 2030s, will probe the 50–350 MHz band with an order‑of‑magnitude improvement in collecting area over LOFAR. Meanwhile, the SKA‑Mid array will extend coverage to 0.35–14.5 GHz, enabling simultaneous searches for both planetary auroral emission and stellar flares. The SKA Science Case [7] explicitly lists exoplanet radio emission as a key science driver, and a dedicated observing program is already in the proposal stage. From a planetary‑science perspective, a confirmed detection would provide the first direct measurement of an exoplanet’s magnetic field strength and topology. Magnetohydrodynamic models suggest that a field of several gauss could shield a hot Jupiter’s atmosphere from intense stellar wind erosion, potentially preserving atmospheric volatiles over geological timescales [2]. Moreover, the polarisation and spectral shape of the radio burst could reveal the presence of ionised species in the planet’s upper atmosphere, offering a new probe of exoplanetary weather and chemistry. For the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence (SETI), the implications are more subtle. While the signal reported by Martinez et al. [4] appears consistent with natural auroral processes, the mere ability to detect low‑frequency radio emission from distant planets opens a new observational window. Future surveys could search for anomalous periodicities, narrowband spikes, or other signatures that deviate from known planetary physics, thereby refining the criteria for technosignature detection. The Cambridge press release [5] noted that the data will be made available to the SETI community, encouraging cross‑disciplinary collaboration. The claim also raises important questions about data‑sharing protocols and the handling of potentially transformative discoveries. The International Astronomical Union (IAU) has recently updated its guidelines to require that any claim of a new class of astrophysical phenomenon be accompanied by open access to raw data and analysis. This move reflects a broader trend toward transparency and reproducibility in astronomy, ensuring that extraordinary claims can be scrutinised and confirmed by independent teams.
