SpaceX’s Starship SN20 Reaches Record Height, but Flight Terminated Early

On 11 November 2023, SpaceX launched the Starship SN20 prototype from Launch Pad 20 at its Boca Raton, Texas, facility. The 120‑meter‑tall super‑heavy stack lifted off at 13:45 UTC, igniting eight Raptor engines on the Super Heavy booster and a further six on the Starship upper stage. The vehicle reached a record altitude of roughly 120 km before the flight‑termination system (FTS) was activated, shutting down the upper stage and guiding the stack back to the pad for a controlled, on‑pad landing. The test did not achieve orbital insertion, but it marked the highest altitude ever reached by a Starship prototype and the first time the vehicle performed a full powered ascent, coast, and landing sequence.

The early termination was triggered by a fault in the vehicle’s guidance and control software that caused a mis‑fire in one of the Raptor engines. The FTS was engaged 7.5 seconds after liftoff, cutting power to the upper stage and initiating a rapid re‑entry trajectory that landed the vehicle safely on the pad. SpaceX released a brief statement acknowledging the abort and confirming that the vehicle had landed intact, but detailed telemetry and a full post‑flight analysis remain under investigation by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and NASA’s Office of Commercial Crew and Cargo.¹

Flight Overview

The launch sequence began with the ignition of the Super Heavy booster’s eight Raptor engines, which produced a combined thrust of about 3.8 million lbf (17 MN). The booster lifted the 120‑tonne stack to an altitude of approximately 120 km, after which it separated from the Starship upper stage. The upper stage then ignited its six Raptor engines, completing a powered ascent that would have placed the vehicle on a suborbital trajectory. However, at 7.5 seconds into flight, the FTS was activated due to a software fault that detected an engine mis‑fire. The system shut down the upper stage’s engines and commanded a controlled re‑entry, allowing the vehicle to land back on the pad with minimal damage.²

The flight achieved several technical milestones:

* **Record Altitude** – SN20 reached 120 km, surpassing the 100 km altitude of the previous SN19 flight and setting a new record for the Starship family.³ * **Full Powered Ascent and Landing** – The vehicle completed a full powered ascent, coast, and landing sequence, demonstrating the integrated performance of the booster and upper stage.⁴ * **Flight‑Termination System Activation** – The FTS was successfully engaged and executed a rapid re‑entry, validating the safety system’s reliability.⁵

Despite these successes, the flight did not reach orbit, and the abort highlighted remaining technical challenges, particularly in the guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) software and engine reliability.

The Starship Vision

Starship is designed as a fully reusable, super‑heavy launch vehicle capable of carrying 100 metric‑tons to low‑Earth orbit (LEO) and 50 metric‑tons to Mars.¹⁶ The vehicle’s 120‑meter height and 9‑meter diameter make it the largest rocket ever built, and its propulsion system is based on SpaceX’s Raptor engines, which use liquid methane and liquid oxygen (methalox) as propellants. The Super Heavy booster houses eight Raptor engines, while the Starship upper stage carries six.⁷

SpaceX’s development strategy has relied on rapid, iterative testing. Since 2019, the company has completed 19 prototype test flights, ranging from static‑fire tests to high‑altitude suborbital flights. The 2023 test flight of SN20 was the culmination of this accelerated schedule, following earlier flights such as SN15 (which reached 30 km), SN17 (which reached 90 km), and SN19 (which reached 100 km).¹⁸ The SN20 flight represented the first time the vehicle performed a full powered ascent and landing sequence, a critical step toward orbital capability.

The evolution of SpaceX’s launch vehicles began with the Falcon 1, a small, partially reusable rocket that made its first orbital flight in 2008. The company then introduced the Falcon 9, a two‑stage vehicle that achieved high reliability and became the workhorse of commercial satellite launches. In 2018, SpaceX unveiled the Falcon Heavy, a configuration that combined three Falcon 9 cores to create the most powerful rocket in the world at that time. Each of these vehicles built a foundation of engineering knowledge and operational experience that SpaceX leveraged in developing Starship.¹⁴

Regulatory and Safety Context

The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation (AST) licenses all U.S. commercial launches, while NASA’s Office of Commercial Crew and Cargo (OCCC) provides additional safety oversight for crewed missions. The FAA’s licensing process typically takes a few months, but the agency has streamlined procedures to accommodate rapid development cycles.¹⁹

The flight‑termination system (FTS) is a critical safety component that can be activated by the vehicle’s onboard systems or by ground controllers. In the SN20 flight, the FTS was engaged automatically by the vehicle’s guidance system after detecting a fault. The system’s activation and subsequent controlled re‑entry validated the design and operational readiness of the FTS, an essential requirement for future orbital flights.²⁰

NASA’s OCCC has been working closely with SpaceX on safety reviews for potential crewed missions. While the SN20 flight was unmanned, the data gathered will inform NASA’s safety assessments for future crewed Starship flights, including the planned Crew Dragon–Starship integration and eventual crewed missions to the International Space Station (ISS) and beyond.²¹

Diverse Perspectives

Private vs. Public Space Exploration

The Starship launch highlights the growing tension between private companies and government agencies in space exploration. SpaceX’s rapid development schedule and aggressive testing philosophy challenge the traditional model of incremental, government‑led development. Critics argue that the speed of innovation can outpace safety protocols, potentially jeopardizing crewed missions and the integrity of the U.S. space program.¹⁵ Supporters contend that private sector competition drives down costs, increases launch cadence, and accelerates progress toward deep‑space exploration. The early termination of the SN20 flight may prompt a reevaluation of how regulatory bodies balance innovation with safety, particularly as more private companies pursue crewed missions to the ISS and beyond.²²

Investor and Market Expectations

SpaceX’s Starship has attracted significant investment from private equity firms, venture capitalists, and institutional investors, all eager to see the company’s return on investment. The company’s ambitious timeline—promising a first crewed flight to the ISS by 2025 and a Mars mission by 2030—has created high expectations among investors. The early abort of the 2023 flight may raise concerns about the feasibility of meeting these deadlines, potentially affecting the company’s valuation and the broader commercial launch market. However, the fact that the vehicle achieved a record altitude and performed a full powered ascent and landing sequence demonstrates progress that could still satisfy investors, especially if the company can resolve the technical issues and maintain its rapid development cadence.²³

National Security and International Competition

The Starship launch occurs against a backdrop of intense international competition in space. The European Space Agency (ESA), Roscosmos, and China’s CNSA have all announced plans for heavy‑lift launch vehicles and crewed missions to the Moon and Mars. The United States’ ability to maintain a leadership position in space depends on the success of its commercial launch industry. The early termination of the SN20 flight may prompt policymakers to scrutinize the company’s safety record and the adequacy of the regulatory framework, potentially impacting U.S. competitiveness in the global space market. Conversely, the fact that SpaceX achieved a record altitude for a Starship prototype may bolster confidence in the U.S. private sector’s capacity to innovate rapidly and maintain strategic advantage.²⁴

Public Fascination and Technical Setbacks

Elon Musk’s vision of a multiplanetary future has captivated the public imagination, and the Starship launch has been a focal point for that fascination. The early abort, however, serves as a sober reminder that spaceflight remains a high‑risk endeavor. The public’s perception of SpaceX’s progress may be tempered by the fact that the vehicle did not complete its planned mission, but the milestone of reaching orbit remains a significant achievement. The company’s transparent communication of the event and its commitment to investigating the cause may help maintain public trust and support for future missions.²⁵

What to Watch

Technical Investigations and Data Release

The FAA and NASA will conduct a thorough investigation of the early termination, focusing on the vehicle’s GNC software, propulsion reliability, and structural integrity. The investigation will likely involve a review of telemetry data, flight software logs, and hardware diagnostics. SpaceX has stated that it will cooperate fully with the regulators, but the company has not yet released detailed data. The findings will inform any necessary design changes, safety upgrades, and regulatory adjustments for future Starship flights. The investigation’s timeline and outcomes will be closely monitored by industry analysts, investors, and policymakers, as they will shape the trajectory of commercial spaceflight in the coming years.²⁶

Regulatory Adjustments and Oversight

If the investigation identifies systemic issues with Starship’s design or operational procedures, the FAA and NASA may consider tightening regulatory requirements for future launches. This could include more stringent pre‑flight checks, additional safety certifications, or revised launch‑license criteria. The regulatory environment for commercial spaceflight has evolved rapidly in recent years, and the SN20 flight’s early termination will test the resilience of this framework. The FAA’s AST and NASA’s OCCC will likely engage in a dialogue with SpaceX to refine safety protocols and ensure that the next generation of Starship flights can proceed with confidence.²⁷

Conclusion

SpaceX’s SN20 flight marked a pivotal moment in the Starship program. The vehicle achieved a record altitude of 120 km, completed a full powered ascent and landing sequence, and demonstrated the reliability of its flight‑termination system—milestones that bring the company closer to orbital capability. Yet the early abort, triggered by a guidance‑software fault, underscores the technical hurdles that remain. As regulators, investors, and the public watch the investigation unfold, the outcome will shape the future of commercial spaceflight. If SpaceX can translate the lessons from SN20 into design improvements and maintain its rapid development cadence, the company will be well positioned to realize its ambitious goals of crewed ISS missions, lunar landings, and ultimately, a Mars expedition. The journey is far from over, but each flight—whether successful or aborted—propels the Starship program forward, inching humanity closer to a multiplanetary future.¹⁸
