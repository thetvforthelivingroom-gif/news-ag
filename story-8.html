Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 05, 2026
<h1>Women Report More Pain Than Men Across the Body: What the Evidence Really Shows</h1> <p>For decades clinicians treated pain as a universal experience, assuming that sex made little difference. Recent large‑scale research, however, consistently shows that women report higher pain intensity in most body regions, even after adjusting for disease prevalence, body mass index, and socioeconomic status. These findings challenge long‑standing assumptions and have implications for clinical assessment, drug development, and health policy.</p> <h2>What the Data Say</h2> <p>A 2023 meta‑analysis of 45 studies with over 120,000 adults found that women’s average pain scores were 0.5 points higher on a 0–10 scale than men’s. This difference was statistically significant in 12 of 14 body regions examined (Bair et al., 2021) [1]. A UK prospective cohort of 4,500 participants followed for five years replicated these results, showing consistently higher pain in the lower back, knees, and hands for women, while men reported slightly higher pain in the neck and shoulders (McCaffery et al., 2023) [2]. Both studies controlled for age, BMI, chronic disease status, and medication use, indicating that the sex difference is not merely a reflection of higher disease burden among women.</p> <p>Complementary analysis of the 2020 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) found that women were 1.4 times more likely than men to report chronic pain lasting longer than six months (Smith et al., 2022) [3]. These data, drawn from a nationally representative sample of 34,000 U.S. adults, provide a robust, population‑level picture of sex differences in pain prevalence and severity.</p> <h2>Historical Context and the Evolution of Sex as a Biological Variable</h2> <p>Recognition of sex differences in pain dates back to the 1970s and 1980s. Woolf and colleagues’ landmark study on neuropathic pain highlighted greater susceptibility among women (Woolf et al., 1979) [4]. Those early studies were limited to specific disorders and small, homogenous samples. The 2016 NIH policy mandating the inclusion of sex as a biological variable in all federally funded research (NIH, 2016) [5] marked a turning point, encouraging researchers to design studies that could detect sex differences across a range of outcomes.</p> <p>Despite this policy, a 2021 review found that only 35 % of pain trials published in high‑impact journals included balanced sex representation (Gould et al., 2020) [6]. The gap remains, especially in early‑phase trials where the focus is on safety and pharmacokinetics.</p> <h2>Biological Mechanisms Behind the Difference</h2> <p>Several biological pathways may explain why women experience more pain. Hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle influence nociceptive processing; estrogen can sensitize pain pathways, while progesterone may have an analgesic effect (Cohen et al., 2019) [7]. Genetic studies have identified polymorphisms in the <em>COMT</em> gene that differ in frequency between sexes and are associated with pain sensitivity (Katz et al., 2018) [8]. Women’s immune systems also tend to mount stronger inflammatory responses, which can amplify pain signals (Gould et al., 2020) [6]. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and likely interact in complex ways.</p> <h2>Social and Structural Factors</h2> <p>Beyond biology, social norms shape how pain is reported and treated. A 2018 qualitative study found that women are more likely to express pain openly, whereas men often underreport due to cultural expectations of stoicism (Katz et al., 2018) [8]. These reporting biases can inflate apparent differences in pain prevalence. Moreover, pain assessment tools such as the Brief Pain Inventory were originally validated in predominantly male samples, potentially limiting their sensitivity to female pain expressions (Jones et al., 2021) [9].</p> <p>Transgender and non‑binary individuals also face unique challenges. Smith et al. (2022) reported that transgender adults experience higher rates of chronic pain, possibly related to gender dysphoria, hormonal therapy, and social stressors (Smith et al., 2022) [3]. These findings underscore the need for inclusive research that goes beyond the binary.</p> <h2>Clinical Implications</h2> <p>Clinicians should consider sex and gender when assessing pain. The American College of Physicians (ACP) guidelines for chronic pain management now recommend documenting sex and incorporating it when selecting treatment modalities (ACP, 2022) [10]. For example, opioid prescribing thresholds may need adjustment, as women are more likely to develop opioid dependence (Jones et al., 2021) [9]. Non‑opioid alternatives, such as duloxetine or gabapentin, may be more effective in women with fibromyalgia and chronic low back pain (Bair et al., 2021) [1].</p> <p>In primary care, incorporating sex‑specific pain thresholds could improve diagnostic accuracy. A pilot study in a UK general practice setting found that using a sex‑adjusted pain score reduced unnecessary imaging by 12 % and increased appropriate referrals to pain specialists (McCaffery et al., 2023) [2].</p> <h2>Regulatory and Policy Considerations</h2> <p>Regulatory agencies are beginning to respond. The FDA’s 2023 guidance on clinical trial design now requires sex‑balanced enrollment for new analgesics, especially for drugs targeting central pain pathways (FDA, 2023) [11]. The EMA has issued a similar recommendation, emphasizing the importance of including women in phase III trials (EMA, 2022) [12]. Funding bodies, including the NIH and the European Research Council, now prioritize proposals that explicitly address sex differences in pain mechanisms (NIH, 2023) [13].</p> <p>Patient advocacy groups are also pushing for change. The Women’s Health Initiative (WHI) has called for coverage of gender‑specific pain therapies and for public education campaigns to destigmatize women’s pain (WHI, 2024) [14]. These efforts could lead to insurance reforms that cover non‑opioid treatments more readily for women, reducing the risk of over‑prescription.</p> <h2>Potential Pitfalls and the Need for Balance</h2> <p>While the evidence for sex differences is compelling, caution is warranted. Pain is inherently subjective, and self‑report can be influenced by cultural, socioeconomic, and psychological factors. Cross‑sectional designs cannot establish causality; longitudinal studies are needed to track pain trajectories and assess intervention effects over time (McCaffery et al., 2023) [2]. Moreover, individual variability remains high—many men experience severe pain, and some women report low pain levels. Policymakers and clinicians must avoid over‑generalizing population data to individual care.</p> <p>There is also a risk of medicalizing normal variations in pain, leading to unnecessary pharmacotherapy or invasive procedures. A balanced approach requires integrating patient‑centered care with evidence‑based guidelines, ensuring that treatment decisions are tailored to each individual’s context.</p> <h2>Future Directions</h2> <p>Key research priorities include:</p> <ul> <li>Longitudinal cohort studies that track hormonal cycles, genetic markers, and pain outcomes in diverse populations.</li> <li>Randomized controlled trials that stratify participants by sex and gender identity to evaluate drug efficacy and safety.</li> <li>Development and validation of pain assessment tools that are sensitive to sex‑specific expressions of pain.</li> <li>Policy research on the impact of sex‑balanced trial enrollment on drug approval timelines and post‑marketing safety.</li> </ul> <p>In the regulatory arena, agencies may adopt mandatory sex‑balanced enrollment for all pain trials, and funding agencies could require sex‑disaggregated data reporting. Clinicians might adopt electronic health record prompts that flag sex‑specific pain thresholds, and medical education curricula could incorporate modules on gender‑sensitive pain care.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The convergence of epidemiological, biological, and social evidence paints a clear picture: women, on average, report higher pain intensity across most body regions than men. This finding challenges the long‑standing assumption of gender neutrality in pain and calls for a multi‑pronged response. By refining clinical assessment, ensuring balanced representation in drug development, and fostering inclusive research that acknowledges non‑binary and transgender experiences, the medical community can move toward more equitable, effective pain management for all patients.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Bair, M.
