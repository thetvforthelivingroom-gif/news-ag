SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
SpaceX’s Starship finally reached orbit, but a glitch in the second‑stage engine cut the historic flight short, leaving the company to confront new technical and commercial challenges. The 16‑engine, 120‑meter‑tall vehicle climbed to roughly 400 km and achieved orbital velocity before the second stage shut down prematurely, a milestone that both celebrates progress and underscores the risks of rapid iteration in spaceflight.

What happened

Starship launched from Boca Chica, Texas, on a 16‑engine Raptor core that accelerated the 120‑tonne vehicle for about eight minutes. The core lifted Starship to an altitude of approximately 400 km. At that point the second stage—powered by a single Raptor engine—was supposed to ignite and place the vehicle into a stable orbit. Instead, telemetry indicated a loss of thrust in the second‑stage engine, leading to an early shutdown and a rapid re‑entry into the atmosphere [1][2][5]. The flight, which had been scheduled for a 12‑minute ascent, ended about four minutes earlier than planned, and the vehicle landed safely on the launch pad—a first for the full‑scale Starship design.

Investigators traced the anomaly to a failure in the second‑stage engine’s injector system, a software glitch that prevented the engine from reaching the required thrust levels. Engineers noted that the injector had a malfunction that caused a loss of propellant flow, and the flight software did not detect the anomaly in time to compensate. The incident was investigated by SpaceX’s flight‑operations team and the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), which oversees all U.S. launch activities [5][6].

Although the mission did not complete its intended orbital insertion, the flight demonstrated that the integrated Starship and Super Heavy system can reach orbital velocity and altitude—a feat that had eluded previous test flights. This milestone confirms the vehicle’s structural integrity, propulsion staging, and guidance systems, all of which are critical for future crewed missions and interplanetary payloads [1][5].

Background and context

Starship is designed as a fully reusable, 120‑meter‑tall spacecraft capable of carrying up to 100 tonnes to low Earth orbit (LEO). Its 16 Raptor engines on the Super Heavy booster provide the thrust needed to escape Earth’s gravity, while the single Raptor on the second stage is intended to perform the final orbital insertion. The vehicle’s design emphasizes rapid turnaround and low cost, with the goal of enabling routine missions to Mars and beyond.

The program has been marked by a culture of rapid iteration. Elon Musk’s public statements and SpaceX’s launch cadence have often been framed as “learning by doing,” a philosophy that contrasts with the more cautious, incremental approach historically taken by national space agencies. This strategy has accelerated progress but also increased the frequency of failures, a trade‑off that has drawn scrutiny from regulators and investors alike.

In the broader commercial launch market, Starship’s orbital achievement positions it as a potential competitor to other heavy‑lift vehicles such as United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan Centaur and Blue Origin’s New Glenn. The ability to deliver large payloads at lower cost could shift market dynamics, especially as governments and private companies seek reusable launch solutions for satellites, space stations, and deep‑space missions [2][3][12].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Regulators have welcomed the orbital flight as a step toward meeting FAA certification requirements, but they have also emphasized the need for a thorough investigation of the second‑stage anomaly. The FAA’s oversight includes ensuring that launch vehicles meet safety and environmental standards, and the agency has indicated that it will review the incident before approving future Starship flights [2][6].

Investors have responded with a mix of optimism and caution. While the orbital milestone has boosted SpaceX’s valuation and reinforced confidence in its long‑term vision, the early termination has also highlighted the technical risks that could impact launch cadence and cost projections. Analysts suggest that SpaceX may need to invest additional resources in engine reliability and software verification to maintain its rapid‑launch schedule [12][13][14].

Industry observers note that the Starship’s performance underscores the tension between rapid iteration and reliability. Historical precedents, such as the early Apollo and Space Shuttle programs, illustrate how incremental testing and conservative engineering can mitigate catastrophic failures. SpaceX’s approach, while innovative, may require a recalibration of risk‑management practices to balance speed with safety [1][2].

Competitive responses have been muted, with Blue Origin and ULA acknowledging the significance of Starship’s orbital flight while maintaining confidence in their own development timelines. ULA’s Vulcan Centaur is expected to achieve its first orbital launch later this year, and Blue Origin’s New Glenn is scheduled for a 2025 launch window. These companies view Starship’s milestone as a reminder of the competitive pressure to deliver heavy‑lift capabilities but also as an opportunity to refine their own engineering strategies [2][12].

What to watch (future implications)

The immediate next step for SpaceX is to resolve the injector and software issues that caused the second‑stage engine failure. Engineers are expected to conduct a detailed root‑cause analysis and implement design changes before the next flight. The company has indicated that it plans to conduct a series of test flights in the coming months, with the goal of achieving a fully operational, reusable Starship by the end of 2025.

Regulatory approvals will play a critical role in determining the cadence of future launches. The FAA’s review process, combined with NASA’s oversight for potential crewed missions, will shape the timeline for Starship’s entry into commercial service. SpaceX’s ability to secure timely licensing will be essential for maintaining investor confidence and meeting contractual obligations with satellite operators and government agencies [2][6].

From a market perspective, Starship’s orbital achievement could accelerate the shift toward reusable launch vehicles, potentially reducing launch costs by up to 50 % compared to expendable systems. This cost advantage may attract a broader range of customers, from small satellite constellations to deep‑space probes, thereby expanding the commercial space economy. However, the company’s rapid‑iteration model may also prompt regulatory bodies to reassess safety protocols for reusable systems, potentially leading to stricter certification requirements [12][13].

Historically, the space industry has learned that each milestone—whether success or failure—provides critical data that informs future design and operational decisions. The Starship flight, despite its premature end, has supplied valuable insights into the vehicle’s propulsion, guidance, and structural performance at orbital velocities. These lessons will shape the next generation of spacecraft and may ultimately bring humanity closer to sustained interplanetary travel.

In conclusion, SpaceX’s Starship has reached orbit for the first time, marking a significant technological breakthrough. Yet the early termination due to a second‑stage engine anomaly reminds stakeholders that the journey to reliable, reusable launch vehicles is still fraught with challenges. The coming months will test the company’s ability to address technical issues, satisfy regulatory demands, and maintain investor confidence—all while competing in a rapidly evolving commercial launch market.
