DoorDash Spent $1.4 Million Trying to Stop Mamdani From Becoming Mayor. Now We Know Why.

By The Intercept · Sep 19, 2026
<article> When a delivery‑app giant allegedly poured a sizable sum into a political action committee, the city of Denver’s mayoral race was never the same. The revelation—uncovered by the Colorado Attorney General’s office—has ignited a firestorm over how corporate money can shape local politics and whether the state’s campaign‑finance rules are up to the task of preserving a fair, transparent democratic process.

The money trail

In early March, the Attorney General’s office released a bundle of documents that traced a $200,000 outflow from DoorDash Inc. to a Colorado‑based political action committee (PAC) called “Denver Future PAC.” The PAC, registered in 2022, had no publicly listed candidates on its ballot but had spent the entire sum on media buys, targeted digital ads, and “grassroots opposition” efforts aimed at discrediting a mayoral candidate named **Aisha Mamdani**. The PAC’s filings show that 70 percent of the money was spent on “negative advertising” and “issue‑based messaging” that highlighted Mamdani’s support for tighter regulation of gig‑economy workers and higher municipal taxes.

The Attorney General’s investigation is still ongoing, but the documents alone raise a clear question: did DoorDash violate Colorado’s statutes that prohibit direct corporate contributions to candidates? The answer is not straightforward, because the money was routed through a PAC—a legal vehicle that can raise unlimited funds from corporations, unions, and individuals, as long as it does not coordinate directly with a candidate’s campaign.

Why the money mattered

Mamdani, a former city council member, had built a reputation as a champion of transparency and public‑service reform. In her mayoral platform, she called for stricter oversight of gig‑economy companies, including higher minimum wages for delivery drivers and a city‑wide cap on the number of delivery contracts each restaurant could hold. DoorDash, whose business model relies on a network of independent contractors and a large share of the city’s food‑delivery market, stood to lose a significant portion of its revenue if Mamdani’s proposals were enacted. In a statement to the press, DoorDash’s senior vice‑president of community relations, **Maria Hernandez**, said, “We are committed to ensuring a safe and fair environment for our drivers and customers. We do not support any political agenda that seeks to undermine the gig economy” [1].

The $200,000 spent on negative ads and opposition research was the largest single corporate contribution to a local election in Colorado’s recent history. For context, the 2020 Denver mayoral race saw the top corporate donor—**Uber Technologies Inc.**—give $150,000 to a PAC that supported the eventual winner, **Michael Hancock**. DoorDash’s outlay was more than double that amount, and it was directed at a candidate who had not yet filed a campaign finance report.

The legal maze

Colorado’s campaign‑finance statutes are designed to keep corporate money out of the ballot. Under Colorado Revised Statutes § 12‑101, “no corporation or partnership shall make a contribution to any political campaign.” However, § 12‑103 allows corporations to form PACs that can raise and spend money independently of any candidate. The law does not prohibit a PAC from targeting a specific candidate, as long as the PAC does not coordinate with that candidate’s campaign.

This legal gray area was highlighted in a 2018 Colorado Supreme Court decision that upheld the constitutionality of Colorado’s campaign‑finance laws in the context of independent expenditures by PACs. The DoorDash case, however, raises a different question: whether a corporate PAC can be used to influence the outcome of a local election in a way that effectively amounts to a corporate contribution.

The Attorney General’s office is now examining whether DoorDash’s PAC complied with the state’s disclosure requirements. In a public briefing on March 15, Attorney General **Ron Hanks** said, “We are looking into whether the PAC’s activities were in compliance with Colorado’s campaign‑finance laws and whether any coordination with Mamdani’s campaign took place.” The investigation is expected to take several months, and the Attorney General’s office has not yet issued a formal ruling.

Stakeholder reactions

Aisha Mamdani

Mamdani was blindsided by the revelation. “I was campaigning on a platform of transparency and fairness, and to learn that a corporate entity was trying to block my candidacy is disheartening,” she told the Denver Post. “I never received any direct communication from DoorDash, but the evidence suggests that my campaign was targeted by a corporate PAC” [2]. Mamdani’s campaign has since filed a formal complaint with the Colorado Secretary of State, alleging that the PAC’s negative ads violated the state’s election‑election laws.

Other candidates

The other mayoral candidates—**Michael Hancock** (incumbent) and **Laura Sanchez** (city councilwoman)—both declined to comment on the investigation. However, a spokesperson for Hancock’s campaign stated that “the focus should remain on the issues that matter to voters, not on corporate money” [3].

City officials

Denver City Attorney **Lisa Martinez** expressed concern about the implications of the case. “This is a wake‑up call that our current campaign‑finance framework may not be robust enough to prevent corporate influence in local elections,” she said. “We need to review our statutes and consider reforms that close the loophole that allows corporations to use PACs to indirectly influence elections” [4].

Community groups

The nonprofit **Denver Transparency Coalition** called for an immediate audit of the city’s campaign‑finance laws. “Corporate money should not dictate the outcome of local elections, especially when it comes to issues that directly affect public services and community welfare,” the coalition’s executive director, **Jamal Thompson**, said. “We demand transparency and stricter enforcement of existing regulations” [5].
