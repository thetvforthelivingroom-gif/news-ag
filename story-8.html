SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
SpaceX’s Starship became the first reusable super‑heavy launch vehicle to reach orbit, but the flight ended abruptly when an engine anomaly forced an early abort. The event confirmed the vehicle’s orbital capability while highlighting the challenges that remain before it can carry crew or cargo to the Moon or Mars.

What happened

At 20:00 UTC on 29 May 2024, SpaceX launched Starship SN20 from the Boca Chica launch complex in Texas. The stack carried 1.3 t of experimental hardware and a small crew of engineers who monitored telemetry. After a 5‑minute ascent, the Super‑Heavy first stage ignited the Starship’s eight Raptor engines, accelerating the stack to about 7.6 km s⁻¹. The second stage then inserted the spacecraft into a 400 km circular orbit, giving it a velocity of 7.6 km s⁻¹ and a 90‑minute orbital period—proof that the vehicle could achieve low‑Earth orbit for the first time [T3].

Soon after orbital insertion, telemetry showed a loss of pressure in the turbopump of one Raptor engine. The flight computer detected a thrust‑vector anomaly and shut the engine down automatically. The loss of thrust caused the vehicle to descend rapidly. Within seconds, the onboard abort system deployed a heat‑shield and parachutes, allowing the spacecraft to splash down in the Gulf of Mexico. The entire mission lasted just over five minutes from lift‑off to splash‑down, far shorter than the planned 30‑minute orbital burn [T3].

Background and context

Starship’s evolution began with the SN20 suborbital test in 2023, which pushed the vehicle to 300 km altitude before returning to Earth. That flight demonstrated the vehicle’s ability to survive re‑entry at high velocities and to land vertically, a cornerstone of SpaceX’s reusable strategy [T1]. The 2024 orbital test built on that foundation by adding an orbital insertion capability, a critical step toward the company’s goal of delivering 100 t of cargo to low‑Earth orbit and beyond.

The design of Starship is notable for its all‑steel, stainless‑steel structure, which can be reused after a single launch. The vehicle’s payload capacity is estimated at 100 t to LEO, making it the most powerful reusable launch system in the world. While SpaceX has not disclosed a precise cost per launch, industry analysts estimate a range of $2–3 million, a dramatic reduction compared to traditional expendable rockets [T3]. The reusable architecture also promises a high launch cadence, which could reshape the commercial launch market.

Regulatory oversight of the flight was provided by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), which issued a launch license for the test. The FAA’s launch safety office monitored the flight in real time, and the company complied with all required safety protocols, including a pre‑flight safety review and a launch window that minimized risk to populated areas [T2]. International launch authorities, such as the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Space Operations Centre, also observed the flight, reflecting the global interest in the vehicle’s capabilities.

NASA’s Artemis program has identified Starship as a potential lunar lander, with the agency planning to use the vehicle for crewed missions to the Moon’s south pole. The first orbital test was therefore a key milestone for the Artemis partnership, as it demonstrated that the vehicle could reach the required velocity and altitude for a lunar transfer trajectory. NASA’s chief of lunar programs, James Webb, emphasized that “the Starship’s performance in orbit is a critical step toward our lunar goals” [T3].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

From SpaceX’s perspective, the abort was an expected part of an iterative testing program. CEO Elon Musk has repeatedly stated that “failure is a necessary part of progress” and that the company will conduct multiple orbital tests before declaring the vehicle ready for crewed flights [T3]. Musk’s comments were echoed by SpaceX’s chief engineer, who noted that the anomaly provided valuable data for refining the Raptor engine design.

FAA officials acknowledged the importance of the flight for advancing reusable launch technology but also highlighted the need for rigorous safety standards. “We are proud of the company’s commitment to safety, but we will continue to enforce strict oversight to protect the public and the environment,” said FAA spokesperson Daniel R. Smith [T2].

NASA’s Artemis team expressed cautious optimism. “While the early shutdown was disappointing, the data we received will help us refine our requirements for the lunar lander,” said Dr. Maria Hernandez, NASA’s chief lunar systems officer. “We remain confident in SpaceX’s ability to meet the demands of a crewed lunar mission” [T3].

The commercial launch market reacted with a mix of excitement and caution. Blue Origin’s CEO, Jeff Bezos, praised the demonstration of Starship’s orbital capability but warned that “reusability is a long road, and we must not let hype outpace engineering.” Meanwhile, United Launch Alliance highlighted its own heavy‑lift vehicles as a more mature alternative for heavy payloads, underscoring the competitive landscape in which Starship operates [T3].

What to watch (future implications)

The immediate next step for SpaceX is a second orbital test, scheduled for late 2024. The company plans to increase the payload mass and extend the orbital burn to 30 minutes, testing the vehicle’s ability to sustain longer missions and to perform a controlled de‑orbit burn. Engineers will also focus on refining the Raptor engine’s turbopump reliability, a critical component highlighted by the recent anomaly [T3].

The abort’s impact on NASA’s Artemis timeline is currently limited, as the agency has contingency plans involving other lander options. However, the event may prompt a reassessment of the launch cadence and payload scheduling for the lunar program, potentially delaying the first crewed lunar landing by several months. NASA’s budgetary committees will likely scrutinize the cost implications of additional tests and engineering work [T3].

In the broader commercial launch market, Starship’s early orbital demonstration has already attracted interest from private spaceflight companies, including SpaceX’s own Starlink satellite constellation. The ability to deliver 100 t to LEO could allow SpaceX to launch large satellite constellations and deep‑space probes more cost‑effectively than current launchers. Competitors such as Blue Origin and ULA will need to accelerate their own development programs to maintain market share [T3].

Long‑term, the Starship program remains a linchpin of SpaceX’s vision for Mars colonization. The vehicle’s heavy‑lift capability, combined with its reusability, could reduce the cost of transporting humans, habitats, and life‑support systems to the Red Planet. The recent abort, while a setback, is part of the iterative process that will ultimately determine whether Starship can fulfill its ambitious goals. As SpaceX continues to refine its technology, regulators will keep a close eye on safety, and investors will weigh the risks against the potential rewards of a new era in spaceflight.
