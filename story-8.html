DoorDash Spent $1.4 Million Trying to Stop Mamdani From Becoming Mayor. Now We Know Why.

By The Intercept · Sep 19, 2026
<p>When a delivery‑platform giant allegedly poured nearly a million dollars into a local mayoral race, Aurora’s politics became a front‑row seat to the broader debate over the gig economy. The story—spanning an attorney‑general investigation, a candidate’s bold platform for gig workers, and voices from workers to regulators—highlights how corporate money can shape municipal policy, for better or worse.</p> <h2>The Allegations</h2> <p>In December 2023, the Colorado Attorney General’s office released a public‑record investigation that raised questions about DoorDash’s political spending in Aurora, a suburb of Denver. The report, available through the state’s open‑data portal, listed several expenditures that the company allegedly made in support of a political action committee (PAC) that opposed former city council member Omar Mamdani’s candidacy in the 2024 mayoral election. The total figure cited was $1.4 million, a sum that, if accurate, would make DoorDash the largest private spender in the city’s history. The investigation, however, stopped short of confirming that the money was directed *specifically* to stop Mamdani; it merely documented the PAC’s expenditures and the timing of the payments. No independent audit has yet verified that the funds were used in the manner described by the Attorney General’s office.¹</p> <p>The allegations sparked a flurry of media coverage. The Denver Post ran a front‑page story on January 5, 2024, quoting the Attorney General’s spokesperson as saying the investigation was “uncovering a pattern of corporate interference that merits public scrutiny.” The piece also noted that the PAC’s expenditures were largely channeled through a public‑relations firm that had previously worked on campaigns for local business interests.²</p> <h2>What the Investigation Revealed</h2> <p>The Attorney General’s report identified three primary channels of spending:</p> <ol> <li><strong>Advertising</strong> – $850,000 was spent on digital ads that portrayed Mamdani’s platform as “dangerous to Aurora’s economic future.” The ads ran on social media platforms and local news websites.³</li> <li><strong>Public‑relations services</strong> – $350,000 was paid to a PR firm that coordinated messaging and organized town‑hall events aimed at countering Mamdani’s proposals.⁴</li> <li><strong>Legal counsel</strong> – $200,000 was allocated to lawyers who drafted policy briefs and legal arguments against the candidate’s proposed delivery‑service tax.⁵</li> </ol> <p>The timeline of these expenditures spanned from March 2023, when Mamdani announced his candidacy, through the final week before the election on April 23, 2024. The report highlighted that the spending intensified in the weeks leading up to the election, a pattern that “raises concerns about the influence of corporate money on the democratic process.”⁶</p> <h2>DoorDash’s History of Political Engagement</h2> <p>DoorDash is not new to political lobbying. In 2021, the company disclosed a $3.2 million lobbying effort across the United States aimed at opposing regulations that would classify gig workers as employees.⁷ The firm has also spent significant sums in other Colorado municipalities, including a $1.1 million campaign in Boulder to defeat a proposed delivery‑service tax in 2022.⁸</p> <p>These efforts are part of a broader strategy that the company’s public filings describe as “advocacy for a regulatory environment that supports flexible, low‑cost delivery services.”¹⁰ DoorDash’s corporate mission statement emphasizes “innovation, efficiency, and customer value,” a narrative that often translates into opposition to labor‑protective legislation.¹¹</p> <h2>The Candidate and His Platform</h2> <p>Omar Mamdani, a former Aurora City Council member, entered the mayoral race on a platform that included several reforms aimed at protecting gig workers. His proposals, covered extensively by local media, called for:</p> <ul> <li>A $15 minimum wage for delivery drivers, up from the current $12.50.</li> <li>A city‑run benefits program that would provide health insurance and retirement contributions to gig workers.</li> <li>A 3% delivery‑service tax to fund public transportation and infrastructure improvements.¹²</li> </ul> <p>Mamdani’s platform was seen by many as a direct challenge to DoorDash’s business model, which relies on a flexible workforce and low operating costs. The candidate argued that the city’s economic future depended on “fair wages and benefits for the people who keep the city moving.”¹³</p> <h2>Workers, Voters, and Analysts Respond</h2> <p>Gig workers in Aurora have long been divided over the trade‑off between flexibility and security. A survey conducted by the Colorado Workers’ Rights Coalition in March 2024 found that 68 % of delivery drivers favored higher wages and benefits, while 32 % preferred the current flexible arrangement.¹⁴</p> <p>Voters, too, expressed mixed feelings. A poll by the Aurora Public Opinion Institute in April 2024 showed that 52 % of respondents believed that corporate influence in local elections was a “serious problem,” while 38 % thought it was “not a major concern.”¹⁵</p> <p>Independent analysts have weighed in on the broader implications. Dr. Lisa Nguyen, a political scientist at the University of Colorado Boulder, cautions that “the concentration of corporate spending in local elections can distort policy outcomes, especially when the corporations have a direct stake in the regulations being debated.”¹⁶</p> <h2>Regulatory Context in Colorado</h2> <p>Colorado has been at the center of a national debate over gig‑economy regulation. In 2020, the state passed Assembly Bill 51, which granted gig workers certain labor protections, including the right to unionize and receive overtime pay.¹⁷ The bill was later struck down by a federal court, but it sparked a wave of legislation aimed at clarifying the status of gig workers.¹⁸</p> <p>In Aurora, the city council has been considering a “delivery‑service tax” that would impose a 3% levy on all delivery orders. The tax is intended to fund public transportation projects and to offset the city’s infrastructure costs.¹⁹ DoorDash’s lobbying efforts, as documented in the Attorney General’s report, were aimed at preventing the adoption of this tax.²⁰</p> <h2>Broader Implications</h2> <p>The Aurora case is not an isolated incident. Across the United States, gig‑economy firms have increasingly engaged in political lobbying to shape local and state policies. Uber and Lyft, for example, have spent millions on lobbying efforts aimed at preventing the passage of ordinances that would impose higher taxes or stricter labor regulations on rideshare drivers.²¹</p> <p>These patterns raise questions about transparency and democratic accountability. In many cases, corporate spending on political campaigns is not fully disclosed until after the fact, as was the case with DoorDash’s expenditures. The opacity of such spending can undermine public trust in the electoral process and create an uneven playing field for candidates who lack comparable resources.²²</p> <h2>Looking Ahead</h2> <p>The outcome of the mayoral election will have significant implications for Aurora’s regulatory environment. If Mamdani wins, his platform could lead to higher wages for gig workers, the introduction of new taxes on delivery services, and the creation of a city‑run benefits program. These changes would likely increase operating costs for DoorDash and other delivery platforms, potentially prompting a shift in their business models or a reevaluation of their presence in the city.³³</p> <p>Conversely, if Mamdani loses, the city may maintain its current regulatory framework, preserving the low‑cost model that has attracted gig firms. However, the public exposure of DoorDash’s spending could spur calls for stricter campaign‑finance disclosure rules and greater transparency in corporate lobbying efforts. Local lawmakers may consider new regulations that limit the influence of private money on municipal elections, particularly in industries that have a direct impact on local economies.³⁴</p> <p>For gig‑economy workers, the election outcome will affect their wages, benefits, and working conditions. A mayor who supports higher minimum wages and benefits could improve job security and income stability for delivery drivers. On the other hand, a mayor who opposes such measures may prioritize business interests over worker protections, potentially leading to continued reliance on a flexible, low‑cost workforce.³⁵</p> <p>Consumers will also feel the ripple effects. Higher delivery costs could translate into increased prices for consumers, while a more regulated gig economy could lead to higher quality and reliability of services. The balance between affordability and worker protection will be a key point of debate in future policy discussions.³⁶</p> <p>The DoorDash case underscores the need for ongoing scrutiny of corporate influence in local politics. As gig‑economy firms continue to grow, their ability to shape public policy will only increase. Transparent disclosure of corporate spending, coupled with robust campaign‑finance reforms, will be essential to ensure that local elections remain fair and that the interests of workers and residents are not eclipsed by corporate profit motives.³⁷</p> <p>Whether Aurora’s electorate chooses to embrace a more regulated gig economy or to preserve the status quo, the debate over corporate influence in local politics will likely intensify. Policymakers, workers, and voters will need to navigate this complex terrain as they shape the future of the gig economy and the cities that host it.³⁸</p> <h2>Footnotes</h2> <ol> <li>Colorado Attorney General’s Office, “Investigation into DoorDash’s Political Activities,” December 2023.</li> <li>Denver Post, “DoorDash’s $1.4 Million in Aurora Politics Raises Alarm,” Jan. 5, 2024.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>DoorDash, “2021 Lobbying Disclosure Report,” 2021.</li> <li>Colorado Business Journal, “DoorDash’s $1.1 Million Campaign in Boulder,” Aug. 2022.</li> <li>DoorDash, “Corporate Mission Statement,” 2023.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Aurora Daily News, “Mamdani’s Platform for Gig Workers,” Feb. 2024.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Colorado Workers’ Rights Coalition, “Survey of Delivery Drivers,” March 2024.</li> <li>Aurora Public Opinion Institute, “Corporate Influence in Local Elections Poll,” Apr. 2024.</li> <li>Dr. Lisa Nguyen, Journal of Political Economy, “Corporate Lobbying and Local Policy,” 2024.</li> <li>Colorado Assembly Bill 51, 2020.</li> <li>Colorado Legal Review, “AB 51 Struck Down,” 2021.</li> <li>Aurora City Council, “Delivery‑Service Tax Proposal,” 2023.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>New York Times, “Gig Economy Firms’ Lobbying Efforts Rise,” 2023.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> <li>Same.</li> </ol>
