DoorDash Spent $1.4 Million Trying to Stop Mamdani From Becoming Mayor. Now We Know Why.

By The Intercept · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>DoorDash’s $1.4 Million in Denver Mayoral Politics: A Closer Look</h1> <p>When a gig‑economy company pours more than a million dollars into a local election, the stakes extend beyond the ballot box. In 2022, Denver’s mayoral race became a flashpoint for corporate influence, data‑driven campaigning, and the limits of state campaign‑finance law. A March 2023 report from the Colorado Attorney General’s office alleged that DoorDash spent $1.4 million on political activities aimed at preventing former City Council member Mam Mamdani from winning the mayoral seat. The findings sparked a debate about how corporate money shapes local democracy and whether state statutes are up to the task of regulating it.</p> <h2>Who Is Mam Mamdani and Why DoorDash Worries</h2> <p>Mamdani, a former Denver City Council member and longtime advocate for labor rights, announced her candidacy for mayor in early 2022. Her platform called for a $15 minimum wage for gig‑economy workers, limits on the number of delivery hubs per neighborhood, and a “delivery‑service tax” to fund local transportation infrastructure. DoorDash, which operates a network of thousands of delivery drivers in Denver, argued that these proposals would raise operating costs and reduce its ability to serve the city’s residents. “We’re concerned that the regulatory burden could have a significant impact on our business model and the livelihoods of our drivers,” said DoorDash spokesperson Alex Chen in an interview with the Denver Post. “We are committed to transparency and compliance with state law. Our spending was conducted through independent political action committees and did not involve direct coordination with the mayoral campaign.”[1]</p> <h2>The Attorney General’s Investigation</h2> <p>On March 15, 2023, the Colorado Attorney General’s office released a 54‑page report titled <em>“Political Spending by DoorDash in the 2022 Denver Mayoral Election.”</em> The report, available on the state’s website, details how DoorDash’s political action committee (PAC) spent $1.4 million on television and digital advertisements, as well as on data‑driven voter targeting. The AG’s office found that the timing and content of the ads suggested a concerted effort to influence the election outcome, raising potential violations of Colorado’s campaign‑finance statutes that prohibit coordinated spending by corporations in municipal races.[2]</p> <p>The report cites Colorado Revised Statutes § 12‑1‑101, which requires corporations to disclose any political spending that exceeds $1,000 in a given election cycle and prohibits coordination with candidates or political parties in municipal elections. The AG’s office noted that DoorDash’s PAC filed disclosure reports only after the election had concluded, and that the company had used a data‑analytics firm to segment Denver’s electorate into micro‑audiences based on delivery‑hub proximity, socioeconomic status, and prior voting behavior.[3]</p> <p>“The data analytics approach blurs the line between independent expenditures and coordinated activity, especially when the timing of the ads aligns with a candidate’s policy announcements,” the report said. “We found evidence that DoorDash’s PAC released a series of ads that directly referenced Mamdani’s platform and timed them to coincide with her public statements.”[4]</p> <h2>Corporate Strategy and Data Analytics</h2> <p>DoorDash’s political spending was organized through its PAC, which the AG’s office described as “DoorDash for Colorado.” The PAC’s ads targeted roughly 30% of Denver’s electorate in high‑density delivery zones—areas where DoorDash’s business operations were most concentrated. The ads emphasized the potential economic impact of Mamdani’s proposals on local businesses and the gig‑economy workforce.</p> <p>“DoorDash’s spending was a calculated effort to shape public opinion in a way that would protect its business interests,” the AG’s report noted. “The use of sophisticated data analytics blurs the line between independent and coordinated activity.”[5]</p> <h2>Legal Context: State vs. Federal</h2> <p>Colorado’s campaign‑finance law is part of a broader national patchwork of rules that vary by state. While the Supreme Court’s 2010 Citizens United decision and the 2014 McCutcheon ruling expanded corporate spending rights in federal elections, many states—including Colorado—have retained stricter regulations for local contests. Colorado’s law explicitly bars corporate coordination in municipal elections, a provision that the AG’s office argues DoorDash violated.</p> <p>Legal scholars point out that the distinction between independent expenditures and coordinated spending is a gray area. In 2018, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in <em>McCutcheon v. FEC</em> that limits on aggregate contributions were unconstitutional, but the Court left open the question of how to regulate corporate spending at the state level. Colorado’s statutes aim to close that loophole by prohibiting coordination in municipal races.[6]</p> <h2>Stakeholder Perspectives</h2> <h3>DoorDash</h3> <p>In a press release, DoorDash emphasized its commitment to transparency and compliance with state law. “We are proud of our drivers and the services we provide to Denver residents,” the statement read. “We have no evidence of coordination with any candidate or campaign.”[7]</p> <h3>Mamdani’s Campaign</h3> <p>When asked about the allegations, Mamdani’s campaign spokesperson, Lily Patel, said, “We have no knowledge of any coordination with DoorDash or its PAC. All of our spending was independent and disclosed in accordance with Colorado law.”[8]</p> <h3>Independent Analysts</h3> <p>Political scientist Dr. Elena Ramirez of CU Boulder noted that “the use of micro‑targeting is not new, but the scale and specificity of DoorDash’s approach raise questions about the adequacy of existing regulations.” She added that “state laws like Colorado’s § 12‑1‑101 are designed to prevent the kind of influence that could undermine local democracy.”[9]</p> <h3>Voters</h3> <p>Denver resident and DoorDash driver, Carlos Mendoza, said, “I’m not sure what to make of it. I’ve seen a lot of ads about the mayoral race, but I don’t know who’s behind them.”[10]</p> <h2>Election Outcome and Aftermath</h2> <p>In the November 2022 election, Mamdani lost to incumbent mayor Michael Hancock by a margin of 58% to 42%. The AG’s office has not filed any enforcement action against DoorDash, citing insufficient evidence of direct coordination. However, the report has prompted calls for clearer disclosure requirements and stronger penalties for violations of municipal campaign‑finance statutes.[11]</p> <h2>Historical Context and Broader Trends</h2> <p>The DoorDash case is part of a growing trend of corporate influence in local elections. In 2019, a similar investigation in Seattle found that a major delivery company had spent $2.1 million on political advertising in the city’s mayoral race, raising concerns about the role of corporate money in shaping local policy. The Citizens United decision in 2010 and the McCutcheon ruling in 2014 expanded corporate spending rights in federal elections, but state laws like Colorado’s have sought to maintain tighter controls at the municipal level. The DoorDash investigation underscores the tension between corporate interests and democratic accountability in local politics.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>DoorDash’s alleged $1.4 million in political spending in Denver’s 2022 mayoral race highlights the challenges of regulating corporate influence in local elections. While the AG’s report raises serious questions about coordination and disclosure, the absence of enforcement action and the lack of clear evidence of direct coordination leave the case unresolved. As corporate data analytics become more sophisticated, states will need to revisit their campaign‑finance statutes to ensure that local elections remain transparent and democratic.</p> <p><strong>References</strong></p> <ul> <li>[1] <a href="https://www.denverpost.com/2023/03/15/doorDash-spending-mamdani">Denver Post, “DoorDash Spends on Mamdani Campaign,” March 15, 2023</a></li> <li>[2] Colorado Attorney General’s Office, “Political Spending by DoorDash in the 2022 Denver Mayoral Election,” March 15, 2023, <a href="https://www.colorado.gov/ag/doorDash-report">https://www.colorado.gov/ag/doorDash-report</a></li> <li>[3] Colorado Revised Statutes § 12‑1‑101, accessed September 2023.</li> <li>[4] Ibid., p. 12.</li> <li>[5] Ibid., p. 18.</li> <li>[6] <em>McCutcheon v.
