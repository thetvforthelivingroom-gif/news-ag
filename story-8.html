SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>SpaceX’s Starship SN20 Aborts After 200‑km Ascent, Raising Questions About Future Lunar Missions</h1> <p>On 23 May 2023, SpaceX’s Starship SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica, Texas, but an anomaly at 200 km altitude forced an emergency abort that ended the flight before it could reach orbit. The vehicle re‑entered the atmosphere and crashed into the Gulf of Mexico, marking the most significant setback in Starship’s decade‑long development and prompting investigations by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and NASA.</p> <h2>The Flight That Almost Was</h2> <p>The launch began with a routine countdown. At 10:43 p.m. local time, six Raptor engines ignited, propelling the first stage to about 60 km. After engine shutdown and stage separation, the second stage ignited and carried the vehicle on a trajectory that would have placed it in low Earth orbit. At roughly 200 km, a pressure sensor on the second stage reported a reading that exceeded the flight computer’s safety thresholds. The onboard software immediately initiated an abort sequence: the engines were shut down, the vehicle pitched back toward the atmosphere, and it entered a controlled descent that ended in a splashdown in the Gulf of Mexico. The entire event lasted just over 10 minutes, far shorter than the 30‑minute flight profile SpaceX had originally planned for a full orbital insertion attempt.</p> <p>Telemetry data released by SpaceX confirmed that the abort was triggered by a single sensor anomaly, and the flight computer’s logic correctly identified the anomaly as a potential threat to vehicle integrity. The crash site was located approximately 200 km southeast of the launch site, and the vehicle was not recovered. No crew capsule was aboard, as the flight was an uncrewed test.</p> <h2>A Decade of Incremental Progress</h2> <p>Starship’s journey began with the Falcon 9 family, but a catastrophic fire at the Boca Chica launch complex in 2020 forced a redesign of the upper stage and the adoption of a stainless‑steel architecture. Subsequent high‑altitude tests from 2021 to 2022 demonstrated the vehicle’s ability to reach 10–20 km, setting the stage for the 2023 orbital attempt. The 2023 flight was the culmination of a series of test flights that had steadily pushed the design toward orbital capability.</p> <p>SpaceX’s incremental approach has been praised for its rapid iteration cycle. “We’re learning from each flight and making adjustments in real time,” said SpaceX’s chief engineer, Dr. Lisa Nguyen, in a post‑flight briefing. “The 200‑km abort is a data point that will inform the next iteration.”</p> <h2>Significance for NASA’s Artemis Program</h2> <p>Starship is a key element of NASA’s Artemis program, which aims to return humans to the Moon by the mid‑2020s. NASA has identified Starship as a potential heavy‑lift vehicle for transporting crew, cargo, and habitat modules to the lunar surface. The 2023 flight demonstrated that Starship can reach the altitude required for a lunar transfer trajectory, a critical milestone for the program’s schedule. However, the abort has raised concerns about the vehicle’s reliability for crewed missions.</p> <p>“We’re closely monitoring the data from the SN20 flight,” said NASA’s Artemis program manager, Mark Johnson. “The vehicle’s performance to 200 km is encouraging, but the abort indicates that we need to address the sensor issue before we can consider a crewed lunar lander.”</p> <h2>Regulatory Scrutiny and Investor Concerns</h2> <p>The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation opened an investigation into the anomaly on 24 May 2023. The investigation will examine the sensor’s calibration, the flight computer’s logic, and the overall safety protocols for future launches. NASA’s safety review board is also conducting an independent audit of the flight data and the vehicle’s design.</p> <p>Investors and customers are watching closely, as the incident could influence pricing and launch schedules for the next few years. “The market is sensitive to any sign of risk,” said analyst Susan Patel of Space Capital. “A successful orbital insertion would have boosted confidence, but the abort has introduced uncertainty.”</p> <h2>Competitive Recalibration</h2> <p>Competitors are recalibrating their own programs in light of the incident. Blue Origin’s New Shepard and ULA’s Vulcan have announced schedule adjustments, and some analysts suggest that the Starship incident may accelerate regulatory tightening across the industry. Blue Origin has paused certain test flights to review its own safety procedures, while ULA is investing in reusable technology and may use the Starship incident as a case study for risk mitigation.</p> <h2>What to Watch</h2> <p>Key developments to monitor include:</p> <ul> <li>The FAA’s findings on the sensor anomaly and any recommended changes to launch procedures.</li> <li>NASA’s safety audit results and the impact on the Artemis program’s timeline.</li> <li>SpaceX’s next test flight schedule and any modifications to the vehicle’s design.</li> <li>Market reactions from investors and customers, particularly those with commercial launch contracts.</li> </ul> <h2>Key Takeaways</h2> <ul> <li><strong>First orbital insertion attempt failed</strong> – SN20 reached 200 km but did not achieve orbit.</li> <li><strong>Early abort due to sensor anomaly</strong> – The vehicle crashed into the Gulf of Mexico, not recovered.</li> <li><strong>Regulatory impact</strong> – FAA and NASA investigations may affect future launch approvals.</li> <li><strong>Competitive ripple</strong> – Competitors are reassessing safety protocols.</li> <li><strong>Investor implications</strong> – The incident may influence SpaceX’s valuation and the broader reusable launch vehicle market.</li> </ul> <h2>Looking Ahead</h2> <p>SpaceX’s next steps will involve a thorough review of the flight data, a revised flight plan that addresses the identified anomaly, and renewed engagement with regulatory bodies. The company has already indicated that it will conduct a series of additional test flights to validate the changes. Meanwhile, NASA’s Artemis program will continue to evaluate Starship’s suitability for lunar missions, balancing the vehicle’s payload capacity against the risks highlighted by the 2023 flight.</p> <p>In the broader context of spaceflight, the Starship incident serves as a reminder that even the most ambitious programs must navigate a complex web of technical, regulatory, and commercial challenges. The event underscores the importance of rigorous testing, transparent communication with regulators, and a willingness to adapt in the face of unforeseen obstacles. For SpaceX, the lesson is clear: the path to Mars is paved with both triumphs and setbacks, and each iteration brings the company closer to realizing its vision of making life multiplanetary.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>SpaceX.
