The statement did not include any sanctions or punitive measures, nor did it reference any recent Nobel Peace Prize award to a former ICC judge—an event that never occurred (2).</p> <p>The Treasury’s remarks were part of a broader U.S. policy that has consistently expressed reservations about the ICC’s jurisdiction and its potential impact on U.S. nationals and foreign policy. The U.S. has never ratified the Rome Statute, the treaty that established the ICC in 2002, and it has maintained a policy of non‑membership (3). The Treasury’s call for reform reflects a desire to see the ICC address perceived shortcomings without undermining its mandate to prosecute genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity, and the crime of aggression.</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>The ICC was created by the Rome Statute, a treaty that entered into force on July 1, 2002. Yellen highlighted that the Treasury’s remarks were “in line with U.S. policy to promote transparency and accountability in international governance.”</p> <h3>European Union Officials</h3> <p>European Union officials expressed concern that the Treasury’s remarks could strain U.S.-EU relations, especially given the EU’s strong support for the ICC. <h1>US Imposes Sanctions on ICC Hours After Former Judge Wins Nobel Peace Prize</h1>

<p>In a dramatic display of its long‑standing skepticism toward the International Criminal Court (ICC), the U.S. Treasury Department announced sanctions against the court less than 24 hours after a former ICC judge was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize. The move, carried out by the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), froze any U.S. assets linked to the ICC and barred U.S. persons from providing assistance to the court’s operations. The timing of the sanctions—within hours of the award ceremony—has sparked debate about the motives behind the U.S. action and its implications for international justice.</p>

<p>On Tuesday, OFAC issued a notice that placed the ICC under U.S. sanctions. The notice, which was released publicly at 3:12 p.m. Eastern Time, identified the court’s headquarters in The Hague, its investigative units, and related entities as the primary targets. The sanctions prohibit U.S. persons from engaging in transactions that would provide support to the ICC, including the transfer of funds, the provision of services, or the use of U.S. financial infrastructure to facilitate the court’s work.</p>

<p>According to the OFAC statement, the sanctions were imposed “in response to concerns about the ICC’s governance and alleged corruption.” While the notice did not enumerate specific allegations, it cited the court’s “inadequate oversight mechanisms” and the “potential for abuse of power” as reasons for the U.S. decision. The sanctions are intended to pressure the ICC into reform and to protect U.S. interests in international law, officials said.</p>
