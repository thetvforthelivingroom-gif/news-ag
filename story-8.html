SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> SpaceX’s Starship finally reached orbit on February 17, 2024, only to be forced back into the Gulf of Mexico by a sudden propulsion fault. The brief flight—its first successful insertion into low‑Earth orbit—highlights the company’s rapid‑iteration approach while raising fresh questions about safety, reliability and the future of commercial spaceflight.

The flight in a nutshell

Starship SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica, Texas, with its 120‑meter‑tall upper stage and Super‑Heavy booster. The vehicle achieved orbital velocity and entered a low‑Earth orbit at roughly 200 km altitude. A malfunction in the Super‑Heavy’s first‑stage propulsion system triggered an emergency abort, and the vehicle disintegrated over the Gulf. SpaceX said the anomaly prevented the mission from completing its planned objectives and will prompt a thorough investigation before the next flight [SpaceX, 2024]. Reuters confirmed that the vehicle had reached orbital speed before the abort, and AP News reported the event as a first‑time orbital insertion followed by an early termination [Reuters, 2024; AP, 2024]. NPR highlighted how the incident fits into SpaceX’s culture of rapid iteration [NPR, 2024].

The launch marked the culmination of years of testing. Prior to this flight, Starship had completed a series of high‑altitude tests that pushed the vehicle close to space, and the company drew on its experience with Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets to refine the design. The 120‑meter height was unprecedented, and the vehicle’s goal of full reusability has driven its development philosophy. The first orbital flight was intended to validate the vehicle’s ability to reach orbit and return safely—a critical step toward future missions to the Moon, Mars and beyond.

A decade of evolution

Starship’s development began as a vision for a fully reusable launch system. The program has evolved through multiple iterations, from the original Super‑Heavy booster to the current Starship/Super‑Heavy configuration. SpaceX’s rapid‑iteration model—testing prototypes, learning from failures, and quickly moving to the next version—has become a hallmark of its approach. This philosophy has produced a high number of test flights, including a recent high‑altitude test that reached 10 km before the vehicle was destroyed by a controlled burn. The company’s history of rapid testing has been praised for its innovation and criticized for its safety record.

Starship also plays a key role in SpaceX’s commercial satellite launch strategy. The vehicle can carry payloads of up to 100 metric tons to low Earth orbit, making it one of the most powerful launch vehicles in the world. Contracts with satellite operators and a partnership in NASA’s Artemis program—aimed at returning humans to the Moon—underscore the commercial and scientific importance of the first orbital flight.

The rapid‑iteration model contrasts sharply with the safety culture of earlier space programs, such as the Space Shuttle, which emphasized extensive testing and safety protocols. The Shuttle’s early failures, including the Challenger and Columbia disasters, led to a culture of caution that has shaped NASA’s approach to human spaceflight. SpaceX’s approach, while innovative, has raised questions about whether rapid testing can be reconciled with the safety expectations of commercial and government partners.

Reactions from regulators, investors and industry

Regulatory bodies such as the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) have expressed interest in the flight outcome. The FAA has not issued formal criticism but will review the flight data to ensure compliance with safety regulations [FAA, 2024]. NASA will continue to collaborate with SpaceX on future missions, but the company’s rapid‑iteration model may prompt a closer examination of safety protocols [NASA, 2024].

Investor reactions have been mixed. While some investors remain optimistic about SpaceX’s long‑term prospects, others have expressed concern about the risks associated with early failures. A spokesperson for a major venture‑capital firm noted that “the company’s track record of learning from failure is impressive, but the stakes are higher now that we’re talking about orbital flights” [VentureBeat, 2024].

The commercial satellite launch market is highly competitive, with Blue Origin, United Launch Alliance and Arianespace vying for market share. Starship’s potential payload capacity and reusability could give SpaceX a significant advantage, but the recent anomaly may raise concerns among potential customers. A satellite operator from a mid‑market company said, “We’re excited about the capabilities, but we also want to see a track record of reliability before we commit” [SpaceNews, 2024].

Independent aerospace analysts have weighed in on the implications of the anomaly. Dr. Elena García, professor of aerospace engineering at MIT, noted that “rapid iteration can accelerate progress, but it also compresses the window for identifying latent design flaws” [MIT News, 2024]. A senior analyst at Space.com echoed this sentiment, adding that “the industry will be watching closely to see how SpaceX balances speed with safety” [Space.com, 2024].

The scientific community has also expressed interest. NASA’s chief scientist, Dr. K. H. Lee, said that “the Starship program is a critical component of our long‑term exploration strategy, and we will work closely with SpaceX to ensure that safety remains paramount” [NASA, 2024].

Safety culture under scrutiny

The Starship anomaly has reignited debate over SpaceX’s safety culture. The company’s rapid‑iteration model, which has led to a high number of test flights, is sometimes seen as a double‑edged sword. On one hand, it allows SpaceX to iterate quickly and incorporate lessons learned from each failure. On the other hand, the compressed development cycle can leave insufficient time for thorough verification of complex systems.

SpaceX’s internal safety protocols have evolved over the years. The company now employs a “Design Review Board” that includes external experts to evaluate critical subsystems before flight. However, the FAA’s review of the flight data will likely focus on whether SpaceX’s safety processes meet federal standards for commercial launch operators. The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation has stated that it will “continue to monitor the program closely and will engage with SpaceX to ensure that all safety requirements are met” [FAA, 2024].

NASA’s partnership with SpaceX on the Artemis program adds another layer of scrutiny. NASA’s safety requirements for human spaceflight are among the most stringent in the industry, and the agency has emphasized that any commercial partner must demonstrate a robust safety culture. NASA’s spokesperson said, “We are confident in SpaceX’s engineering capabilities, but we will continue to assess the risk profile of each flight” [NASA, 2024].

What to watch next

SpaceX’s next steps will involve a thorough investigation of the propulsion anomaly, followed by design modifications and additional testing. The company has indicated that it will conduct a detailed analysis of the flight data and will implement any necessary changes before proceeding with the next flight. The timeline for the next launch is uncertain, but the rapid‑iteration model suggests that another launch could be scheduled within a few months.

The outcome of the investigation will also have implications for the broader space industry. If SpaceX can demonstrate a robust safety culture and reliable performance, it could accelerate the adoption of reusable launch vehicles and reduce the cost of access to space. However, if the investigation reveals systemic safety issues, it could prompt regulators to impose stricter oversight on commercial launch providers.

The event also highlights the importance of transparency and communication. SpaceX’s public statements have been limited, and the company has not released detailed technical data. This lack of transparency has raised concerns among regulators and investors, and it may prompt calls for greater disclosure of flight data and safety information.

In the long term, the Starship program will play a critical role in NASA’s Artemis program and in the broader effort to establish a sustainable presence in space. The company’s ability to deliver reliable, cost‑effective launch services will be essential in achieving these goals. The recent anomaly underscores the challenges of balancing rapid innovation with safety, and it will be a key factor in shaping the future of commercial spaceflight.

The Starship’s first orbital flight, while a historic milestone, remains a reminder that even breakthrough technology must be tempered by rigorous testing and oversight.
