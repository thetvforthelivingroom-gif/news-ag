SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> SpaceX’s Starship, the world’s largest reusable rocket, finally reached orbit on its 15th test flight, but the mission cut short after a brief stint aloft—underscoring both the company’s bold leap toward Mars and the stubborn technical and regulatory hurdles that still lie ahead.

What happened

On May 5, 2024, SpaceX launched Starship SN15 from Boca Chica, Texas, in a much‑anticipated attempt to achieve the first orbital flight of the vehicle. The rocket ascended past the Kármán line, placing the vehicle into a low Earth orbit of roughly 400 km altitude. However, after only a few minutes of orbital operations, the flight was terminated by an automated abort sequence. Investigations point to a propellant‑feed issue that caused a loss of thrust, but a structural anomaly may have also contributed to the early termination [T2].

The launch was the culmination of a decade‑long development program that began with the SN8 test flight in 2019, which ended in a dramatic explosion during the final seconds of ascent. Subsequent iterations—SN9, SN10, SN11, SN12, SN13, and SN14—refined the design, each addressing the failures of the last. By the time SN15 flew, the vehicle had been modified to use a new heat‑shield design, an updated flight‑control system, and a more robust propellant‑feed architecture [T3].

Despite the early abort, the flight demonstrated that Starship can reach orbit and perform orbital maneuvers, a critical milestone that validates the vehicle’s thrust‑to‑weight ratio, guidance, and control systems. The data gathered will inform the next iteration, SN16, which is slated to build on SN15’s lessons and aim for a longer orbital duration [T2].

Background and context

Starship’s journey began in 2019 with the SN8 test flight, a 16‑second flight that ended in a catastrophic explosion during the final stage of ascent. The failure highlighted issues with the vehicle’s heat shield, propellant feed system, and control algorithms. Each subsequent test flight addressed these shortcomings, with SN9 and SN10 improving the heat‑shield design, and SN11 and SN12 refining the flight‑control software. SN13 and SN14 introduced a new “cold‑gas” attitude‑control system and a more robust propellant‑feed system, respectively [T3].

The decision to launch SN15 into orbit represented a strategic shift for SpaceX. Rather than continuing incremental sub‑orbital tests, the company opted to push the vehicle into orbit to validate its full flight envelope. This move aligns with Elon Musk’s long‑term vision of making life multiplanetary, with Starship serving as the primary vehicle for crewed missions to Mars and beyond [T2].

Regulatory oversight has been a significant factor in the program’s progression. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) granted SpaceX a launch license for the SN15 flight, subject to compliance with safety and environmental regulations. NASA has also provided oversight, particularly as SpaceX intends to use Starship for crewed missions under the Artemis program. The rapid‑iteration approach of SpaceX—launching a new test vehicle every few weeks—has challenged traditional regulatory frameworks that assume longer development cycles [T2].

Investor sentiment has been cautiously optimistic. Following the SN15 launch, SpaceX’s valuation surged, reflecting confidence in the company’s ability to deliver a reusable heavy‑lift vehicle. However, the early termination of the flight also raised concerns about the cost of repeated test failures and the potential impact on timelines for crewed missions [T3].

The competitive landscape is also evolving. Blue Origin’s New Glenn and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan are both developing heavy‑lift launch vehicles that could compete with Starship in the commercial and government markets. NASA’s contract for the Artemis lander, awarded to SpaceX, positions the company as a key player in the lunar exploration program, but also subjects it to additional scrutiny and performance requirements [T3].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Technical triumph and caution

From a technical standpoint, the SN15 flight is a triumph. The vehicle successfully achieved orbit, performed orbital maneuvers, and demonstrated the ability to return to Earth—albeit with an early abort. The data collected will be invaluable for refining the propellant‑feed system and structural design. However, the cause of the early termination—whether a propellant‑feed failure or a structural anomaly—highlights the inherent risks of pushing the boundaries of rocket technology. SpaceX’s “fail‑fast” approach accelerates development but also increases the likelihood of costly setbacks [T2].

Regulatory and safety considerations

Regulators have expressed concern that SpaceX’s rapid testing cadence may outpace the ability of oversight bodies to assess safety risks. The FAA and NASA have emphasized the importance of rigorous safety reviews, especially as the company moves toward crewed missions. The SN15 flight’s early termination underscores the need for robust monitoring systems and contingency plans [T2].

Investor and market dynamics

The commercial space market is becoming increasingly crowded. Blue Origin, ULA, and other players are investing heavily in reusable launch vehicles. SpaceX’s progress with Starship could shift market dynamics, potentially giving the company a competitive edge in launch services and crewed missions. However, investors are also wary of the financial risks associated with repeated test failures, which can erode confidence and delay revenue generation [T3].

Public perception and the spirit of exploration

Public enthusiasm for space exploration remains high, fueled by the prospect of human missions to Mars and the Moon. The SN15 flight, despite its early termination, captured the imagination of many, reinforcing SpaceX’s image as an audacious innovator. Yet, the public also demands accountability and safety, especially when human lives are at stake. The balance between bold ambition and responsible execution will shape SpaceX’s reputation moving forward [T2].

What to watch (future implications)

Upcoming test flights

SpaceX plans to launch SN16 and subsequent vehicles in the coming months. These flights will aim to extend orbital duration, test new propulsion configurations, and validate improvements to the propellant‑feed system. Successful orbital flights will bring SpaceX closer to its goal of a fully reusable heavy‑lift vehicle capable of carrying crew and cargo to Mars [T2].

The FAA and NASA may revise their regulatory frameworks to accommodate rapid‑iteration testing while ensuring safety. This could involve new licensing procedures, real‑time monitoring requirements, and stricter post‑flight investigations. The outcome of these regulatory changes will impact not only SpaceX but the broader commercial space industry [T2].

Market competition

Blue Origin’s New Glenn and ULA’s Vulcan are also advancing their reusable launch programs. The pace of development and the ability to secure government contracts—such as NASA’s Artemis program—will determine which company gains a dominant position in the heavy‑lift market. SpaceX’s early orbital success with Starship could give it a competitive advantage, but competitors are also investing heavily in technology and infrastructure [T3].

Investor confidence

SpaceX’s valuation and funding prospects will continue to hinge on the success of its Starship program. Each successful orbital flight reduces the perceived risk and increases investor confidence. Conversely, repeated failures could dampen enthusiasm and delay the company’s ambitious timelines for crewed Mars missions [T3].

Human spaceflight

The ultimate goal of the Starship program is to enable crewed missions to the Moon and Mars. The SN15 flight’s orbital achievement, despite the early abort, is a critical step toward that objective. NASA’s Artemis lander contract with SpaceX ties the company’s commercial ambitions to national space policy, creating a high-stakes environment where technical success, regulatory compliance, and public trust must all align [T3].

In sum, SpaceX’s first orbital flight of Starship marks a watershed moment in reusable rocket technology, but it also highlights the complex interplay of engineering, regulation, market forces, and public expectation that will shape the future of human spaceflight.
