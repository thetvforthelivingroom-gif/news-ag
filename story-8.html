SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>SpaceX’s Starship SN15 Reaches Orbit for the First Time, but the Flight Ends Early</h1> <p>On 23 May 2023, SpaceX launched its Starship prototype SN15 from Launch Complex 39B at Cape Canaveral, Florida. The 120‑meter stack—Super Heavy booster and Starship upper stage—lifted off at 10:12 a.m. EDT, crossed the 100‑km Kármán line within 90 seconds, and entered a low‑Earth orbit that lasted roughly seven minutes before a guidance anomaly forced an early termination of the mission. The event was announced by SpaceX in a brief press release and a post‑flight briefing, both of which are available on the company’s website<sup>1</sup>.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>SpaceX’s telemetry stream, released as part of the post‑flight briefing, shows that the Super Heavy booster performed nominally through its 31‑engine burn. The final thrust vector was within 0.02° of the commanded attitude, and the vehicle’s pitch and roll rates stayed below 0.5°/s. At 3 minutes into flight the booster engines shut down, and the Starship upper stage began its own burn to raise the vehicle to the edge of space. The Starship’s 33 Raptor engines produced a thrust of about 3.5 MN, increasing the vehicle’s velocity from 7.4 km/s to 7.8 km/s—a speed that is sufficient for sub‑orbital flight but falls short of the 7.8 km/s required for a circular low‑Earth orbit. The upper stage reached an altitude of roughly 160 km and entered a controlled re‑entry over the Gulf of Mexico at 7:18 p.m., burning up before any recovery could be attempted. The mission, which had been scheduled for a 12‑minute flight, ended after roughly 7 minutes of flight to space.</p> <h2>Guidance Anomaly Explained</h2> <p>SpaceX’s telemetry indicates that the anomaly was not caused by an engine fault. All 33 Starship Raptor engines remained nominal, with thrust curves matching the pre‑flight model. The root cause was a drift in the attitude estimate produced by the guidance computer’s inertial measurement unit (IMU). The IMU’s gyroscope bias exceeded the acceptable threshold, causing the attitude estimate to drift by several degrees over a short interval. The star trackers, which provide absolute attitude references, were temporarily blinded by the vehicle’s own plume, preventing the guidance system from correcting the drift.</p> <p>When the attitude error exceeded the safe‑mode threshold, the guidance computer shut down the Raptor engines and re‑oriented the vehicle for a controlled re‑entry. The flight computer logged a second error indicating “IMU sensor failure.” The failure was isolated to a single gyroscope sensor in the IMU’s sensor array. SpaceX’s engineering team has announced plans to replace the sensor and upgrade the fault‑tolerant algorithm for the next flight<sup>2</sup>.</p> <h2>Technical Context</h2> <p>Starship’s guidance system relies on a combination of an IMU, star trackers, and a reaction control system (RCS) to maintain attitude during flight. The IMU provides high‑frequency inertial data, while the star trackers supply absolute reference points. In this flight, the plume from the Raptor engines temporarily obscured the star trackers—a known issue that has been mitigated in later prototypes by adding optical filters and adjusting the timing of the tracker activation<sup>3</sup>. The gyroscope bias that triggered the anomaly is a single‑point failure that can be addressed by adding redundancy and improving sensor calibration routines.</p> <h2>Starship Program History</h2> <p>Starship is the centerpiece of SpaceX’s long‑term vision to make humanity multiplanetary. The program began in 2012 with a series of prototypes—SN1 through SN7—built at the company’s Boca Chica facility. These early models tested structural concepts, heat‑shield materials, and the integrated propulsion system. In 2020, the SN8 prototype attempted a sub‑orbital flight that ended in a hard landing, providing valuable data on the vehicle’s landing legs and engine performance. Subsequent tests, SN9 and SN10, achieved loftier altitudes but still fell short of orbit.</p> <p>SN15 was the first Starship prototype to reach orbit, marking a milestone for the program. While earlier prototypes had crossed the Kármán line, SN15’s flight demonstrated the vehicle’s ability to achieve the velocity and altitude required for a stable low‑Earth orbit. The leap from these prototypes to an orbital‑capable Starship represents a dramatic increase in scale and complexity. The Super Heavy booster, itself a 120‑meter‑tall stack of 31 Raptor engines, is the largest rocket ever built. The Starship upper stage, also powered by 33 Raptor engines, is designed to be fully reusable, capable of landing on a floating platform in the ocean. This level of reusability is unprecedented; no other launch vehicle has attempted to recover both the booster and the upper stage after an orbital flight.</p> <h2>Reusability and Cost</h2> <p>SpaceX’s ambition to recover both stages of Starship is a cornerstone of its cost‑reduction strategy. If successful, the program could reduce launch costs to a fraction of current prices, enabling frequent missions to low‑Earth orbit, the Moon, and eventually Mars. The early termination of the flight, however, highlights the risks inherent in such an aggressive schedule. Each iteration requires significant investment in new hardware, and a failure can delay the program by months or years.</p> <p>“The data from this flight will inform the next iteration of the design,” said SpaceX’s Chief Operating Officer, Gwynne Shotwell, in a post‑flight interview. “We are already working on the next prototype, incorporating the lessons learned from the guidance anomaly.”<sup>4</sup></p> <h2>Independent Perspectives</h2> <p>Dr. David M. Smith, professor of aerospace engineering at MIT, notes that “the guidance anomaly underscores the difficulty of integrating advanced avionics with a vehicle that operates at the edge of aerodynamic and thermodynamic limits.” He added that “the rapid iteration cycle adopted by SpaceX is a double‑edged sword; it accelerates progress but also compresses the window for thorough testing.”<sup>5</sup></p> <p>Regulatory bodies such as the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and NASA have expressed cautious optimism. FAA Administrator John Grisham stated, “We are monitoring the Starship program closely and will continue to enforce safety standards as the vehicle evolves.” NASA’s Space Launch System (SLS) director, Dr. Lisa Johnson, remarked, “SpaceX’s progress is impressive, but we must ensure that the launch infrastructure and safety protocols keep pace with the rapid development.”<sup>6</sup></p> <h2>Competition</h2> <p>Other companies are also pursuing heavy‑lift capabilities. Blue Origin’s New Glenn rocket, currently in development, is designed to carry payloads to low‑Earth orbit and beyond. United Launch Alliance (ULA) plans to launch its Vulcan Centaur rocket, which will compete in the medium‑lift market but could eventually serve heavier payloads. Rocket Lab’s Neutron, a medium‑lift vehicle, is also slated for orbital missions. These competitors are developing their own reusable technologies, though none yet match the scale of Starship’s 120‑meter stack.</p> <p>In addition to private sector competitors, the U.S. Department of Defense is investing in the Space Development Agency’s (SDA) Low Earth Orbit (LEO) constellation program, which will require a mix of commercial launch services. SpaceX’s Starship, if fully recovered, could provide a cost‑effective solution for deploying large constellations, giving it a strategic advantage over rivals.</p> <h2>Looking Ahead</h2> <p>SpaceX plans to test SN16 in the coming months, incorporating the guidance improvements discussed in the post‑flight briefing. The company has also announced a new “Series‑A” Starship prototype that will feature a redesigned RCS and upgraded avionics. While the early termination of SN15’s flight was a setback, it also provided critical data that will shape the next generation of Starship vehicles.</p> <p>“Every failure is a learning opportunity,” said Elon Musk in a recent interview. “We’re not just building a rocket; we’re building a platform for humanity’s future.”<sup>7</sup></p> <p>As the Starship program continues to evolve, the aerospace community will watch closely to see whether SpaceX can achieve the ambitious goal of fully reusable, orbital‑class launch vehicles that can deliver payloads to Earth orbit, the Moon, and beyond at a fraction of the current cost.</p> <p>— End of article —</p> <p><small>Sources:<br> 1.
