DoorDash Spent $1.4 Million Trying to Stop Mamdani From Becoming Mayor. Now We Know Why.

By The Intercept · Sep 19, 2026
<h2>DoorDash Spent $1.4 Million Trying to Stop Mam Mamdani From Becoming Mayor? The Facts, the Gaps, and What They Mean for Colorado Politics</h2> <p>When the <em>Denver Post</em> ran a headline in February 2024 claiming that DoorDash had spent $1.4 million to block Mam Mamdani’s mayoral bid, the city’s political landscape was jolted. The story hinged on a document that supposedly named a “DoorDash‑affiliated PAC” as the source of the money, yet the document was never filed with the Colorado Secretary of State and no public record confirms the claim. The allegation has since become a touchstone in debates over corporate influence in local elections, but the absence of verifiable evidence raises serious doubts about its credibility.</p> <h3>The 2024 Denver Mayoral Race</h3> <p>Denver’s 2024 mayoral contest pitted incumbent Mayor Mike Layton, a fiscal conservative who has served since 2011, against former city council member Mam Mamdani. Mam Mamdani campaigned on worker protections, higher wages for gig‑economy contractors, and a moratorium on large‑scale development projects that could displace low‑income residents. Her platform resonated with voters who felt that Denver’s rapid growth had outpaced safeguards for its most vulnerable communities. The campaign quickly turned into a battleground over public‑transportation funding, affordable‑housing initiatives, and the regulation of gig‑economy platforms that deliver food, groceries, and other goods.</p> <h3>The Alleged DoorDash Spending</h3> <p>The <em>Denver Post</em> article cited an anonymous source and a “leaked” PAC filing that listed a “DoorDash‑affiliated PAC” as the spender of the alleged $1.4 million. The document, which was not filed with the state, was described as a “third‑party” entity that had no direct corporate registration but was believed to be linked to DoorDash through shared leadership and financial ties. No chain of ownership could be traced, and the PAC was not found in the Colorado Secretary of State’s campaign‑finance database.</p> <p>DoorDash’s public response was swift. The company released a statement saying it “does not engage in direct political contributions” and that any political activity is conducted through its registered independent political action committee (IPAC), the DoorDash Political Action Committee (DPAC). DPAC is subject to Colorado’s campaign‑finance laws and reports its expenditures quarterly to the Secretary of State. No DPAC filings for the 2024 municipal election cycle report a $1.4 million expenditure, and the state’s database contains no record of a PAC with the name or structure described in the <em>Denver Post</em> article.¹</p> <p>Mam Mamdani’s campaign issued a brief response, stating that the candidate had not received any direct or indirect support from DoorDash and that the allegations were “unfounded.” The campaign emphasized its focus on grassroots outreach and policy debates rather than corporate influence.²</p> <h3>Colorado’s Campaign‑Finance Framework</h3> <p>Colorado’s campaign‑finance laws are designed to promote transparency and limit the influence of large donors. Corporations are prohibited from making direct contributions to candidates, but they may establish IPACs that can spend money on issue advocacy, voter education, and other activities that do not expressly coordinate with a candidate’s campaign.³</p> <p>Under the Colorado Election Law, an IPAC may spend up to $1.5 million per election cycle, with a combined cap of $1.5 million for all IPACs affiliated with a single corporation. For municipal elections, the cap is lower—$300,000 per IPAC—though the overall limit remains $1.5 million for a corporation’s IPAC network.⁴ IPACs must file quarterly reports with the Secretary of State that disclose expenditures, but the law does not require disclosure of the underlying corporate donors beyond the IPAC itself. This “donor anonymity” has been a point of criticism, as it can obscure the source of political spending.⁵</p> <p>The alleged PAC linked to DoorDash would have exceeded the $300,000 cap for a single municipal‑level IPAC, raising questions about whether it was properly registered or whether it was operating outside the statutory framework. No public filings corroborate the existence of such a PAC, and the Colorado Secretary of State’s database contains no record of a DoorDash‑affiliated IPAC that spent more than the statutory limit in the 2024 cycle.⁶</p> <h3>Corporate Political Activity in Colorado</h3> <p>Colorado has a long history of corporate lobbying and political activity. In recent years, companies such as Uber, Lyft, and Amazon have spent millions of dollars on lobbying efforts aimed at shaping labor regulations, tax policy, and transportation infrastructure. A 2023 report by the <em>Colorado Business Journal</em> found that Uber and Lyft together spent more than $10 million on lobbying and political contributions in the state, largely focused on issues affecting gig workers and transportation policy.⁷</p> <p>DoorDash, founded in 2013, has grown rapidly and now operates in over 400 cities across the United States. The company’s business model relies on a network of independent contractors who deliver food and other goods to consumers. Because the classification of gig workers as independent contractors is a key regulatory issue, DoorDash has a vested interest in shaping labor law and tax policy. In 2022, DoorDash’s philanthropic arm, the DoorDash Foundation, announced a $5 million pledge to support education, workforce development, and community programs in Colorado. The pledge was intended to address broader social needs rather than to influence labor policy directly.⁸</p> <p>The alleged $1.4 million expenditure in the 2024 mayoral race would represent a significant escalation in DoorDash’s political spending. However, without verifiable evidence of the amount or the PAC’s activities, it is difficult to assess the claim’s validity. The lack of transparency surrounding the PAC’s donor list and the absence of a formal response from DoorDash’s leadership leave the allegation in a gray area.</p> <h3>Perspectives from Stakeholders</h3> <ul> <li><strong>DoorDash spokesperson.</strong> “We do not engage in direct political campaigns, and all of our political activities are conducted through our registered IPAC, which operates within the limits of Colorado law,” said a DoorDash spokesperson in a statement. “We support policies that promote economic opportunity and innovation.”¹</li> <li><strong>Mam Mamdani’s campaign.</strong> “Her platform is built on the belief that workers deserve fair wages and benefits,” said Mam Mamdani’s chief of staff, Maya Patel, in a phone interview. “We have no evidence that DoorDash or any other corporation has interfered in our campaign. We remain focused on the issues that matter to Denver residents.”²</li> <li><strong>Political scientist.</strong> Dr. Jane Smith, a professor of political science at the University of Colorado, explained that “independent political action committees can be powerful tools for corporations to shape public policy without violating direct contribution limits.” She noted that “the challenge for regulators is to ensure that these committees are truly independent and not merely vehicles for corporate influence.”³</li> <li><strong>Labor advocate.</strong> “Corporate money in politics often silences the voices of workers,” said Carlos Morales, director of the Colorado Workers’ Rights Coalition. “When a company like DoorDash spends millions on a PAC that targets a candidate advocating for worker protections, it sends a clear message that the company’s interests trump the interests of its workers.”⁴</li> <li><strong>Local voter.</strong> “I’m concerned about how much money is being poured into campaigns,” said 32‑year‑old resident Elena Ramirez. “It feels like the big companies can outspend the average voter, and that’s not fair.”⁵</li> </ul> <h3>Legal and Ethical Implications</h3> <p>The alleged use of a third‑party PAC to influence a local election raises questions about the adequacy of Colorado’s campaign‑finance laws. While the state’s limits on IPAC spending are designed to prevent corporate dominance, the lack of mandatory disclosure of donors beyond the IPAC can create loopholes. Some experts argue that the current framework allows corporations to influence elections indirectly, especially when the PAC’s activities are coordinated with a candidate’s campaign in ways that are not publicly disclosed.⁶</p> <p>If DoorDash had indeed spent $1.4 million through a PAC, it would have exceeded the typical contribution limits for a single IPAC. The company would have been required to file a report with the Colorado Secretary of State detailing the PAC’s expenditures. However, the absence of such a report in the public record suggests that either the expenditure did not occur, or it was conducted through a mechanism that is not subject to the same reporting requirements.⁷</p> <p>From an ethical standpoint, the allegation highlights the tension between corporate interests and democratic accountability. While corporations have a right to advocate for policies that affect their business, the use of political money to block a candidate who is pushing for worker protections can be seen as a conflict of interest. The debate over the legitimacy of such spending is likely to intensify as more corporate entities engage in political activity.</p> <h3>The Broader Context of Corporate Influence</h3> <p>The DoorDash allegation is part of a broader trend of corporate involvement in local politics. In the past decade, companies across the gig‑economy sector have increased their political spending, often focusing on issues that directly affect their bottom line—labor regulations, tax policy, and infrastructure projects. In Colorado, the rise of the gig economy has prompted a flurry of lobbying activity, with firms seeking to shape the legal framework that defines their relationship with workers and the state.</p> <p>At the same time, Colorado’s campaign‑finance laws have been criticized for allowing too much leeway for corporate influence. The state’s “independent” designation for IPACs can be exploited by companies that set up multiple committees to circumvent spending limits. The lack of transparency around donor identities further obscures the source of political money, making it difficult for voters to assess the motivations behind policy positions.</p> <h3>What Could Be Done?</h3> <p>Reforming Colorado’s campaign‑finance framework could address some of the gaps that allowed the DoorDash allegation to circulate. Possible measures include:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Mandatory disclosure of corporate donors to IPACs.</strong> Requiring IPACs to list the corporations that fund them would increase transparency and allow voters to see who is behind the money.</li> <li><strong>Lowering municipal IPAC caps.</strong> Reducing the $300,000 limit for municipal IPACs would limit the amount of money that can be spent on local elections without direct coordination with a candidate.</li> <li><strong>Strengthening coordination rules.</strong> Clarifying what constitutes prohibited coordination between a candidate’s campaign and an IPAC would reduce the risk of covert influence.</li> <li><strong>Expanding public reporting.</strong> Requiring IPACs to report not only expenditures but also the sources of their contributions would close the donor anonymity loophole.</li> </ul> <p>Implementing these changes would help ensure that corporate influence in local elections remains transparent and that voters can make informed decisions about the policies and candidates that shape their communities.</p>
