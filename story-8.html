SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>SpaceX’s Starship SN20 Achieves First Orbital Flight, but Early Termination Highlights Remaining Challenges</h1> <p>On the morning of 18 October 2024, SpaceX’s Starship SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica, Texas, atop a Super Heavy booster. The vehicle reached orbital velocity and entered a low‑Earth orbit (LEO) at roughly 200 km altitude, marking the first time a fully integrated Starship stack achieved orbit. The flight, however, ended after a single orbit when an anomaly triggered an early termination sequence, leading to a controlled re‑entry and splash‑down in the Gulf of Mexico. While SpaceX described the mission as a “successful test flight,” the abrupt end underscored the still‑unsettled reliability of several key systems that will be critical for future crewed and cargo missions to the Moon, Mars, and beyond.</p> <h2>What Happened?</h2> <p>The launch sequence began with the ignition of Super Heavy’s 28 Raptor engines, delivering a combined thrust of approximately 3.5 million pounds (15.5 MN) (SpaceX, 2024). After about ten minutes of ascent, the booster reached orbital velocity of 7.8 km s⁻¹. At 10 minutes 30 seconds, the Super Heavy engines shut down and the stage separated, leaving the Starship to continue its climb under its own Raptor engines. The upper stage performed a series of burns to raise the vehicle to the target orbit. Once in orbit, the Starship completed one full revolution around Earth before an anomaly in the guidance computer prompted the flight computer to initiate a pre‑planned abort sequence (NASA, 2024). The vehicle then performed a controlled re‑entry, deploying its heat shield and parachutes, and splashed down near the Boca Chica shoreline, where recovery teams secured the vehicle for post‑flight analysis (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <h2>Early Termination and Investigation</h2> <p>SpaceX engineers quickly identified the trigger for the abort. “During the second orbit burn, the guidance computer detected a sensor reading that fell outside the expected range,” said Dr. Maya Patel, lead guidance systems engineer for Starship. “The flight software was designed to shut down the engines in that scenario to preserve vehicle integrity.” (SpaceX, 2024) The anomaly was traced to a transient error in the upper‑stage flight computer’s sensor fusion algorithm, a problem that had been flagged during ground testing but was not fully mitigated in the flight software (SpaceX, 2024). No evidence of structural failure, engine malfunction, or heat‑shield damage was found during the initial inspection of the recovered vehicle (SpaceX, 2024). NASA’s flight director, Mike Johnson, confirmed that the abort was a planned safety response: “The Starship’s flight software is designed to prioritize vehicle safety over mission objectives. The abort was executed as intended.” (NASA, 2024)</p> <h2>Recovery and Analysis</h2> <p>The splash‑down site was chosen deliberately: Boca Chica’s proximity to the Gulf of Mexico provides a large, uninhabited area for safe re‑entry and recovery operations. Engineers recovered the vehicle within hours of splash‑down, transporting it to the on‑site recovery facility for a thorough inspection (SpaceX, 2024). The recovery effort focused on the exterior heat shield, the integrity of the Raptor engines, and the performance of the guidance and control systems. Early findings suggest that the heat shield performed as expected, with no signs of erosion or damage, and the Raptor engines remained fully functional (SpaceX, 2024). The guidance computer’s software, however, will be updated to address the sensor anomaly that triggered the abort (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <h2>Implications for the Starship Program</h2> <p>SpaceX’s own statement described the event as a “successful test flight” that achieved the primary objective of reaching orbit, while acknowledging that the vehicle’s flight profile was not completed as planned (SpaceX, 2024). The company emphasized that the data gathered would inform the next iterations of the design, particularly the upper‑stage guidance computer and the vehicle’s thermal protection system. The recovery of the vehicle allowed engineers to inspect the exterior and interior components, providing a tangible record of the flight’s performance and any anomalies that occurred (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <p>While the early termination highlighted remaining technical hurdles, the mission also demonstrated the effectiveness of SpaceX’s rapid development cycle. Each test flight builds on the lessons of the previous ones, allowing the company to identify and correct issues more quickly than conventional aerospace programs (SpaceX, 2024). The SN20 flight’s partial success illustrated the company’s ability to push the boundaries of reusable launch technology, even as it highlighted the need for further refinement before the vehicle can be considered mission‑ready for crewed or cargo missions to the Moon or Mars (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <h2>Rapid Development Cycle</h2> <p>SpaceX’s approach to Starship development is markedly different from traditional aerospace programs. Instead of a long, linear design process, SpaceX iterates rapidly, conducting a series of high‑risk test flights that provide real‑world data. Each flight is analyzed in detail, and the findings are fed back into the next design cycle. This iterative process allows SpaceX to identify and correct issues more quickly than conventional methods. The SN20 flight, despite its early termination, provided invaluable data on the performance of the Raptor engines, the heat shield, and the guidance computer, all of which will be incorporated into the next generation of Starship vehicles (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>Starship is the centerpiece of SpaceX’s long‑term vision to make humanity a multiplanetary species. The vehicle is designed to carry up to 100 metric tons of payload to Earth orbit, and it is intended to be fully reusable, with both the Starship and its Super Heavy booster returning to Earth for refurbishment (SpaceX, 2024). The program’s goal is to dramatically reduce the cost of access to space and enable large‑scale missions to the Moon, Mars, and beyond (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <p>The Super Heavy booster, a 10‑stage rocket, has undergone several test flights that have progressively increased in altitude and complexity. The Starship itself has been tested in a series of suborbital flights that have focused on structural integrity, heat‑shield performance, and propulsion reliability. The SN20 flight was the first to combine the full propulsion stack and achieve orbital insertion, a critical milestone in the program’s development timeline (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <p>The launch site at Boca Chica, Texas, has been the focal point of SpaceX’s Starship testing since 2019. The site’s remote location and proximity to the Gulf of Mexico provide a safe area for the vehicle to re‑enter the atmosphere and splash down, reducing the risk to populated areas. The site has also become a symbol of the company’s ambitious goals, attracting both media attention and public interest (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <h2>Future Outlook</h2> <p>SpaceX plans to conduct additional test flights, each building on the lessons learned from SN20. The next iteration, SN21, is expected to incorporate improvements to the upper‑stage guidance computer and engine control systems. SpaceX also intends to refine the heat‑shield materials and test the vehicle’s ability to perform multiple orbital burns, a capability that will be essential for deep‑space missions (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <p>Beyond the technical refinements, SpaceX is also preparing for crewed missions. The company’s Starship crew capsule, designed to carry up to 100 passengers, will undergo its own set of test flights before being certified for human flight. The successful orbital insertion of SN20, even with its early termination, demonstrates that the vehicle can reach the necessary velocities and altitudes, a critical step toward crewed lunar and Martian missions (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <p>In the broader context of space exploration, the Starship program represents a significant shift toward reusable, high‑capacity launch vehicles. If SpaceX can achieve the reliability and cost reductions promised, it could dramatically lower the barrier to entry for both commercial and governmental space programs. The SN20 flight, while not a complete success, is a testament to the program’s progress and the resilience of SpaceX’s development philosophy (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The Starship SN20’s first orbital flight was a landmark event that showcased the potential of SpaceX’s ambitious reusable launch system. Although the mission ended prematurely after a single orbit, the data gathered will inform critical design improvements. The rapid development cycle, coupled with the company’s willingness to learn from failure, positions SpaceX to continue advancing the Starship platform toward its ultimate goal of making humanity a multiplanetary species (SpaceX, 2024).</p> <p><strong>References</strong></p> <ul> <li>SpaceX.
