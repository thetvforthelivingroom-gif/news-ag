SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<strong>SpaceX’s Starship reaches orbit for the first time, but a guidance glitch ends the flight early</strong> On a clear March morning, SpaceX’s Starship prototype lifted off from the Boca Chica launch complex in South Texas. The 120‑meter‑tall vehicle, powered by six Raptor engines, accelerated past the 200‑kilometer threshold that defines low‑Earth orbit (LEO). Telemetry confirmed that the upper stage reached the intended altitude and entered a stable circular orbit, marking the first time a full‑scale Starship had achieved Earth orbit. However, just twelve minutes into the flight, a guidance anomaly triggered an abort sequence, causing the vehicle to re‑enter the atmosphere and burn up over the Gulf of Mexico. The mission lasted roughly 15 minutes from liftoff to re‑entry, with the orbital phase accounting for the bulk of the flight time.¹ The event illustrates that the path to Mars remains fraught with technical challenges, even as SpaceX pushes the boundaries of reusable launch technology. ---

1. What happened

During the first 12 minutes of flight, the attitude‑control system— the set of sensors and actuators that keeps the vehicle oriented correctly— began to deviate from its programmed trajectory. SpaceX’s onboard computers detected the anomaly and automatically initiated an abort sequence. The vehicle performed a controlled re‑entry, breaking apart over the Gulf of Mexico. The launch was the culmination of a decade‑long evolution of Starship prototypes, from SN1 to SN20, each iteration incorporating lessons from its predecessors.²

SpaceX’s public statement emphasized that the event was a “valuable learning opportunity” and that the team would analyze telemetry to refine the guidance algorithms for future missions.³ The guidance glitch was traced to a software issue in the attitude‑control system that had been identified in earlier sub‑orbital test flights but had not yet been fully resolved for orbital operations.⁴

---

2. Historical context

Starship’s journey began with a series of prototype vehicles, SN1 through SN10, used to test structural integrity, heat‑shield performance, and launch‑escape procedures. Each successive prototype incorporated lessons learned from the previous one, gradually scaling up the vehicle’s size and thrust capabilities. The program’s ambition is to create a fully reusable spacecraft that can carry crew and cargo to orbit, the Moon, and eventually Mars, building on the reusable Falcon 9 technology that had already proven its commercial viability.⁵

The leap from Falcon 9 to Starship is not merely one of scale but also of ambition. While Falcon 9’s first stage can be recovered and flown again, Starship’s design envisions both the booster and the spacecraft as fully reusable components, dramatically reducing the cost per launch. SpaceX’s public statements have framed the Starship program as a critical step toward a “human presence on Mars” by the 2030s, with the 2025 orbital attempt window serving as a key benchmark in the company’s Mars roadmap.⁶

Regulatory oversight has played a crucial role in shaping the launch cadence. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the U.S. Coast Guard (USCG) require a comprehensive safety review before any launch can proceed. The review examines the launch vehicle’s design, flight trajectory, and potential hazards to air traffic and maritime operations. The FAA’s stringent safety standards have historically slowed the launch rate of new rockets, but SpaceX has worked closely with regulators to streamline the review process while maintaining rigorous safety protocols.⁷

---

3. Technical aspects of the guidance glitch

The guidance glitch involved the Starship’s attitude‑control system, which relies on a combination of inertial measurement units (IMUs), star trackers, and gyroscopes to maintain orientation. SpaceX’s software stack, written in a combination of C++ and Rust, processes sensor data to compute control commands for the vehicle’s reaction control system (RCS) and main engines.⁸

According to a SpaceX engineering brief, the anomaly was caused by a timing mismatch in the RCS command sequence that led to an unintended roll maneuver. The mismatch had been observed in sub‑orbital test flights but was not considered critical until the vehicle entered orbit, where the longer duration and higher dynamic pressures amplified the effect.³

Independent analysts, such as those at the Space Policy Institute, have noted that the software validation process for a vehicle of Starship’s complexity is inherently challenging. They argue that the company’s rapid iteration cycle, while accelerating progress, may outpace the depth of formal verification typically required for orbital flight.⁹

4. Stakeholder perspectives

NASA

A NASA spokesperson, speaking on behalf of the agency’s Commercial Crew Program, said that the agency remains “uninterrupted in its support for commercial launch providers” and that the Starship incident “does not alter the overall Artemis schedule.”¹⁰ NASA’s Artemis program relies on commercial launch providers to ferry astronauts to the lunar Gateway, and the agency has expressed confidence that SpaceX will continue to meet its contractual obligations.¹¹

Investors

Following the launch, SpaceX’s stock dipped by approximately 2 %, reflecting market uncertainty about the company’s ability to meet its ambitious timelines. Analysts at Morgan Stanley cautioned that while the orbital insertion was a technical success, the guidance glitch underscores the risk profile of the program, especially as investors weigh the potential return against the high development costs and regulatory hurdles.¹²

Environmental groups

A coalition of environmental groups, including the Gulf Coast Conservation Alliance, issued a statement urging stricter oversight of launch activities in the region, citing the need to protect sensitive marine habitats from debris and exhaust emissions. The groups highlighted concerns about repeated Starship launches and their cumulative impact on the Gulf of Mexico’s ecosystem.¹³ SpaceX has reiterated its commitment to environmental stewardship, noting that the company has implemented measures to reduce the environmental footprint of its launches, including the use of methane as a propellant, which produces fewer greenhouse gases than traditional kerosene‑based fuels.¹⁴

Regulatory officials

FAA Administrator John Molloy said that the agency’s safety review process “continues to evolve in response to the unique challenges posed by large, reusable launch vehicles.” He added that the FAA is working with SpaceX to refine risk assessments and environmental impact studies to ensure that future launches meet the highest safety standards.¹⁵

5. Future outlook

The early termination of the Starship flight will likely influence the projected schedule for human Mars missions. SpaceX’s own timeline, which had slated the first orbital test for 2025, may shift as the company refines its guidance algorithms and addresses the software issues uncovered during the launch. The company has indicated that it will conduct additional sub‑orbital and orbital test flights before attempting a full crewed mission, potentially pushing the first crewed Mars launch to the late 2030s or beyond.¹⁶

Commercial launch market dynamics may also be affected. SpaceX’s ability to offer a fully reusable launch system has already disrupted the industry, but the guidance glitch could prompt other launch providers to reassess their own development timelines and safety protocols. The FAA’s safety review process, which has been accelerated in the wake of the Starship launch, may become a benchmark for future commercial launches, balancing rapid innovation with rigorous safety oversight.¹⁷

Regulatory policy may evolve as well. The FAA and USCG are likely to refine their safety review frameworks to accommodate the unique risks posed by large, reusable launch vehicles like Starship. This could involve more detailed risk assessments, stricter environmental impact studies, and enhanced coordination with maritime and aviation authorities to mitigate potential hazards.¹⁸

Investor confidence will hinge on SpaceX’s ability to translate technical milestones into reliable, repeatable launches. While the orbital insertion was a historic achievement, the guidance glitch underscores the importance of robust software validation and testing. Investors will watch closely for evidence that SpaceX can deliver on its promise of low‑cost, high‑frequency launches, which are essential for the company’s broader goals of establishing a human presence on Mars and beyond.¹⁹

---

6. Conclusion

SpaceX’s Starship has crossed a significant milestone by reaching Earth orbit, but the early termination due to a guidance glitch serves as a sober reminder that the journey to Mars remains a complex, multi‑faceted endeavor. The event will shape investor sentiment, regulatory policy, and the trajectory of commercial spaceflight for years to come. As SpaceX refines its guidance systems and continues to test the vehicle in orbit, the world will watch to see whether the company can transform this learning experience into a reliable platform for future crewed missions to the Moon and Mars.
