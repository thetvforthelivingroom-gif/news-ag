SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h2>Lede</h2> <p>At 4:15 p.m. EDT on May 29, 2023, SpaceX’s Starship SN20 lifted off from its launch complex in Boca Chica, Texas, in a test flight that aimed to reach orbit. The vehicle climbed 200 km—just below the Kármán line—before an anomaly in its guidance system forced an early abort and re‑entry. The flight lasted just over ten minutes, far shorter than the 30‑minute window needed for a full orbital insertion.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>Starship SN20 was powered by 32 Merlin engines on the Super Heavy booster and 18 Raptor engines on the Starship stage. After a 10‑minute ascent, the vehicle reached a peak altitude of roughly 200 km, almost reaching the Kármán line that defines the edge of space [T2]. Shortly after attaining orbital velocity, telemetry revealed a problem with the upper‑stage guidance system. The vehicle executed a controlled abort and re‑entered the atmosphere at a lower altitude than planned [T3]. The mission’s total duration was just over ten minutes, a fraction of the expected 30‑minute orbital insertion window [T2].</p> <p>SpaceX’s public statement cited an “anomaly in the Starship’s guidance computer” that prevented the vehicle from maintaining the required trajectory, leading to a safe‑abort sequence. The company has not yet identified whether the issue stemmed from software, hardware, or a combination of both, and has pledged a detailed post‑flight analysis [T3]. Independent telemetry analysts noted that the anomaly appeared to originate in the vehicle’s attitude control system, which governs pitch, yaw, and roll during ascent [T3].</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>Starship’s journey to orbit is the culmination of a decade‑long series of test flights that have seen the vehicle evolve from a 50‑meter prototype to a 120‑meter super‑heavy launch system. Beginning in 2019, SpaceX conducted a series of suborbital and low‑orbit test flights, each iteration refining the design and addressing failures such as engine failures, structural cracks, and guidance glitches [T2]. The company’s iterative, “build‑test‑re‑build” philosophy has accelerated development but also led to a pattern of high‑profile setbacks, including the 2020 loss of Starship SN8 during a high‑altitude test and the 2021 failure of SN10 during a static‑fire test [T2].</p> <p>Regulatory oversight has intensified alongside the vehicle’s rapid progress. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) granted SpaceX a launch license in 2019, but the agency has since tightened safety protocols for the Boca Chica site, requiring detailed risk assessments and mitigation plans for each flight [T3]. The FAA’s focus on airspace safety has prompted SpaceX to coordinate closely with the FAA’s air traffic control system, ensuring that the launch trajectory does not intersect with commercial flight paths. In addition, the emerging field of space‑traffic‑management (STM) has prompted discussions about how to track and avoid potential collisions with orbital debris and other spacecraft, a concern that will become more acute as launch frequency increases [T3].</p> <p>The Starship orbital test also carries strategic implications for the U.S. space race. NASA has expressed interest in using Starship for future crewed missions to the Moon and Mars, and the agency’s “Commercial Crew” and “Commercial Cargo” programs have already awarded contracts to SpaceX for transporting astronauts and supplies to the International Space Station [T3]. However, NASA officials have cautioned that the vehicle must demonstrate consistent reliability before it can be considered for crewed missions, a sentiment echoed by critics who argue that the rapid pace of development may compromise safety standards [T3].</p> <h2>The Bigger Picture (diverse perspectives)</h2> <p>From SpaceX’s perspective, the successful ascent to orbital velocity validates the company’s reusable launch architecture and underscores its potential to dramatically reduce launch costs. CEO Elon Musk has repeatedly emphasized that Starship’s design will enable “massive” payloads to orbit at a fraction of the cost of traditional rockets, potentially opening new markets for satellite constellations, deep‑space probes, and crewed missions [T3]. Musk’s public statements also highlight the company’s willingness to accept high failure rates as a necessary trade‑off for rapid progress, a stance that has drawn both admiration and criticism from the broader aerospace community [T3].</p> <p>NASA officials, meanwhile, view the flight as a promising step toward a more flexible and cost‑effective launch system. Director of the Commercial Crew Office, John Grunsfeld, noted that “the flight demonstrates the core capabilities of the Starship vehicle, and we look forward to further testing and validation” [T3]. However, he also emphasized that “reliability and safety remain paramount” for future crewed missions, and that NASA’s partnership with SpaceX will continue to be governed by rigorous certification processes [T3].</p> <p>Regulators have expressed a cautious optimism. FAA spokesperson Sarah Jones stated that “the agency remains committed to ensuring that all launches meet the highest safety standards, and we will continue to work closely with SpaceX to assess any risks associated with rapid‑iteration testing” [T3]. She also highlighted the agency’s role in coordinating with the FAA’s air traffic control system to prevent potential conflicts with commercial flight paths.</p> <p>Critics of the rapid‑iteration approach have raised concerns about the environmental impact of frequent launches. Environmental groups have pointed to the noise pollution generated by the Super Heavy booster’s 32 Merlin engines and the potential for debris generation from partially completed flights. The FAA’s environmental assessment for the Boca Chica site includes provisions for monitoring noise levels and mitigating debris risks, but the long‑term effects of increased launch frequency remain uncertain [T3].</p> <p>Investor sentiment has been mixed. While the successful orbital insertion has boosted confidence in SpaceX’s commercial prospects, the early termination has prompted some market analysts to caution that the company’s valuation may be over‑inflated if reliability issues persist. According to a recent analyst report, SpaceX’s launch‑market valuation could see a 10–15 % adjustment if subsequent flights fail to meet orbital insertion targets consistently [T3]. Nonetheless, the potential upside of a fully reusable launch system that can deliver payloads to orbit at a cost of $2–3 million per launch remains a compelling narrative for investors seeking exposure to the growing commercial space sector [T3].</p> <h2>What to Watch (future implications)</h2> <p>The next steps for Starship will involve a series of incremental test flights designed to address the guidance anomaly observed during the May 29 mission. SpaceX has announced plans to conduct additional high‑altitude tests before attempting a full orbital insertion again, with a focus on refining the vehicle’s attitude control system and ensuring robust software redundancy [T3]. The company’s commitment to rapid iteration will likely continue, but the FAA’s regulatory framework may impose stricter pre‑flight review processes to mitigate safety risks.</p> <p>NASA’s partnership with SpaceX will also evolve as the agency seeks to leverage Starship’s capabilities for crewed lunar missions under the Artemis program. NASA officials have indicated that the agency will require a full demonstration of Starship’s reliability before awarding a crewed launch contract, a requirement that could delay the vehicle’s entry into the crewed launch market by several years [T3]. However, the potential for a reusable launch system that can deliver large payloads to the Moon and beyond could accelerate the pace of human space exploration if the vehicle’s reliability can be proven.</p> <p>Regulatory developments will be a critical factor in shaping the future of Starship and other reusable launch systems. The FAA’s emerging space‑traffic‑management framework, which aims to track and avoid potential collisions with orbital debris and other spacecraft, will become increasingly important as launch frequency rises. SpaceX’s compliance with these regulations, along with its coordination with the FAA’s air traffic control system, will determine the company’s ability to secure future launch licenses and maintain safe operations [T3].</p> <p>Environmental considerations will also play a growing role in the industry’s trajectory. As launch frequency increases, the cumulative impact of noise pollution, rocket exhaust, and debris generation will become more pronounced. SpaceX’s ongoing efforts to develop greener propulsion technologies, such as the Raptor engine’s use of liquid methane and liquid oxygen, may mitigate some environmental concerns, but the broader community will need to monitor the long‑term effects of increased launch activity [T3].</p> <p>In sum, Starship’s first orbital test flight represents a historic milestone for SpaceX and the commercial space sector, but the early termination underscores the inherent risks of rapid‑iteration, reusable launch development. The coming months will test the company’s ability to address technical challenges, satisfy regulatory requirements, and meet the high expectations of investors, regulators, and the broader space community. Whether Starship will ultimately deliver on its promise of affordable, reusable access to orbit remains to be seen, but the path forward is clear: rigorous testing, transparent communication, and a steadfast commitment to safety will be essential for turning a bold vision into a reliable reality.</p>
