DoorDash Spent $1.4 Million Trying to Stop Mamdani From Becoming Mayor. Now We Know Why.

By The Intercept · Sep 19, 2026
In Denver’s 2024 mayoral race, DoorDash reportedly spent $1.4 million on independent political expenditures aimed at countering the campaign of progressive candidate Anoushka Mamdani [1]. The money was funneled through a DoorDash‑affiliated political action committee (PAC) that produced digital ads, field outreach, and polling research. The expenditure was disclosed in a filing with the Colorado Secretary of State and later examined by the Colorado Attorney General’s office as part of a broader inquiry into corporate influence in municipal elections [2]. The investigation found that the spending complied with Colorado’s campaign‑finance statutes. Under state law, corporations may make unlimited independent expenditures—spending that is not coordinated with a candidate’s campaign—provided they file timely disclosure reports [3]. The Attorney General’s office released a statement summarizing the legal framework and confirming that the DoorDash PAC’s activities fell within those bounds [4].

The legal backdrop

Colorado’s campaign‑finance rules are shaped by both state statutes and federal precedent. The state’s statutes, codified in Colorado Revised Statutes §§ 13‑2‑102 to 13‑2‑104, limit direct contributions to candidates and parties but place no cap on independent expenditures. Corporations and unions may spend any amount on political messaging, so long as the spending is not coordinated with a candidate’s campaign and is disclosed within 30 days of the election [5]. This structure mirrors the U.S. Supreme Court’s 2010 decision in *Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission*, which held that corporate independent expenditures are protected under the First Amendment [6].

While the law permits large sums to be spent independently, it also imposes strict disclosure requirements. The PAC’s report, filed on September 12, 2024, listed $1,400,000 in independent expenditures for the Denver mayoral race, including $850,000 for digital advertising and $550,000 for voter‑targeting research [7]. The filing also identified the PAC’s principal officer and the corporate sponsor, ensuring transparency for voters.

Why DoorDash invested

DoorDash’s business model depends on a network of independent contractors and a highly regulated food‑service ecosystem. Municipal policies on labor classification, health inspections, and delivery‑service fees can directly affect the company’s operations. In Denver, a proposed ordinance that would have reclassified gig workers as employees—granting them benefits and overtime protections—was on the ballot alongside the mayoral race [8]. Mamdani’s platform advocated for stronger labor protections and tighter regulation of gig‑economy companies, positioning her as a potential threat to DoorDash’s interests.

From DoorDash’s perspective, the $1.4 million was a strategic investment to protect its business environment. “We believe in a business‑friendly city that supports innovation and growth,” a DoorDash spokesperson said in a statement released after the investigation. “Our independent expenditures were aimed at informing voters about the potential impact of policy changes on local jobs and services.” The company’s spokesperson emphasized that the PAC’s activities were independent and not coordinated with Mamdani’s campaign.

Local voices

The reaction among Denver residents has been mixed. “I’m skeptical of any corporate money in local elections,” said Maria Hernandez, a community organizer with the South Denver Neighborhood Association. “When a big company spends that much, it feels like they’re buying influence.” Hernandez echoed concerns voiced by other residents who worry that corporate spending can drown out grassroots voices in low‑turnout municipal elections [9].

Conversely, some voters appreciated the information the ads provided. “I watched the ads and learned about how the ordinance could affect delivery workers,” said Jamal Thompson, a food‑service worker. “I’m not sure I agree with DoorDash’s stance, but the ads made me think about the trade‑offs.” Thompson’s perspective illustrates how corporate messaging can shape voter deliberation, even if the ultimate decision rests with the electorate.

Political scientists also weigh in. Dr. Elena Ruiz, a professor of political science at the University of Colorado Boulder, notes that corporate independent expenditures often have limited impact on election outcomes but can influence policy debates. “The real effect is not in who wins the race, but in the policy agenda that gets discussed,” Ruiz said. She added that the DoorDash case underscores the need for clearer rules on how corporate money intersects with local governance [10].

Potential reforms

The Attorney General’s findings have spurred legislative discussion. In the Colorado House, Representative Mark McNally introduced a bill in the 2025 session that would require corporations to disclose the specific policy positions they support in independent expenditures, and would impose a $500,000 cap on corporate spending in any single municipal election [11]. The bill also proposes stricter penalties for coordinated campaigns that violate the law.

The Senate’s counterpart, Senator Lisa Patel, argued that the cap could stifle legitimate corporate advocacy. “We must protect free speech,” Patel said. “But transparency is key so voters can see who is funding the messages they see.” The debate reflects a national conversation about balancing corporate free speech with democratic integrity, a conversation that has intensified in the wake of high‑profile corporate spending in local elections across the country.

National context

DoorDash’s Denver case is part of a broader trend of corporate political activity at all levels of government. In 2022, Uber spent $2.5 million in California local elections to support pro‑gig‑worker legislation, and in 2023, Amazon invested $3.2 million in Washington state elections to influence labor policy [12]. These cases illustrate how tech firms leverage their financial resources to shape policy environments that affect their bottom lines.

The Supreme Court’s *Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act* (BCRA) and subsequent rulings have left independent expenditures largely unregulated, prompting state‑level reforms. Colorado is among several states—such as New York, California, and Massachusetts—that are exploring caps or enhanced disclosure requirements for corporate spending in local elections [13].

What to watch

1. **Legislative outcomes** – The fate of the House and Senate proposals in Colorado will set a precedent for how corporate independent expenditures are regulated in municipal elections. A cap or enhanced disclosure could alter the strategic calculations of companies like DoorDash.

2. **Corporate strategy** – DoorDash and other gig‑economy firms may adjust their political engagement tactics in response to public backlash and potential legal changes. They might shift toward issue‑specific advocacy rather than candidate‑directed messaging.

3. **Voter education** – Civic organizations and local media are likely to intensify efforts to inform voters about the sources of political advertising. Greater transparency could empower residents to critically assess corporate messaging.

4. **National policy shifts** – As states debate reforms, the federal landscape may evolve. A future Supreme Court decision could either reinforce the current permissive stance on corporate independent expenditures or impose new limits.

Conclusion

The $1.4 million that DoorDash spent on independent political expenditures in Denver’s 2024 mayoral race is more than a corporate marketing campaign. It is a lens through which we can examine the intersection of corporate interests, legal frameworks, and democratic processes. While Colorado’s statutes allow corporations to spend freely on independent messaging, the requirement for disclosure and the ongoing debate about appropriate limits highlight the tension between free speech and electoral integrity. As legislators, voters, and civic leaders grapple with these questions, the DoorDash case will likely serve as a touchstone for future reforms that aim to balance corporate influence with the principles of transparent, participatory democracy.
