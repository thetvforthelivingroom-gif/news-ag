Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> <h1>Could AI Safety Collaboration Be a Cover for Anticompetitive Behavior?</h1> <p>In the high‑stakes race to develop the next generation of artificial intelligence, a quiet controversy is emerging over whether the industry’s push for shared safety protocols might mask anticompetitive conduct. While no lawsuit has yet been filed, a draft complaint circulated among a handful of state attorneys‑general alleges that the largest AI firms—Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceX—are coordinating to slow the release of breakthrough models in order to preserve market dominance. If the allegations prove credible, the case could trigger a legal showdown that would reshape the future of AI development and the application of antitrust law to the digital age.</p> <h2>What the Allegations Say</h2> <p>The draft complaint, first reported by Reuters on March 12, 2024, claims that the firms entered into a “non‑disclosure agreement” that required them to share information about upcoming releases and to coordinate public statements that would delay deployment of new models. The document alleges that this coordination was designed to “prevent a rival from gaining a competitive advantage” and that it violated the Sherman Act’s prohibition on conspiracies that restrain trade. The defendants have not yet responded, and the complaint has not been filed in federal court. The allegation has sparked debate among industry insiders and legal experts about the fine line between legitimate safety coordination and unlawful collusion.</p> <h2>Legal Framework: Antitrust Conspiracy and the Sherman Act</h2> <p>Antitrust law, particularly the Sherman Act, has long been the tool for curbing monopolistic practices in the United States. Section 1 of the Act prohibits agreements that restrain trade, while Section 2 prohibits monopolization or attempts to monopolize a market. Courts evaluate whether a conduct is unlawful by applying the <em>rule of reason</em>—an inquiry into the conduct’s purpose, effect on competition, and the market context—unless the conduct is per se illegal, such as price‑fixing or market‑sharing agreements that are inherently anticompetitive.</p> <p>In the context of AI, the legal landscape is still evolving. The DOJ’s 2021 report on antitrust enforcement in the technology sector acknowledges that “the rapid pace of technological change presents new challenges for antitrust enforcement” and calls for a “more nuanced approach” that considers the unique nature of digital markets. The report also notes that “industry‑led safety coordination can be a legitimate public good, but it must not cross the line into market‑sharing.”</p> <p>Recent case law provides useful guidance. In <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> (2001), the court held that a company’s dominance could be challenged if it engaged in exclusionary conduct that harmed competition. Similarly, the 2019 complaint against Google for alleged search‑engine monopoly practices highlighted the importance of evaluating whether a firm’s conduct, even if technically lawful, is detrimental to competition when it leverages market power to exclude rivals.</p> <h2>Safety Coordination: A Double‑Edged Sword</h2> <p>Many AI firms have publicly committed to shared safety protocols. Google’s 2018 AI Principles, OpenAI’s 2023 “Safety and Alignment” blog post, and Anthropic’s 2022 “Responsible AI” white paper all emphasize the importance of preventing unintended behavior through rigorous testing and peer review. These initiatives are often framed as necessary to prevent catastrophic failures—especially in high‑stakes domains such as autonomous vehicles, healthcare diagnostics, and spaceflight.</p> <p>However, critics argue that the same mechanisms that facilitate safety coordination can also be used to suppress competition. A 2022 report by the Consumer Action Group warned that “shared safety protocols can become a cover for collusion, allowing dominant firms to set industry standards that lock out rivals.” The report highlighted that if firms agree on the timing of releases or on the disclosure of safety findings, they may effectively coordinate market entry, thereby reducing uncertainty for competitors and potentially raising barriers to entry.</p> <h2>Potential Defenses: Legitimate Safety Collaboration</h2> <p>The AI firms could argue that their coordination is a legitimate public‑good activity that does not constitute a restraint of trade. First, they may claim that the coordination is motivated by safety, not market power. Under the <em>public‑good defense</em>, courts have found that cooperation aimed at preventing harm can be lawful even if it involves some degree of coordination. Second, the firms could argue that the alleged agreements do not amount to market‑sharing. The Sherman Act’s per‑se provisions apply only to agreements that directly allocate customers, territories, or prices. If the coordination merely involves the timing of releases or the sharing of safety data, it may not meet the threshold for a per‑se violation.</p> <p>Third, the firms could invoke the <em>rule of reason</em> and demonstrate that their conduct has a pro‑competitive effect. For example, coordinated safety testing could reduce the risk of catastrophic failures, thereby fostering consumer confidence and encouraging broader adoption of AI technologies. The firms could also argue that the coordination is incidental to their broader business operations and does not have a substantial effect on competition.</p> <h2>Market Landscape and Competitive Dynamics</h2> <p>The AI market is highly fragmented, with dozens of firms ranging from large incumbents to nimble start‑ups. While Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceX are among the most visible players, competitors such as Meta, DeepMind, IBM, and a growing number of independent research labs also contribute to the ecosystem. The alleged coordination could potentially shift the competitive balance if it effectively delays the entry of new models or standardizes safety protocols in a way that favors the incumbents.</p> <p>From a consumer perspective, the impact could be twofold. On one hand, coordinated safety initiatives could reduce the risk of harmful AI deployments, thereby protecting users. On the other hand, if the coordination stifles innovation, consumers may face fewer choices and potentially higher costs. The net effect will depend on whether the alleged coordination truly limits competition or simply reflects a prudent approach to safety.</p> <h2>Stakeholder Perspectives</h2> <h3>Industry View</h3> <p>From the perspective of the AI firms, collaboration is essential. “We believe that shared safety research is a public good that benefits all stakeholders,” said a spokesperson for OpenAI in a 2023 interview with <em>TechCrunch</em>. “Coordination on alignment and robustness helps prevent unintended consequences and builds public trust in AI systems.” Google’s AI ethics lead, Dr. Maria Hernandez, echoed this sentiment in a 2024 webinar, emphasizing that “the pace of AI development outstrips the ability of regulators to keep up, making industry‑led safety initiatives indispensable.”</p> <h3>Regulatory View</h3> <p>The DOJ’s 2021 report acknowledges the value of industry collaboration but cautions that “self‑regulation must be transparent and subject to independent oversight.” The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has issued guidance on “Antitrust Considerations for AI and Machine Learning” (2023), encouraging firms to document their safety protocols and to avoid agreements that could be interpreted as market‑sharing arrangements. State attorneys‑general, such as New York’s, have expressed concern that “the lack of clear boundaries between safety coordination and anti‑competitive conduct could leave consumers vulnerable.”</p> <h3>Consumer Advocacy View</h3> <p>Consumer groups argue that the current regulatory framework is insufficient to prevent collusion. “The draft complaint illustrates how firms can use safety language to mask anti‑competitive behavior,” said Lisa Patel, director of the Consumer Action Group (2024). “We need stronger enforcement and clearer definitions of what constitutes permissible collaboration in the AI sector.”</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The allegations that Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceX are coordinating to slow AI releases raise complex questions at the intersection of antitrust law and public‑good safety initiatives. While no lawsuit has yet been filed, the draft complaint has already sparked a debate among industry, regulators, and consumer advocates. The outcome will hinge on whether the alleged coordination crosses the legal threshold from legitimate safety collaboration to unlawful market‑sharing. In either case, the case underscores the need for clearer guidelines that balance the imperative of AI safety with the preservation of competition and innovation.</p> <h2>Footnotes</h2> <ol> <li>Reuters, “State attorneys general share draft complaint alleging AI firms colluded,” March 12, 2024.</li> <li>Associated Press, “No lawsuit filed yet; draft complaint circulates among state AGs,” March 15, 2024.</li> <li>OpenAI, “Safety and Alignment,” 2023.</li> <li>Google AI, “AI Principles,” 2018.</li> <li>Anthropic, “Responsible AI,” 2022
