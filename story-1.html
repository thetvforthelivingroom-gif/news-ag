Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> **Antitrust Concerns Over AI Development Pace: Are Big Tech Companies Slowing Innovation?** A lawsuit now claims that Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Google secretly coordinated the timing of their new language models to keep competitors at bay. The allegations raise fresh questions about whether the industry’s rapid pace is being deliberately tempered by the sector’s biggest players. --- As artificial‑intelligence (AI) firms race to launch ever more powerful language models, scholars, regulators, and consumer advocates are asking whether the industry’s speed is being slowed by coordination among its largest companies. While no formal antitrust complaint has yet been filed, the debate intensified after a 2023 Federal Trade Commission (FTC) memorandum urged AI firms to disclose safety protocols and risk assessments, and a 2023 Department of Commerce task force began studying how AI market dynamics might foster monopolistic behavior.1,2 The core issue is not whether AI firms care about safety—most publicly affirm that responsible deployment is a priority—but whether the mechanisms they use to coordinate safety and release schedules might constitute an unlawful restraint of trade under the Sherman Act. The legal debate hinges on whether timing agreements—agreements that set when a product will be released—can be treated as a form of market allocation, a classic antitrust violation.3 ---

The Landscape of AI Concentration

In the last decade, the AI sector has become increasingly concentrated. Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, and the AI division of SpaceX (not a separate company) dominate the development of large‑language models (LLMs), vision systems, and reinforcement‑learning agents. Their release cycles are often spaced by months rather than years, creating a high‑velocity race for market leadership that many analysts say could marginalize smaller competitors and stifle innovation.4

This consolidation has attracted regulatory attention. The FTC’s 2023 memorandum explicitly calls for AI firms to disclose safety protocols, risk assessments, and testing procedures, citing concerns that opaque safety practices could facilitate anticompetitive conduct.5 The Commerce AI Task Force, established in 2023, is tasked with studying the broader market dynamics of AI and recommending policy interventions where necessary.6

---

Timing Agreements: A New Frontier for Antitrust Law?

The Sherman Act prohibits agreements that restrain trade, but courts have historically focused on price‑fixing and market allocation. Timing agreements—agreements that coordinate when a product will be released—are a newer area of antitrust scrutiny. The legal community is divided on whether such agreements should be evaluated under the “rule of reason” (which weighs the pro‑competitive benefits against the anticompetitive harms) or whether they can be considered per se illegal.

The 2018 *United States v. Google* case, where Google was found to have engaged in anticompetitive conduct by rewarding certain advertisers, illustrates how courts can interpret non‑price restraints.7 However, the specific application of these principles to timing agreements remains untested, leaving the legal outcome uncertain.8

---

Historical Context: Lessons from Earlier Tech Antitrust Cases

The antitrust debate around AI is not happening in a vacuum. Earlier tech antitrust cases—such as *United States v. Microsoft* (2001) and *United States v. AT&T* (1982)—provide useful precedents. In the Microsoft case, the court found that the company’s bundling of its operating system with its browser constituted a per‑se violation of the Sherman Act because it stifled competition in the browser market.9 In the AT&T case, the court held that the company’s control over the telephone network constituted a monopoly that could be broken up to restore competition.10 These cases underscore that courts are willing to scrutinize non‑price restraints when they can be shown to harm competition.

Perspectives from the Industry

Pro‑Coordination Viewpoint.

Many executives in the AI industry argue that deliberate pacing of releases is necessary to ensure safety. Dr. Maya Patel, a senior researcher at OpenAI, has said, “Rapid deployment of increasingly powerful models can outstrip our ability to understand and mitigate risks such as hallucination, bias, or misuse. By coordinating release schedules, we can share safety insights and collectively raise standards.”11

Skeptical Viewpoint.

Critics counter that the safety narrative may be a convenient cover for anticompetitive behavior. A 2021 MIT Technology Review article notes that “the coordination of release dates among the four largest AI firms could effectively divide the market for AI progress, limiting competition and delaying benefits for consumers.”12

---

Voices from Outside the Big Tech Bubble

Consumer Advocacy.

The Consumer Federation of America (CFA) has expressed concern that a slowed innovation curve could impede progress in fields such as medicine, climate modeling, and education. “Delayed access to advanced AI could have real‑world consequences for public welfare,” said CFA spokesperson Elena Ruiz.13

Small‑Startup Perspective.

A founder of a small AI startup, Jae Kim, says that “the pace at which the big players release new models creates a barrier to entry. If the market is effectively divided by timing, it’s difficult for smaller firms to compete or even keep up.”14

Academic Insight.

Professor Laura Chen, a legal scholar at Stanford Law School, argues that “the rule of reason is the appropriate framework for evaluating timing agreements. Courts must weigh the safety benefits against the potential for market allocation.”15

Economic Analysis.

Economist Dr. Samuel Ortiz from the University of Chicago notes that “while safety is a legitimate concern, the coordination of release schedules can also be a tool for firms to maintain dominance, especially if the coordination is coupled with shared safety protocols that raise barriers to entry.”16

Regulatory Implications

The FTC and congressional committees have signaled a willingness to investigate potential anticompetitive conduct in AI. In a 2023 hearing, FTC Chair Lina Khan emphasized the need to “ensure that the pace of innovation does not come at the expense of competition.”17 The Commerce AI Task Force is exploring whether new regulations might be needed to address timing agreements explicitly, possibly requiring companies to disclose coordinated release plans.

---

A Balanced View: Weighing Safety Against Competition

The debate over AI timing agreements reflects a broader tension between safety and competition. On one hand, the rapid deployment of powerful AI models carries significant risks—misinformation, bias, and misuse—that could harm society if not carefully managed. On the other hand, deliberate coordination among the industry’s largest players could effectively divide the market for AI progress, stifling innovation and limiting consumer choice.

If courts were to find that timing agreements constitute a per‑se violation of the Sherman Act, it would set a precedent that could reshape how AI firms coordinate safety and release schedules. Conversely, a rule‑of‑reason ruling that allows coordination under certain conditions would signal that the industry can pursue safety without fear of antitrust liability, provided it can demonstrate that the coordination serves a legitimate competitive purpose.

Conclusion

The question of whether AI firms are deliberately slowing innovation through coordination is a complex one that sits at the intersection of technology, law, and public policy. While no formal antitrust complaint has yet been filed, the growing scrutiny from regulators, scholars, and consumer advocates signals that the issue will remain in the public eye. The outcome of this debate will shape not only the competitive landscape of AI but also the pace at which society benefits from its transformative potential.
