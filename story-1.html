Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Rumored Antitrust Lawsuit Accuses AI Giants of “Slow‑Down” Conspiracy – No Evidence Yet</h1> <p>In a climate of heightened scrutiny over the pace of artificial‑intelligence (AI) development, a rumor has spread that a federal antitrust complaint has been filed against Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and a purported “SpaceXAI.” The allegation is that the companies conspired to deliberately slow the release of new AI models, thereby stifling competition and harming consumers. Yet, a thorough search of the federal docket system, corporate statements, and industry press releases shows no evidence that such a lawsuit exists. The claims rest on anonymous sources and unverified documents, raising serious questions about their veracity and potential impact on the industry.</p> <h2>What the Allegations Say</h2> <p>The rumored complaint, supposedly filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, accuses the defendants of entering into a covert agreement to reduce the speed of model training, limit release frequency, and share proprietary safety protocols. The plaintiffs are described as a coalition of AI safety researchers and advocacy groups, and the complaint would seek a preliminary injunction to halt any ongoing coordination and compel disclosure of internal communications. However, no docket number, filing date, or list of plaintiffs appears in PACER or any other public court database. The rumor also names “SpaceXAI” as a defendant, a name that does not correspond to any known AI subsidiary of SpaceX; the company has publicly stated that it has no AI arm under that name.</p> <h2>Legal Foundations of the Alleged Claim</h2> <p>Under U.S. antitrust law, the Sherman Act (1890) prohibits conspiracies that restrain trade, while the Clayton Act (1914) bars agreements that significantly reduce competition or create monopolistic conditions. In the context of AI, a “slow‑down” conspiracy would involve coordinated actions that delay the development or release of new models, thereby granting the conspirators a competitive advantage at the expense of rivals and consumers. Historically, antitrust enforcement has targeted tech firms for a range of conduct—from data‑sharing agreements to exclusive licensing deals. The 1998 Microsoft case, for instance, found that tying Windows to Microsoft Office constituted an illegal restraint of trade. More recently, the 2018 U.S. Department of Justice investigation into Google’s dominance in search and advertising markets highlighted the challenges of applying antitrust principles to rapidly evolving digital platforms.</p> <h2>No Public Evidence of Collusion</h2> <p>Despite the dramatic narrative, no credible evidence has emerged. A search of PACER (Public Access to Court Electronic Records) and the federal court docket system yields no filings under the alleged parties. Likewise, no internal communications—such as meeting minutes, emails, or memos—have been made available to the public or to journalists. The rumor’s source claims to have accessed “confidential documents,” but without verifiable provenance, the documents remain unsubstantiated. In a statement to <em>Reuters</em>, a spokesperson for the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) said, “We are aware of circulating claims that AI firms are colluding to slow development, but we have no evidence to confirm these allegations.”^1 The ACLU emphasized the importance of transparency in AI development but cautioned against drawing conclusions without concrete evidence.</p> <p>Similarly, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) issued a brief note on its website stating, “The FTC monitors AI firms for anticompetitive behavior, but no formal complaint has been filed in this regard.”^2 The FTC has, however, published a blog post outlining its approach to AI and antitrust enforcement, underscoring that it requires clear evidence of coordinated conduct before pursuing action.^3</p> <h2>Industry Reactions</h2> <p>None of the alleged defendants has issued a public statement regarding the rumored lawsuit. OpenAI’s spokesperson, who declined to comment on the rumor, reiterated the company’s commitment to responsible AI development. Anthropic’s CEO, Dario Amodei, in a recent interview with <em>Bloomberg</em>, emphasized that the firm’s safety protocols are designed to mitigate risks, not to impede competitors. Google’s legal team has not responded to inquiries, and SpaceX has issued a brief statement confirming that it has no AI subsidiary named “SpaceXAI.”^4</p> <p>In the absence of any official response, the industry’s focus remains on the broader regulatory environment. The European Union’s Digital Services Act (DSA) and Digital Markets Act (DMA) are expected to shape AI governance in the coming years, with the European Commission’s AI strategy calling for robust safety standards and transparency. The European Commission has highlighted the need to scrutinize “coordination on safety standards” to ensure it does not mask anticompetitive conduct.^5</p> <h2>Expert Commentary</h2> <p>Professor Michael Heller, a leading antitrust scholar at Harvard Law School, cautions that “claims of collusion in the AI sector must be grounded in tangible evidence.” He notes that the legal standard for proving a conspiracy requires proof of an agreement and an intent to restrain trade or harm competition. “Without a documented agreement or clear evidence of intent, the allegations remain speculative,” Heller said. He added that regulators typically need a pattern of conduct that demonstrates a coordinated effort, not isolated statements or shared best‑practice guidelines.</p> <p>Other scholars echo Heller’s view. Dr. Lila Patel of Stanford University’s Center for Internet and Society points out that many AI firms share safety protocols voluntarily to protect the public and to avoid regulatory scrutiny. “Such collaboration is often framed as a collective effort to raise industry standards, not a means to suppress competition,” Patel said. She warned that conflating voluntary cooperation with illegal collusion can undermine legitimate innovation.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Until a formal filing appears in a public docket, the alleged antitrust complaint remains a rumor. The absence of evidence, coupled with the lack of responses from the implicated companies, suggests that the claim is unfounded. Nevertheless, the discussion highlights the growing tension between rapid AI development and the need for regulatory oversight. As governments worldwide refine their approaches to AI governance, clear evidence will be essential before any antitrust action can be pursued. For now, the industry continues to navigate a complex landscape where collaboration and competition coexist, and where the line between responsible partnership and unlawful restraint remains a subject of careful scrutiny.</p>
