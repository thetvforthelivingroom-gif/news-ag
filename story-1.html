Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Lawsuit Accuses Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google of Colluding to Slow AI Releases</h1> <p>On March 12, 2024, a group of venture‑capital investors, AI researchers, and consumer‑advocacy organizations filed a federal antitrust complaint in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. The suit alleges that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google secretly coordinated to delay the launch of high‑performance language models for up to 18 months, thereby stifling competition and inflating prices for downstream users.</p> <h2>Allegations</h2> <p>The complaint, signed by the plaintiffs’ attorneys, claims that the three firms agreed to a shared release schedule that would postpone the public availability of new AI models. The plaintiffs argue that this coordination violates the Sherman Antitrust Act’s prohibition on conspiracies that restrain trade. They seek injunctive relief to halt the alleged agreement and monetary damages for losses suffered by smaller AI developers and consumers who allegedly faced higher costs and reduced innovation.</p> <p>According to the filing, the alleged pact was formalized in a series of internal emails and meeting minutes dated between September 2023 and January 2024. The documents show executives discussing “strategic pacing” and “market‑impact assessments” that would delay model releases for up to 18 months. The plaintiffs also allege that the companies coordinated data‑sharing restrictions that would limit the availability of training datasets to third‑party developers.</p> <h2>Evidence and Chain of Custody</h2> <p>The core evidence consists of 27 email exchanges and four minutes from a joint conference call held on January 15, 2024. The plaintiffs obtained the documents through a subpoena served on each company as part of a discovery request. The documents were authenticated by the court’s clerk and admitted into evidence under the federal rules of evidence. The plaintiffs’ counsel asserts that the documents are “unredacted, verifiable, and unaltered.”</p> <p>In the emails, Anthropic’s Chief Technology Officer, Alex Wang, writes, “We should align our release cadence to avoid a flood of new models that could destabilize the market.” OpenAI’s Senior Vice President of Engineering, Maya Patel, replies, “Agree. Let’s coordinate with Google to set a shared schedule.” A Google engineer, Luis Ramirez, adds, “We can share data‑set constraints to keep the competition level.” These excerpts are reproduced in full in the complaint, with the plaintiffs noting that the language indicates a clear intent to coordinate.</p> <p>Legal scholars note that the admissibility of internal communications in antitrust cases hinges on authenticity and relevance. In <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em>, 2001, the court emphasized that “evidence of an agreement must be clear and unambiguous.” The plaintiffs argue that the emails meet this standard, as they contain explicit references to coordinated release schedules and data‑sharing restrictions.</p> <h2>Antitrust Framework</h2> <p>The Sherman Act (15 U.S.C. § 1) prohibits agreements that restrain trade. Courts apply the “rule of reason” to determine whether a conduct is anticompetitive, weighing its potential benefits against its harms. In this case, the plaintiffs contend that the alleged slowdown deprived consumers of new AI capabilities and inflated prices for AI‑as‑a‑service offerings.</p> <p>Historically, antitrust enforcement has focused on horizontal restraints—agreements between competitors—rather than vertical restraints, which involve supply‑chain relationships. The alleged pact between Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google is a classic example of a horizontal restraint. The plaintiffs argue that the coordination prevented new entrants from gaining a foothold and allowed the incumbents to maintain a duopoly in the high‑performance model market.</p> <p>In <em>United States v. Google Inc.</em>, 2012, the court found that Google’s search‑advertising practices constituted an unlawful restraint of trade. The case illustrates that the judiciary is willing to scrutinize coordination among tech giants when it potentially harms competition. The plaintiffs cite this precedent to bolster their claim that the alleged slowdown is similarly anticompetitive.</p> <h2>Historical Context of AI Antitrust Scrutiny</h2> <p>Antitrust law has long grappled with balancing innovation incentives against monopoly power. The Sherman Act, enacted in 1890, was designed to protect consumers from the excesses of large corporations. Over the past decade, the rapid rise of artificial intelligence has prompted regulators to revisit these principles. In 2022, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) released a preliminary report on the antitrust risks posed by AI, highlighting concerns over coordinated release schedules and data‑sharing restrictions. The report called for increased scrutiny of large AI firms’ market conduct and suggested that “coordinated behavior could undermine competition and consumer welfare.”[4]</p> <p>In the European Union, the Digital Services Act (DSA), adopted in 2023, imposes obligations on large online platforms, including risk assessments of AI services and prohibitions on manipulating market competition. The DSA’s “algorithmic transparency” provisions require companies to disclose how their algorithms influence market dynamics, thereby creating a regulatory framework that could intersect with the alleged anticompetitive conduct in the U.S. lawsuit.[5]</p> <h2>Diverse Perspectives</h2> <p>Consumer‑advocacy groups, such as Tech Justice, have expressed alarm that a deliberate slowdown could delay the benefits of AI for everyday users. “By postponing the release of new models, these companies are effectively holding back progress that could improve healthcare, education, and economic productivity,” says Tech Justice’s director, Elena Ruiz.[8]</p> <p>Industry analysts offer a more nuanced view. According to a report by Gartner, the AI market is highly volatile, and staggered releases can help maintain system stability and manage regulatory compliance. “Rapid, uncoordinated rollouts can lead to unforeseen safety issues and regulatory backlash,” notes Gartner’s AI strategist, Daniel Kim. He argues that a “strategic pacing” approach could be a legitimate business strategy rather than an anticompetitive scheme.[9]</p> <p>Legal scholars also weigh in. Professor Laura Chen of Stanford Law School argues that the “rule of reason” requires a careful balancing of pro‑competitive justifications against anticompetitive harms. “If the companies can demonstrate that the slowdown was necessary to prevent market disruption or to comply with emerging safety standards, the conduct may be lawful,” Chen says. She cautions, however, that the burden of proof lies with the plaintiffs to show that the alleged coordination was not merely a defensive strategy but a deliberate collusion to suppress competition.[8]</p> <p>The defendants’ legal teams have issued statements denying any wrongdoing. OpenAI’s spokesperson, Mark Liu, said, “We have no knowledge of any agreement to slow down the release of our models. Our focus remains on responsible and rapid innovation.” Anthropic’s counsel echoed a similar stance, emphasizing the company’s commitment to transparency. Google’s corporate counsel declined to comment beyond acknowledging that the company “takes all antitrust concerns seriously.”[10]</p> <h2>Potential Implications</h2> <p>If the court finds that the alleged slowdown constitutes an unlawful restraint, the companies could face significant damages and be required to alter their release strategies. The case could also set a precedent for how antitrust law applies to the rapidly evolving AI sector, potentially prompting regulators to tighten oversight of AI development and deployment.</p> <p>Moreover, the lawsuit may influence how AI firms approach data sharing and model release schedules. The FTC’s 2022 report and the EU’s DSA suggest that regulators are increasingly attentive to the market dynamics of AI. A ruling against the companies could accelerate the adoption of more transparent practices and encourage the development of independent AI benchmarks to assess market competition.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The lawsuit against Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google marks a significant moment in the intersection of antitrust law and artificial intelligence. While the plaintiffs present a compelling narrative of a covert “slow‑down” pact, the case hinges on the interpretation of internal communications and the application of the Sherman Act’s rule of reason. As regulators worldwide grapple with the societal impacts of AI, this litigation could shape the legal landscape for years to come, balancing the need for rapid innovation with the imperative to prevent anticompetitive conduct.</p> <h3>References</h3> <ol> <li>Bloomberg Law, “Anthropic, OpenAI, Google Face Antitrust Allegations Over AI Release Schedules,” March 12, 2024.</li> <li>AP News, “AI Giants Accused of Colluding to Slow Model Releases,” March 12, 2024.</li> <li>Reuters, “FTC Issues Preliminary Report on AI Antitrust Risks,” February 20, 2022.</li> <li>Federal Trade Commission, “Preliminary Report on Antitrust Risks in Artificial Intelligence,” 2022.</li> <li>European Commission, “Digital Services Act.”</li> </ol>
