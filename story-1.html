Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> A federal lawsuit filed on May 15, 2024 alleges that four of the world’s leading artificial‑intelligence (AI) companies—Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Google—conspired to slow the release of new AI models and coordinate data‑sharing in a way that would give them a competitive edge while stifling innovation from smaller players. The suit, brought by a coalition of consumer‑advocacy groups and small‑business plaintiffs, could reshape how antitrust law is applied to the rapidly evolving AI sector.

What the complaint says

The complaint, lodged in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, claims that the defendants exchanged internal memos and emails that outlined a “slow‑down” timetable. According to the plaintiffs, the documents detail specific milestones for model training, testing, and public deployment, and they also describe an agreement to share proprietary datasets under conditions that would disadvantage third‑party developers. The suit argues that these actions constitute an illegal agreement to restrict competition, violating the Sherman Act, a federal statute that prohibits contracts, combinations, or conspiracies that unreasonably restrain trade [T2].

The defendants have denied the allegations. They contend that any coordination was limited to standard industry practices, such as safety‑testing protocols, and that the documents cited by the plaintiffs were taken out of context. The companies argue that the lawsuit mischaracterizes routine collaboration aimed at mitigating existential risks associated with advanced AI systems [T2]. The case has attracted attention from regulators in Washington, Brussels, and Beijing, all of whom are grappling with how to balance rapid technological progress with robust oversight.

How antitrust law has been applied to tech giants

Antitrust law in the United States has long been used to scrutinize the conduct of large technology firms. The 2018–2020 investigations into Google’s dominance in search and its alleged collusion with advertisers established that agreements that restrict competition—even if framed as “collaborative”—can violate the Sherman Act if they substantially lessen competition or create a monopoly. The current lawsuit extends that framework to the nascent field of AI, a domain where the pace of development and the stakes for public safety are unprecedented.

Globally, governments are tightening AI governance. The European Union’s AI Act, adopted in 2023, imposes strict requirements on high‑risk AI systems, including transparency, human oversight, and rigorous testing [T3]. In the United States, lawmakers have proposed the AI Bill of Rights, which would codify safeguards for privacy, bias, and accountability. China’s AI governance framework emphasizes state oversight and the alignment of AI development with national interests. These regulatory trends underscore the urgency of determining whether coordinated actions among leading AI firms constitute anti‑competitive conduct or a necessary safety measure.

The alleged agreement in context

The alleged partnership between Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI, and Google is unprecedented in its scope. While the firms have historically collaborated on open‑source projects and shared research findings, the lawsuit contends that this partnership extends to deliberate pacing of releases and controlled data sharing—practices that could delay the availability of powerful models to the broader market. The plaintiffs argue that such a slowdown would benefit the four companies by reducing competition from emerging startups and smaller research labs, thereby consolidating market power.

Diverse perspectives on the lawsuit

Regulators have expressed concern that the lawsuit could have far‑reaching implications for AI innovation. The U.S. Department of Justice’s Office of the Attorney General released a statement saying that the agency is “examining the allegations” and that it “will consider whether the conduct alleged in the complaint violates federal antitrust laws” [T2]. The European Commission, meanwhile, has indicated that it will monitor the case as part of its broader AI oversight strategy, noting that “any coordination that restricts competition must be scrutinized under EU competition rules” [T3].

Consumer‑advocacy groups view the lawsuit as a necessary check on the growing concentration of power in the AI sector. They argue that the alleged slowdown would delay access to potentially life‑saving technologies, such as AI‑driven medical diagnostics and climate‑modeling tools. “When the very companies that are shaping the future of technology collude to delay progress, it is the public who bears the cost,” said a spokesperson for the coalition of plaintiffs [T2].

Investors, however, warn that the lawsuit could increase compliance costs and create uncertainty for the industry. Several venture‑capital firms that have backed AI startups have issued statements cautioning that “antitrust litigation at this scale could deter future investment” and might lead to a “re‑allocation of capital away from high‑risk, high‑reward AI ventures” [T3].

Academic researchers are divided. Some scholars argue that coordinated safety testing is essential to prevent catastrophic outcomes, citing the 2023 incident where an advanced language model produced biased outputs that caused reputational damage for its developer. Others caution that “co‑ordination among dominant firms can create a barrier to entry for smaller innovators, thereby stifling the very diversity that fuels breakthroughs” [T2].

Quantitatively, the four firms together command roughly 70 % of the global AI model‑training market, according to a recent industry analysis. Their combined annual investment in AI research and development exceeds $15 billion, dwarfing the $1.5 billion invested by the next largest cluster of firms [T3].
