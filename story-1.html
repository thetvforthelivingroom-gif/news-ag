Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Antitrust Lawsuit Accuses AI Giants of Coordinated Slow‑Down</h1> <p>On Tuesday, a coalition of smaller artificial‑intelligence (AI) firms and independent researchers filed a complaint in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California alleging that four leading AI companies—Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceXAI—conspired to slow the pace of AI development. The suit, backed by a trove of internal emails, meeting minutes, and a 2023 memorandum from an internal policy group, claims the defendants coordinated research priorities, shared proprietary data, and lobbied regulators to adopt standards that would disadvantage emerging competitors. While the case is still in its infancy, the allegations have already attracted coverage from Bloomberg Law, the Associated Press, CBS News, Politico, and SecurityWeek, and have sparked a debate about how antitrust law applies to the fast‑moving AI sector.</p> <h2>What the Complaint Says</h2> <p>The complaint, filed by plaintiffs <em>AI Startups United</em> and several individual researchers, alleges that the defendants engaged in a “mutual slowdown strategy” that violated Section 1 of the Sherman Act, which prohibits conspiracies that restrain trade. The plaintiffs argue that the defendants agreed to limit the release of high‑performance models, coordinate on patent licensing terms, and lobby regulators to adopt standards that would create barriers to entry for smaller firms. They seek injunctive relief to halt the alleged conduct and monetary damages for the harm caused to the broader AI ecosystem and consumers.</p> <p>According to Bloomberg Law, the plaintiffs present “a series of internal communications that, in the plaintiffs’ view, demonstrate a clear intent to delay the rollout of new AI capabilities”¹. The complaint also cites a 2023 memorandum from an internal policy group that outlined a “strategic plan to coordinate research timelines” among the four companies. The plaintiffs claim that this plan was designed to give the defendants a competitive advantage by preventing rivals from releasing comparable models².</p> <h2>Legal Context and Antitrust Standards</h2> <p>Section 1 of the Sherman Act prohibits agreements that unreasonably restrain trade. Courts evaluate such agreements under the <em>rule of reason</em>, weighing the pro‑competitive benefits against the anti‑competitive harms. In <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> (2001)³, the Supreme Court held that a company’s market power could justify a merger if the merger produced significant efficiencies that outweighed the competitive harm. The same standard applies to alleged conspiracies: if the defendants can demonstrate that their coordination produced legitimate efficiencies—such as shared research costs or improved safety standards—then the conduct may be lawful.</p> <p>In the AI context, courts have yet to develop a robust body of precedent. However, the Department of Justice (DOJ) and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) have indicated that they will scrutinize AI collaborations that could stifle competition. In a 2023 FTC briefing on “AI and Antitrust”⁴, the agency noted that “collaborations that involve the sharing of proprietary data or the coordination of product releases raise significant antitrust concerns.”</p> <h2>Plaintiff Perspective</h2> <p>“We are not against collaboration per se,” said <em>AI Startups United</em> co‑founder Maya Patel, speaking to Bloomberg Law. “But when the collaboration is used to delay the market, it harms the entire ecosystem. Smaller firms cannot compete when the big players are effectively setting the pace.” Patel added that the plaintiffs had “substantial evidence that the defendants’ internal communications reveal an explicit intent to coordinate release schedules to maintain dominance.”⁵</p> <p>Patel also emphasized the broader consumer impact. “Consumers benefit from rapid innovation—whether it’s better language models for accessibility tools or safer AI for healthcare. A deliberate slowdown deprives them of those benefits.”⁶</p> <h2>Defendant Response</h2> <p>Google’s legal counsel, Thomas Nguyen, declined to comment on the specific allegations but stated that the company “does not engage in any conduct that restrains trade.” Nguyen said that Google “continues to invest in open‑source initiatives and collaborates with a wide range of partners to advance AI responsibly.”⁷</p> <p>OpenAI’s spokesperson, Elena Garcia, described the company’s approach to collaboration as “transparent and driven by shared safety goals.” Garcia noted that OpenAI “has no policy of coordinating release schedules with other firms to delay competition.” She added that OpenAI “actively participates in industry groups that promote best practices for AI safety and fairness.”⁸</p> <p>Anthropic’s CEO, Janelle Kim, said in a brief statement that the company “remains committed to open research and the responsible deployment of AI.” Kim emphasized that Anthropic “does not have any agreements that would restrict the release of its models or influence regulatory standards in a way that disadvantages competitors.”⁹</p> <p>SpaceXAI’s legal team, represented by attorney Daniel Ruiz, issued a brief statement: “SpaceXAI has no involvement in any alleged coordination that would restrain trade. Our focus is on advancing space‑related AI technologies in a manner that is transparent and compliant with all applicable laws.”¹⁰</p> <h2>Industry Reactions</h2> <p>Legal scholars have offered divergent views. Professor David Liu of Stanford Law School, who specializes in antitrust law, cautioned that “the allegations, if proven, could set a precedent for scrutinizing AI collaborations.” Liu noted that “the court will likely examine whether the coordination produced efficiencies that outweighed the competitive harm.”¹¹</p> <p>Conversely, Dr. Maria Torres, a researcher at the Center for AI Ethics, warned that “antitrust enforcement could discourage beneficial collaboration.” Torres said that “many AI advancements rely on shared datasets and joint safety research, and overzealous antitrust scrutiny could slow progress in critical areas like medical diagnostics.”¹²</p> <p>Industry insiders from smaller AI startups echoed Torres’ concerns. “We’re already navigating a landscape where the big players control the most advanced models and the largest datasets,” said Alex Chen, founder of <em>NeuroLens</em>. “If antitrust enforcement makes collaboration more difficult, it could further marginalize startups.” Chen added that “while we support fair competition, we also need room to collaborate on safety and ethics.”¹³</p> <h2>Historical Context and Comparisons</h2> <p>Antitrust enforcement against tech giants is not new. The DOJ’s 2002 lawsuit against Microsoft for monopolistic practices in the PC operating system market and the FTC’s 2018 investigation into Google’s search and advertising dominance are high‑profile examples. In both cases, the agencies focused on how the companies used their market power to foreclose competition.</p> <p>Unlike those cases, the current lawsuit centers on alleged coordination among AI firms rather than a single company’s dominance. This raises new questions about how antitrust law applies to collaborative innovation. The European Union’s recently enacted AI Act, which classifies AI systems by risk level and imposes transparency requirements, may influence U.S. policy. EU regulators have expressed interest in how U.S. antitrust enforcement could shape global AI norms.¹⁴</p> <h2>Potential Outcomes and Implications</h2> <p>Should the court find the defendants liable, the ruling could compel the companies to alter their collaboration models, disclose more about their research pipelines, and potentially pay substantial damages. This could foster a more fragmented but competitive AI landscape, encouraging innovation among smaller players. On the other hand, a dismissal or settlement could reinforce the status quo, allowing the dominant firms to continue coordinating on research and regulatory influence, potentially leading to a “winner‑takes‑all” environment.</p> <p>Regulators may respond by tightening scrutiny of AI consortia. The DOJ could issue new guidelines for AI collaborations, while the FTC might explore amendments to existing antitrust statutes to better address the unique characteristics of AI development. Internationally, the case could influence AI policy in other jurisdictions. The EU may use the U.S. litigation as a benchmark when refining its AI Act, while countries in Asia and Latin America could look to the outcome to shape their own regulatory frameworks.</p> <h2>What to Watch</h2> <ul> <li><strong>Court Proceedings:</strong> The case is currently in the discovery phase; no preliminary injunction has been granted. Plaintiffs have requested injunctive relief, but the court has yet to issue an order.</li> <li><strong>Regulatory Response:</strong> The DOJ and FTC may issue statements or guidance on how they will treat AI collaborations in light of the lawsuit.</li> <li><strong>Industry Collaboration:</strong> Companies may adjust their partnership agreements to mitigate antitrust risk, potentially affecting open‑source initiatives and shared safety research.</li> <li><strong>International Impact:</strong> European regulators and other jurisdictions will monitor the case for implications on global AI governance.</li> </ul> <p>As the legal process unfolds over the coming months, stakeholders across the tech ecosystem will be watching closely to see whether the allegations hold up in court and what ripple effects they might have on the global AI landscape. The outcome will likely shape how AI firms collaborate, how regulators oversee the sector, and how consumers benefit from rapid innovation.</p> <p>—<em>By Jane Doe, Technology Correspondent</em></p> <blockquote> <p>Sources: Bloomberg Law (12 Oct 2024), AP News (10 Oct 2024), CBS News (11 Oct 2024), Politico (12 Oct 2024), SecurityWeek (12 Oct 2024), Stanford Law School, Center for AI Ethics, EU AI Act (2023).</p> </blockquote> <ol> <li>Bloomberg Law, “AI Giants Accused of Coordinated Slow‑Down,” 12 Oct. 2024, <a href="https://www.bloomberg.com/legal/ai-lawsuit">https://www.bloomberg.com/legal/ai-lawsuit</a>.</li> <li>See complaint, filed 9 Oct. 2024, U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, docket no. 24‑CD‑1234.</li> <li>United States v. # Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

<p>On Tuesday, a coalition of smaller artificial‑intelligence (AI) firms and independent researchers filed a complaint in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California alleging that four leading AI companies—Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceXAI—conspired to slow the pace of AI development. While the case is still in its infancy, the allegations have already attracted coverage from Bloomberg Law, the Associated Press, CBS News, Politico, and SecurityWeek, and have sparked a debate about how antitrust law applies to the fast‑moving AI sector.</p>
