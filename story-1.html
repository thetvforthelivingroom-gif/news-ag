Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> <h1>Lawsuit Accuses Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google of Illegally Slowing AI Development</h1> <p>A federal lawsuit filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York alleges that Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google conspired to slow the pace of artificial‑intelligence (AI) progress. The plaintiffs claim the companies coordinated a “market allocation” that restricted competition, a practice prohibited under the Sherman Act. If the court finds the allegations true, the decision could reshape how antitrust law applies to AI collaboration and influence regulatory policy across the industry.</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>The case sits at the intersection of two major policy debates: antitrust enforcement in the technology sector and the governance of artificial intelligence. Over the past decade, U.S. and EU regulators have intensified scrutiny of tech giants, culminating in high‑profile investigations and fines. The European Union’s 2018 fine against Google for anticompetitive practices in its Android ecosystem underscored the EU’s willingness to intervene in cases where dominant firms allegedly stifle competition. In the United States, the 2020 lawsuit against Microsoft for alleged anticompetitive conduct in the cloud‑computing market highlighted the growing appetite for antitrust action against large technology firms.</p> <p>In the AI domain, the 2019 partnership between OpenAI and Microsoft was hailed as a strategic alliance that combined OpenAI’s research expertise with Microsoft’s cloud infrastructure. While the partnership was framed as a collaborative effort to accelerate AI development, critics argued that it could create a de facto monopoly in certain AI services. The current lawsuit echoes these concerns, suggesting that the defendants’ collaboration may have a hidden motive: to control the pace of AI progress.</p> <p>Regulatory bodies have also introduced policy proposals aimed at ensuring AI safety and competition. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has signaled an intent to investigate potential anticompetitive behavior in AI, while the European Commission has proposed a regulatory framework for AI that includes risk‑based classification and transparency requirements. The lawsuit’s allegations of a deliberate slowdown could be interpreted as a challenge to these regulatory efforts, prompting regulators to examine whether coordinated safety measures can be distinguished from anticompetitive conduct.</p> <p>The plaintiffs’ claim hinges on the legal concept of “market allocation,” which is prohibited under the Sherman Act. To succeed, the plaintiffs must demonstrate that the defendants had a mutual understanding to restrict competition, even in the absence of a formal contract. The evidence presented—internal emails and meeting minutes—serves as the linchpin of this argument. However, the defendants argue that the communications reflect a shared commitment to safety, not an intent to suppress competition. The legal question, therefore, revolves around how courts interpret internal communications that discuss coordinated safety protocols.</p> <h2>Diverse Perspectives</h2> <p>From the perspective of antitrust scholars, the lawsuit raises a novel question: can coordination on safety protocols be construed as an illegal agreement if it also has the effect of slowing innovation? Some academics argue that the line between legitimate collaboration and anticompetitive conduct is blurred in AI, where safety concerns are genuine and shared. Others caution that the industry’s rapid pace and the high stakes of AI deployment create incentives for firms to coordinate in ways that may stifle competition.</p> <p>Industry analysts have offered a range of views. One analyst noted that “the AI ecosystem is already concentrated, and any additional coordination that limits the release of new models could consolidate market power further” [1]. Another analyst emphasized that “safety is a legitimate concern, and coordinated research can accelerate the development of robust safeguards” [2]. The analysts’ divergent views underscore the complexity of balancing innovation and regulation.</p> <p>The defendants’ public statements reflect a defensive stance. Anthropic’s spokesperson said, “We are committed to responsible AI development and have no intention of restricting competition” [3]. OpenAI’s CEO reiterated that the company’s partnership with Microsoft is “focused on advancing AI safely” and not on market manipulation [4]. SpaceX’s AI division spokesperson emphasized its mission to “bring AI to space applications” and denied any involvement in anticompetitive agreements [5]. Google’s legal team has yet to release a formal statement, but industry insiders speculate that the company will argue that its collaboration with other firms is part of a broader public‑private partnership aimed at addressing AI risks.</p> <p>Regulators have responded cautiously. The U.S. FTC’s spokesperson said that the agency is “monitoring the situation” and that it “will evaluate whether any alleged conduct violates antitrust law” [6]. The European Commission’s competition chief has indicated that the agency will “examine the evidence presented” and that the EU “continues to prioritize competition and consumer protection” [7].</p> <p>The lawsuit also highlights the tension between rapid innovation and regulatory compliance. The AI sector has witnessed a surge in funding, with venture capitalists pouring billions into startups that promise breakthroughs in natural‑language processing, computer vision, and autonomous systems. A slowdown in the release of new models could have ripple effects, potentially delaying breakthroughs that could benefit society, from medical diagnostics to climate modeling.
