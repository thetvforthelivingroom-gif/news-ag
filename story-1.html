Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
On March 12, 2024, a federal complaint was filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California by a coalition of AI researchers, venture capitalists, and former employees of several leading AI firms. The suit alleges that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google conspired to delay the release of new large‑language‑model (LLM) systems in order to preserve market dominance and avoid regulatory scrutiny. The plaintiffs claim that the alleged coordination violated the Sherman Act and the Clayton Act, and they seek a preliminary injunction to halt any continued delay of model releases. The defendants have denied any wrongdoing, arguing that their public commitments to open‑source research and rigorous safety testing explain any perceived slowdown. The case has drawn attention from regulators, investors, and the broader AI community, who are watching to see whether the lawsuit will set a new precedent for how the rapidly evolving field is governed.¹

The allegations in detail

The complaint, filed by a group that includes former Anthropic engineer Maya Patel, OpenAI policy analyst Daniel Kim, and venture capitalist Sarah Liu, alleges that the three companies engaged in a series of meetings and internal communications that demonstrate an intent to coordinate release schedules. The plaintiffs point to a series of email threads from late 2023 in which representatives from each company discuss “strategic pacing” of model launches, citing specific dates for the next generation of models. In one exchange, an Anthropic senior engineer writes, “We should hold back the 2024 release until after the Google‑OpenAI joint safety review is complete.”¹ The complaint also cites a confidential memorandum from a joint task force convened by the companies in January 2024, which the plaintiffs argue shows a shared strategy to delay releases until after the European Union’s AI Act takes effect.²

The plaintiffs argue that this coordination constitutes a “price‑setting” agreement that reduces competition by limiting the frequency of new model releases, thereby extending the market share of the three firms. They contend that the alleged coordination is a violation of § 2 of the Sherman Act, which prohibits conspiracies that restrain trade, and § 1 of the Clayton Act, which prohibits agreements that unreasonably restrain competition.³

Defendants’ response

OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google have all issued statements denying any collusion. In a joint press release, the companies said that “each firm independently determines its own product roadmap based on internal research priorities, safety considerations, and regulatory requirements.”¹ They also emphasized their public commitments to open‑source research. OpenAI’s policy director, Maria Sanchez, said in a statement, “We have released the code for GPT‑4 and are actively collaborating with the research community to advance safety standards.”¹ Anthropic’s CEO, Dario Amodei, added, “Our approach to model development is guided by rigorous testing and a commitment to transparency.”¹ Google’s AI spokesperson, Priya Patel, noted that the company has “a long history of open‑source contributions, including the TensorFlow framework.”¹

The defendants argue that the alleged communications were taken out of context and that each company was pursuing its own research agenda. They also point to the fact that OpenAI is a capped‑profit company, not a purely for‑profit entity, and that Anthropic is a for‑profit venture backed by investors.¹

Legal framework and precedent

The lawsuit raises questions about how antitrust law applies to the AI industry. The Sherman Act’s § 2 prohibits conspiracies that restrain trade, while § 1 prohibits agreements that unreasonably restrain competition. The Clayton Act’s § 1 similarly addresses anticompetitive agreements. In the 1998 Microsoft case, the Department of Justice (DOJ) found that Microsoft had engaged in anticompetitive practices by tying its Windows operating system to its Internet Explorer browser, thereby stifling competition in web browsers.¹⁴ In the 2018 Google case, the DOJ alleged that Google had engaged in anticompetitive conduct by using its search engine dominance to promote its own shopping service.¹⁵ These cases demonstrate that the government will intervene when large tech firms are perceived to stifle competition.

In the AI context, courts have yet to establish a clear test for whether coordination among firms constitutes anticompetitive conduct. However, the Supreme Court’s 2021 decision in *United States v. Microsoft Corp.* emphasized that the key question is whether the conduct has a substantial effect on competition.¹⁶ The plaintiffs argue that delaying model releases has a substantial effect on competition by limiting the frequency of new entrants and innovations. The defendants counter that the delays are driven by safety and regulatory compliance, not by a desire to suppress competition.

Expert commentary

Legal scholars have offered divergent views on the case. Professor Emily Chen of Stanford Law School, an expert on antitrust law, said, “If the plaintiffs can prove that the coordination was intentional and that it had a substantial anticompetitive effect, the case could be a landmark for the AI industry.”¹⁷ Professor Chen added that the fact that OpenAI is a capped‑profit company does not exempt it from antitrust liability.¹⁸

AI ethicist Dr. Luis Martinez of MIT’s CSAIL noted, “The industry’s rush to deploy powerful models has outpaced the development of safety protocols. If firms are coordinating to delay releases, it may be a sign that they are prioritizing regulatory compliance over innovation.”¹⁹

From an industry perspective, analyst James O’Connor of Gartner cautioned that “any ruling that confirms anticompetitive conduct could shake investor confidence and lead to stricter regulatory oversight.”²⁰ O’Connor also warned that “a dismissal may reinforce the status quo, allowing firms to continue coordinating under the guise of safety.”

Regulatory context

The lawsuit arrives amid heightened scrutiny from U.S. regulators. The FTC has announced an investigation into AI firms’ data practices, while the DOJ has opened a probe into potential antitrust violations by major tech companies.¹¹ The European Union’s AI Act, slated for enforcement in 2025, imposes strict requirements on high‑risk AI systems, including transparency and safety obligations. The plaintiffs argue that the alleged coordination was aimed at avoiding the EU AI Act’s requirements.¹

Regulators have expressed interest in the case. FTC Commissioner Sarah Bloom, in a briefing, said, “We are monitoring developments in the AI sector closely. Any evidence of collusion that undermines competition is a concern for us.”²¹ The DOJ’s Antitrust Division has not yet issued a statement on the lawsuit, but a spokesperson indicated that the agency is reviewing the complaint.²²

Potential implications

If the court finds evidence of collusion, the case could set a new benchmark for how AI firms are held accountable for market‑shaping decisions. A ruling in favor of the plaintiffs could lead to:

* **Increased regulatory scrutiny**: The FTC and DOJ may launch broader investigations into AI firms’ competitive practices. * **Changes in industry collaboration**: Firms may be more cautious about joint initiatives that could be perceived as anticompetitive. * **Investor sentiment shifts**: Investors may demand greater transparency and risk‑mitigation measures, potentially affecting valuations and funding flows. * **Legal precedent**: The case could influence how courts interpret antitrust law in the context of rapidly evolving technology sectors.

Conversely, a dismissal could reinforce the status quo, allowing firms to continue coordinating under the guise of safety and regulatory compliance. It could also signal to regulators that the current legal framework is sufficient to address antitrust concerns in AI, potentially delaying further regulatory action.

What to watch

The case will unfold over several key milestones:

1. **Preliminary injunction**: The court’s decision on the injunction will determine whether the alleged slowdown can continue. A denial could force the companies to accelerate releases, while an injunction could halt further delays. 2. **Discovery**: The availability of internal communications and the ability to prove intent will be pivotal in establishing liability. The plaintiffs’ success in obtaining emails and meeting minutes will be closely scrutinized. 3. **Regulatory response**: The FTC and DOJ may use the lawsuit as a springboard to launch broader investigations, potentially leading to stricter compliance requirements for AI firms. 4. **Market reaction**: Investors will monitor court rulings and regulatory announcements, as these could affect valuations and funding flows for AI startups and established firms alike. 5. **Industry behavior**: The outcome may influence how AI companies approach collaboration and competition in the future, potentially reshaping the industry’s trajectory.

Conclusion

The lawsuit against Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google is more than a legal dispute over release schedules; it is a litmus test for the AI ecosystem’s relationship with law, policy, and public trust. Whether the court finds evidence of anticompetitive conduct will have far‑reaching implications for regulatory oversight, industry collaboration, and investor confidence. As the case proceeds, stakeholders across the spectrum will be watching closely to see whether the alleged collusion, if proven, will rewrite the rules of the AI race or reinforce the existing framework for innovation and competition.
