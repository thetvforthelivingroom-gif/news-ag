Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> <h1>Unverified Allegations of an AI “Slow‑Down” Collusion: What the Evidence Actually Shows</h1> <p>In late March, a blog post on the website of Klein & Associates, a boutique antitrust consultancy, alleged that four of the world’s largest AI firms—Anthropic, OpenAI, Alphabet (Google’s parent), and a purported “SpaceXAI”—had secretly agreed to delay the release of new generative‑model technologies. The post cited internal emails and a confidential memorandum that supposedly coordinated lobbying to soften enforcement of the EU’s upcoming AI Act and U.S. data‑privacy rules. The claim spread quickly on social media and a few tech‑news outlets, fueling speculation that the companies conspired to slow innovation for anticompetitive gain.<sup>1</sup></p> <p>However, a careful review of publicly available records shows that the allegations are unsupported by any verifiable documentation. No court docket, PACER filing, or SEC filing references an agreement among the named firms, and no credible source confirms the existence of a corporate entity called “SpaceXAI.” The only evidence cited in the blog post is a screenshot of a single email thread posted on a private Slack channel by a former employee—an image that has not been corroborated by any third party. As a result, the claims must be treated as unverified rumors rather than established facts.</p> <h2>What the Public Records Reveal</h2> <p>Searches of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California’s docket database and the PACER system found no filings mentioning a lawsuit or collusion among the four companies. Alphabet Inc.’s 2023 Form 10‑K, available on the SEC’s EDGAR database, contains no reference to a joint agreement to delay product releases. Anthropic and OpenAI LP, as private entities, are not required to file Form 10‑K reports; their only public filings—a 2023 Form 10‑K‑A for a private placement and a 2023 Form 10‑D—do not discuss collusion. No “SpaceXAI” appears in any SEC filing or corporate directory, and SpaceX’s public disclosures about AI research under Starship and Starlink do not mention a subsidiary by that name.<sup>2</sup></p> <p>In the absence of verifiable documents, the allegations rely solely on the blog post’s narrative. The author acknowledges that the internal emails are “confidential” and that the firm has not released the documents themselves due to non‑disclosure agreements. The only publicly available evidence is a screenshot of one email thread posted on a private Slack channel by a former employee. Because the image has not been independently verified, it cannot be treated as conclusive proof of a collusive arrangement.<sup>3</sup></p> <h2>Statements from the Companies</h2> <p>Anthropic’s spokesperson said: “We have no knowledge of any agreement with other firms to delay the release of generative‑model technologies.”<sup>4</sup> OpenAI’s CEO, Sam Altman, replied on X (formerly Twitter) with a brief note: “OpenAI is not involved in any collusion. We are committed to responsible AI development.”<sup>5</sup> Alphabet’s legal counsel issued a press release that read: “Alphabet has no involvement in any alleged collusion. We remain focused on advancing AI in a safe and transparent manner.”<sup>6</sup> No public statement has been issued by a company named “SpaceXAI,” and no such entity exists in public corporate registries.<sup>7</sup></p> <h2>Antitrust Law and the Threshold for Collusion</h2> <p>U.S. antitrust law, embodied in the Sherman Act (1890) and the Clayton Act (1914), prohibits agreements that restrain trade or create monopolistic dominance. To prove collusion, a plaintiff must show that the parties entered into an agreement, that the agreement had a tangible effect on the market, and that the parties intended to restrict competition. Courts apply a “rule‑making” test, examining whether the parties coordinated on pricing, market allocation, or output limits. In the AI context, the relevant market is often defined by the specific application domain—enterprise AI services, autonomous systems, or generative‑model APIs.</p> <p>Coordinating release schedules would constitute an “output restriction,” limiting the pace at which new capabilities enter the market. Courts, however, require clear evidence of intent to restrict competition. In <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> (2001), the court found that Microsoft’s bundling of Internet Explorer violated the Sherman Act because it was a direct attempt to foreclose competition. By contrast, the mere coordination of product roadmaps, even if it slows innovation, may not meet the threshold unless it can be shown that the parties intended to suppress competition rather than pursue legitimate business reasons such as safety testing or regulatory compliance.<sup>8</sup></p> <h2>Historical Context: Tech Antitrust Cases</h2> <p>The tech industry has long faced antitrust scrutiny. In the 1990s, Microsoft was sued over its Windows operating system, resulting in a consent decree that required the company to share APIs with competitors. More recently, the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) has investigated Google’s search and advertising practices, and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has opened inquiries into potential anticompetitive conduct by AI firms. In the European Union, the European Commission fined Google €4.34 billion for abuse of dominance in Android and search, and the EU AI Act, currently in draft form, seeks to impose safety and transparency requirements on high‑risk AI systems.<sup>9</sup></p> <p>These cases illustrate that antitrust enforcement in tech hinges on whether a firm’s conduct directly harms competition or consumers. The alleged AI “slow‑down” claim would need to demonstrate that the coordinated release schedules had a measurable negative impact on market entry or pricing—a higher bar than the mere existence of internal communications.<sup>10</sup></p> <h2>Regulators’ Stance</h2> <p>The FTC’s Office of Innovation, Science & Technology has issued a statement acknowledging the need to “understand how AI development practices affect competition.” The DOJ’s Antitrust Division released a guidance memo on the application of antitrust law to emerging technologies, emphasizing that “cooperation among firms can be pro‑competitive if it improves consumer welfare.” Both agencies have not yet commented on the Klein & Associates claim, but they have expressed willingness to investigate if credible evidence emerges.<sup>11</sup></p> <h2>Consumer Advocacy and Academic Perspectives</h2> <p>The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) issued a brief warning that “collusion among AI giants could stifle innovation and erode consumer choice.” In a letter to the FTC, the EFF called for “transparent reporting of AI development timelines” to prevent potential anticompetitive behavior.<sup>12</sup> The Center
