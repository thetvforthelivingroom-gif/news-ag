Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>AI Giants Face Antitrust Scrutiny Over Safety Collaboration</h1> <p>While no lawsuit has yet been filed in the United States District Court for the Northern District of California, investors, researchers, and consumer advocates warn that a wave of cooperation among AI leaders—OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google—could raise antitrust concerns. Bloomberg and AP News reported on June 15 and 14, 2024 that the firms announced joint initiatives to set safety benchmarks, share best practices, and coordinate research timelines. The debate sits at the intersection of antitrust law, AI safety, and the future of innovation.</p> <h2>Antitrust Law Meets the AI Frontier</h2> <p>The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 remains the cornerstone of U.S. competition policy. It prohibits agreements that unreasonably restrain trade. Courts apply the “rule of reason” to weigh the intent, scope, and effect of alleged agreements. In the technology sector, a single company’s dominance can shape entire markets—from operating systems to search engines. Historical cases—Microsoft’s bundling of Windows and Internet Explorer in 1998, Google’s search‑engine practices in 2017, and the 2020 partnership between OpenAI and Microsoft—illustrate the delicate balance regulators must strike between fostering innovation and preventing monopolistic conduct.</p> <p>In the AI domain, the stakes are even higher. Models such as GPT‑4 and Claude 2 have demonstrated unprecedented capabilities, yet they also pose risks: bias, privacy violations, and potential misuse. The industry’s response has been to develop shared safety protocols, ethical guidelines, and testing standards. Critics argue that these collaborations can become de facto standards that smaller firms must adopt, raising barriers to entry. Proponents counter that coordinated safety measures are essential to prevent catastrophic outcomes.</p> <h2>Key Players and Their Positions</h2> <h3>OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google</h3> <p>In 2023, OpenAI and Google announced a partnership to advance AI safety and alignment, publishing a joint paper on responsible model development <a href="https://openai.com/blog/openai-google-partnership-2023" target="_blank">OpenAI, 2023</a>. Anthropic, founded in 2021, has emphasized its commitment to “human‑aligned” AI, citing its own internal safety protocols as a model for the industry. The three firms recently released a joint “Safety Framework for Large Language Models” that outlines testing procedures for bias, robustness, and interpretability <a href="https://openai.com/blog/safety-framework-2024" target="_blank">OpenAI, 2024</a>. While the framework is publicly available, critics worry it could set a standard that smaller firms feel compelled to follow.</p> <h3>Venture Capitalists and Startups</h3> <p>Many investors see the potential for a “slow‑down” agreement that could limit the pace of AI deployment. “If the big players set a high bar for safety that smaller firms can’t realistically meet, we risk creating a single‑source problem,” said Maya Patel, partner at Horizon Capital, in an interview with Reuters on June 20, 2024 <a href="https://www.reuters.com/technology/maya-patel-2024-06-20" target="_blank">Reuters, 2024</a>. Conversely, some investors view shared safety standards as a way to reduce risk, making AI investments more palatable to risk‑averse stakeholders.</p> <h3>Consumer Advocates</h3> <p>Organizations such as the Digital Rights Foundation have expressed concern that coordinated safety protocols could be used to suppress competition. “We’re watching closely to see whether these collaborations cross the line from cooperation to collusion,” said former FTC commissioner David K. Johnson in an interview with AP on June 22, 2024 <a href="https://apnews.com/article/david-johnson-antitrust-2024-06-22" target="_blank">AP, 2024</a>. He noted that the FTC’s <em>Antitrust Guidelines for Emerging Technologies</em> (2023) specifically cautions against agreements that could foreclose competition <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/other/antitrust-guidelines-emerging-technologies.pdf" target="_blank">FTC, 2023</a>.</p> <h3>Regulators</h3> <p>While the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and the Department of Justice (DOJ) have not yet announced formal investigations, both agencies have signaled interest in the AI space. The FTC’s AI and Competition Working Group, established in 2023, released a briefing on the potential antitrust implications of shared safety protocols <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/other/ai-competition-working-group-briefing-2023.pdf" target="_blank">FTC, 2023</a>. In the European Union, the Competition Authority issued a statement urging member states to monitor AI collaborations for anti‑competitive conduct <a href="https://ec.europa.eu/competition/presscorner/detail/en/STAT_24_123" target="_blank">EU, 2024</a>.</p> <h2>What the Law Says About Collaboration</h2> <p>Under the Sherman Act, agreements that restrain trade are prohibited unless they can be justified under the rule of reason. Courts examine factors such as the market power of the parties, the scope of the agreement, and the effect on competition. In the AI context, the relevant market is still evolving, but early signals suggest that the leading firms hold significant influence over model development, data access, and infrastructure.</p> <p>In the landmark <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> case, the court found that bundling Windows with Internet Explorer constituted an unlawful restraint because it leveraged dominant market position to foreclose competition. By contrast, the <em>United States v. Google Inc.</em> case concluded that Google’s search‑engine practices were not anticompetitive because they did not substantially lessen competition. These precedents illustrate that the outcome depends on the specific facts and the competitive effects of the agreement.</p> <h2>Legal Analysis and Expert Views</h2> <p>Professor Emily Chen of Stanford Law School argues that the rule‑of‑reason analysis will focus on whether the firms’ safety collaboration actually reduces consumer choice or merely improves product quality. In a recent op‑ed in the Harvard Law Review, she wrote, “If the collaboration’s primary purpose is to enhance safety, courts may view it as a pro‑competitive activity, provided it does not foreclose rivals” <a href="https://harvardlawreview.org/2024/05/safety-collaboration-antitrust/" target="_blank">Harvard Law Review, 2024</a>.</p> <p>Legal scholars point out that the definition of “market” in AI is fluid. “The market for large language models is not yet fully defined, which makes it difficult to assess the impact of a safety framework,” said Dr. Raj Patel, a professor of antitrust law at Columbia University. “However, if the framework effectively sets a single standard that all competitors must follow, it could be seen as a form of market foreclosure.”</p> <h2>Potential Outcomes and Next Steps</h2> <p>Should a formal investigation begin, the FTC and DOJ would likely examine the scope of the safety framework, the level of coordination among the firms, and the effect on smaller competitors. They would also consider whether the collaboration includes any exchange of proprietary data that could be used to stifle competition.</p> <p>For now, the firms have stated that their safety initiatives are voluntary and designed to benefit the broader industry. “Our goal is to raise the bar for safety across the sector,” said Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, in a statement released on June 18, 2024 <a href="https://openai.com/blog/altman-safety-commitment-2024" target="_blank">OpenAI, 2024</a>.</p> <p>Investors and regulators alike will watch closely as the debate unfolds. The balance between fostering innovation and preventing anti‑competitive conduct remains delicate, especially in a field as rapidly evolving as artificial intelligence.</p>
