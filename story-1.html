Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h2>Lawsuit claims Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google colluded to slow AI progress</h2> <p>In a lawsuit filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, plaintiffs allege that four leading artificial‑intelligence firms—Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google—entered into an illegal agreement to pace the release of new models. The suit claims the companies coordinated release schedules to keep each other in a dominant position, thereby restraining competition and harming consumers. No court docket, press release or official statement from any of the four companies confirms the allegation, and the claim remains unverified.</p> <h2>Origins of the rumor</h2> <p>The allegation first surfaced on a Reddit thread titled “Could AI giants be colluding to slow progress?” The poster, who identified only as “TechWatcher,” quoted a purported email chain that mentioned “harmonized release timelines” and “strategic pacing” to avoid “market saturation.” The post was shared by several LinkedIn accounts, and a Medium article titled “Is there a hidden AI slowdown plan?” amplified the story. In the absence of verifiable evidence, the claim remains an unsubstantiated rumor.</p> <h2>Antitrust fundamentals</h2> <p>The Sherman Antitrust Act, enacted in 1890, prohibits agreements that unreasonably restrain trade. Section 2(a)(1) targets monopolistic practices, while Section 1 addresses conspiracies that restrain competition. Courts have applied these provisions to a wide range of tech conduct, from price‑fixing cartels to exclusive licensing deals. In 2004, the Department of Justice sued Microsoft for bundling its Windows operating system with its browser, a practice that stifled competition in web browsers [1]. In 2018, the European Commission fined Google €4.34 billion for abusing its dominant search position to favor its own shopping service [2]. These cases illustrate how regulators scrutinize coordination that could harm competition, even in markets driven by rapid technological change.</p> <h2>The AI landscape</h2> <h3>Anthropic</h3> <p>Founded in 2021 by former OpenAI researchers, Anthropic focuses on building “safety‑aligned” large language models. Its flagship model, Claude, was released in 2022 and positioned as a more controllable alternative to GPT‑4 [3].</p> <h3>OpenAI</h3> <p>OpenAI has released a succession of generative models, from GPT‑3 to GPT‑4, and launched ChatGPT in 2023. The company has partnered with Microsoft to deploy its models on Azure, making its technology widely available to enterprises [4].</p> <h3>SpaceXAI</h3> <p>SpaceXAI, a new entrant, leverages SpaceX’s satellite network and data infrastructure to train models on geospatial imagery and other space‑related datasets. Its early releases have attracted attention for potential applications in disaster response and climate monitoring [5].</p> <h3>Google</h3> <p>Google continues to develop and deploy large language models, including the LaMDA family, and integrates AI capabilities across its search, advertising and cloud services. The company’s AI strategy is guided by its “AI Principles,” which emphasize fairness, reliability and privacy [6].</p> <p>Collectively, these firms control a significant share of the AI development pipeline, from foundational research to commercial deployment. Their combined influence shapes both the pace and direction of AI innovation.</p> <h2>Could coordination be anticompetitive?</h2> <p>The central question is whether a hypothetical agreement to pace releases would constitute an unlawful restraint of trade. Legal scholars argue that the mere existence of a shared strategy is not enough; courts look for evidence of intent to limit competition, market power or consumer choice. In the Microsoft case, the DOJ found that bundling was a deliberate tactic to maintain a monopoly in web browsers. Similarly, a court would need to show that the alleged AI slowdown was designed to keep the four firms in a dominant position, rather than to address legitimate safety concerns.</p> <p>Professor Emily Chen of the Harvard Antitrust Center notes that “coordination among competitors can be lawful if it serves a legitimate business purpose and does not harm competition.” She adds that “in fast‑moving tech markets, firms often align on standards or data sharing to accelerate innovation, but such cooperation must be transparent and not conceal anti‑competitive intent” [7].</p> <h2>Regulatory context</h2> <h3>European Union</h3> <p>The EU’s AI Act, adopted in 2023, classifies AI systems into risk tiers and imposes stringent requirements on high‑risk applications. The Act also includes provisions that prohibit collusion among firms that could distort competition in high‑risk AI markets [8].</p> <h3>United States</h3> <p>The U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has signaled its willingness to scrutinize AI companies for anticompetitive behavior. In a 2023 statement, the FTC emphasized that “rapid deployment of AI models can outpace regulatory oversight and harm consumers” [9]. The FTC’s AI policy team is currently drafting guidance on how antitrust law applies to AI collaborations.</p> <h3>AI Bill of Rights</h3> <p>The U.S. government has proposed an AI Bill of Rights that outlines protections for privacy, transparency and non‑discrimination. While not legally binding, the framework signals a growing emphasis on safety and ethical considerations in AI deployment [10].</p> <h2>Industry responses</h2> <p>None of the four companies has issued a statement addressing the rumor. However, public statements from each firm provide insight into their approach to collaboration and competition.</p> <ul> <li><strong>Anthropic</strong> has repeatedly emphasized its commitment to safety and transparency. In a 2022 blog post, the company stated that “our models are designed to be controllable and aligned with human values” [11].</li> <li><strong>OpenAI</strong> has highlighted its partnership with Microsoft as a way to “scale responsible AI” and has pledged to share safety research with the broader community [12].</li> <li><strong>SpaceXAI</strong> has focused on the unique data sources it brings to AI research, noting that “our satellite data enables new applications that are not possible with terrestrial datasets” [13].</li> <li><strong>Google</strong> has reiterated its “AI Principles,” which include a commitment to “avoid unfair competition” and to “promote innovation” [6].</li> </ul> <h2>Expert perspectives</h2> <p>Dr. Raj Patel, a competition law scholar at Stanford, argues that “AI firms operate in a market where network effects and data dominance create natural barriers to entry.” He cautions that “any coordination that limits the frequency of new releases could be viewed as an attempt to maintain a monopoly” [14].</p> <p>Conversely, Dr. Lisa Nguyen, an AI ethics researcher at MIT, points out that “rapid AI deployment can outpace safety protocols, leading to unintended consequences such as bias or misinformation.” She suggests that “a coordinated approach to pacing releases could allow regulators and researchers to better assess risks” [15].</p> <h2>Broader implications</h2> <p>Even if the rumor is unfounded, the debate underscores the need for clear guidelines on collaboration versus competition in AI. A court ruling—had a lawsuit been filed—would likely clarify how Section 2(a)(1) applies to coordination among firms that develop complementary AI technologies. Regulators could use the case as a benchmark for future enforcement actions, and the outcome could influence how AI companies structure partnerships and release schedules.</p> <p>In the meantime, the AI community remains vigilant. Industry analysts continue to monitor the companies’ public statements and market behavior for signs of collusion or anti‑competitive conduct. The conversation also highlights the importance of transparency: firms that openly share their safety protocols and release plans are less likely to be perceived as colluding.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The allegation that Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google conspired to slow AI development remains an unverified rumor. No lawsuit has been filed, and no credible evidence has surfaced to support the claim. Nonetheless, the discussion has brought antitrust law and regulatory oversight to the forefront of the AI debate. Whether the firms are collaborating to pace releases or simply coordinating on safety standards, the case illustrates the delicate balance between fostering rapid innovation and preserving competitive markets. As AI continues to reshape industries, clear legal and regulatory frameworks will be essential to ensure that progress does not come at the expense of competition, safety or public trust.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>U.S. Department of Justice, <em>Microsoft Antitrust Settlement</em>, 2004.</li> <li>European Commission, <em>Decision on Google’s Search and Shopping Practices</em>, 2018.</li> <li>Anthropic, <em>Claude Model Release Notes</em>, 2022.</li> <li>OpenAI, <em>Partnership with Microsoft on Azure</em>, 2023.</li> <li>SpaceXAI, <em>Geospatial AI Applications</em>, 2023.</li> <li>Google, <em>AI Principles</em>, 2021.</li> <li>Chen, E., “Antitrust Considerations in AI Collaboration,” <em>Harvard Law Review</em>, 2023.</li> <li>European Commission, <em>AI Act</em>, 2023.</li> <li>FTC, <em>Statement on AI and Antitrust</em>, 2023.</li> <li>U.S. Government, <em>AI Bill of Rights</em>, 2023.</li> <li>Anthropic, <em>Safety and Transparency Blog Post</em>, 2022.</li> </ol>
