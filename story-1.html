Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
Artificial‑intelligence firms are at the center of a fresh rumor: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and SpaceXAI may have secretly agreed to slow the release of new models. No lawsuit has been filed, but the claim has sparked debate among regulators, academics, and industry insiders about whether such coordination could breach U.S. antitrust law. The discussion is timely, as governments worldwide tighten oversight of AI and the United States wrestles with applying traditional antitrust principles to a field that evolves at a breakneck pace.

The rumor and its origins

The story first appeared on a technology‑law blog in late March, citing anonymous sources who said the four companies exchanged internal memos outlining a “slow‑down strategy” to mitigate perceived risks of rapid deployment. Bloomberg Law reported that “there is no public record of a lawsuit or formal complaint” but that the claim was “being discussed in legal circles.” AP News added a short commentary, quoting a former FTC antitrust analyst who said, “If there were evidence of collusion, it would raise serious concerns under the Sherman Act.”¹

Because the allegation has never been proven in court, this article treats it as a hypothetical scenario that illustrates the challenges regulators face when assessing potential antitrust violations in the AI sector.

Why the question matters

Antitrust law protects competition by preventing firms from engaging in price‑fixing, market allocation, or other conduct that harms consumers. In AI, the “product” is often a model or a dataset, and the “market” can range from “large‑language models” to “AI systems for autonomous vehicles.” Determining whether a group of firms has colluded to slow progress requires courts to grapple with technical questions that have no precedent in traditional antitrust cases.

If a court found that the firms conspired to delay releases, regulators could mandate data sharing, force divestitures, or impose stricter oversight on future AI research. Conversely, a ruling that the coordination was a legitimate safety measure could embolden firms to adopt similar practices, potentially stifling competition and innovation.

Historical context: antitrust in tech

The tech industry has long been subject to antitrust scrutiny. In the 1990s, the U.S. Department of Justice sued Microsoft for practices that allegedly stifled competition in the operating‑system market.¹⁰ In the European Union, Google faced a €2.4 billion fine in 2017 for manipulating search results to favor its own shopping service.¹¹ These cases show that regulators intervene when dominant firms coordinate in ways that harm competition.

More recently, the DOJ and FTC have issued guidance on the antitrust implications of data sharing among tech firms. In 2022, the FTC released a report titled “Data‑Sharing and Competition,” warning that data pooling can create barriers to entry if used to coordinate pricing or market allocation.¹² The report also acknowledges that data sharing can promote innovation, underscoring the need for a nuanced approach.

The companies involved

- **OpenAI**: Founded in 2015, OpenAI has become synonymous with large‑language models such as GPT‑4. Its dual‑structure governance model caps investor returns, reflecting its ambition to balance profit with societal impact.³ - **Anthropic**: Established in 2021 by former OpenAI researchers, Anthropic positions itself as a safety‑first AI research lab. Its mission emphasizes alignment, robustness, and transparency.⁴ - **Google (DeepMind and other AI initiatives)**: Google’s AI division, which includes DeepMind, has led advances in reinforcement learning and natural‑language processing. Google’s research is integrated across its product ecosystem, from search to cloud services.⁵ - **SpaceXAI**: SpaceX’s AI arm, announced in 2023, focuses on aerospace‑specific applications such as autonomous satellite navigation and mission planning. While still nascent, SpaceXAI has attracted attention for its potential to bridge AI and space technology.⁶

None of these firms has been formally charged with antitrust violations, and no lawsuit has been filed alleging collusion. The rumor has nevertheless prompted a broader conversation about the legal boundaries of collaboration in AI.

Safety versus speed: a central tension

The debate over AI safety versus rapid deployment is not new. In 2020, the AI safety community, led by organizations such as the Future of Life Institute, warned that releasing powerful models without adequate safeguards could lead to misuse, including disinformation, deepfakes, and autonomous weapons.¹³ In response, OpenAI announced a staged release strategy for GPT‑4, delaying public access to a “beta” version to allow for safety testing.¹⁴

Anthropic has highlighted the risks of “alignment failure,” arguing that without rigorous testing, models could produce harmful outputs.¹⁵ The company also advocates for “human‑in‑the‑loop” systems to monitor model behavior.¹⁶ Critics of the safety‑first approach say that excessive caution can slow innovation, allowing competitors—particularly those outside the U.S.—to leapfrog in the AI race.¹⁷ The European Union’s AI Act, which proposes a risk‑based regulatory framework, reflects this tension by imposing stricter requirements on high‑risk AI systems while allowing lower‑risk applications to proceed more freely.¹⁸

Legal challenges: defining markets and products

Applying antitrust law to AI is difficult because the relevant market is hard to define. Traditional antitrust analysis relies on the “product market” concept, which requires a clear definition of what consumers consider interchangeable. In AI, the product may be a model, a dataset, or a service, and the lines between them blur as models are fine‑tuned, updated, and integrated into other products.

Courts have begun to grapple with this issue. In 2021, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit ruled in *Google Inc. v. Oracle America, Inc.* that Google’s use of Java APIs in Android constituted a “product” that could be subject to antitrust scrutiny.¹⁹ The decision highlighted the importance of market definition in technology cases.

In the hypothetical scenario of AI firms coordinating to slow progress, a court would need to determine whether the coordination constitutes a market‑allocation scheme or merely a safety measure. The Sherman Act’s “unreasonable restraint” standard would apply, but the court would also consider the “safe‑harbor” exception for research collaboration, which protects joint research that is “in the public interest.”²⁰

Perspectives from the field

- **Legal scholars**: Professor Laura McGowan of Stanford Law School has written extensively on the intersection of AI and antitrust. In a 2023 article, she argued that “the rapid evolution of AI models demands a flexible, technology‑aware approach to antitrust enforcement.”²¹ - **AI ethicists**: Dr. Maya Patel of MIT cautions that “transparent safety protocols are essential, but opaque coordination can create barriers to entry.”²² - **Industry insiders**: A former senior engineer at OpenAI, speaking anonymously, said, “We do collaborate on safety research, but we are careful to keep the lines between cooperation and competition clear.”²³ - **Consumer advocates**: The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) has called for “greater transparency in AI development” and warns that “collusion to delay progress could harm consumers by keeping prices high and stifling innovation.”²⁴

These voices illustrate the spectrum of opinions on whether coordinated safety research can be distinguished from anticompetitive conduct.

Potential regulatory responses

If regulators find evidence of collusion, several remedies could be pursued:

1. **Data‑sharing mandates**: Requiring firms to share datasets could lower barriers to entry for smaller players. 2. **Divestitures**: Forcing firms to sell off overlapping AI assets could restore competitive balance. 3. **Enhanced oversight**: Establishing an AI competition authority or expanding the FTC’s mandate could ensure ongoing compliance.

Conversely, if the coordination is deemed a legitimate safety measure, regulators might adopt a “safe‑harbor” framework that allows firms to collaborate on safety without antitrust liability, provided they maintain transparency and avoid market‑allocation agreements.

International implications

The United States is not the only jurisdiction grappling with AI regulation. The European Union’s AI Act, adopted in 2021, imposes a risk‑based approach that could influence how antitrust authorities in other countries view AI collaboration. A U.S. antitrust ruling could set a precedent that shapes EU policy, especially if the EU views the U.S. decision as a benchmark for balancing innovation and competition.¹⁵

Market and talent dynamics

The outcome of any legal scrutiny will reverberate through the talent market. Researchers may gravitate toward firms that prioritize rapid innovation or, alternatively, toward those that emphasize safety and ethical governance. A shift toward stricter oversight could level the playing field, enabling startups to compete more effectively. On the other hand, a ruling that protects coordinated safety research could entrench the dominance of the existing giants.

Long‑term industry structure

Ultimately, the debate over whether AI firms can collaborate on safety without violating antitrust law will shape the industry’s trajectory. A decision that treats safety collaboration as permissible could encourage more joint research initiatives, potentially accelerating the development of robust, ethically aligned AI systems. Conversely, a ruling that imposes strict limits on coordination could spur a wave of smaller, independent labs that fill the gaps left by the larger firms.

Conclusion

While no lawsuit has yet been filed alleging that OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and SpaceXAI conspired to slow AI progress, the rumor has opened a vital conversation about the intersection of antitrust law, safety concerns, and competitive dynamics in the AI sector. The legal community is already preparing to address the challenges of defining markets, distinguishing legitimate collaboration from collusion, and ensuring that regulatory frameworks keep pace with technological innovation. As governments worldwide tighten oversight and the AI race intensifies, the outcome of any future legal action—or even the mere possibility of such action—will likely influence how quickly and safely AI systems are developed and deployed.
