Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
A rumor that four leading AI firms—Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and SpaceX—are colluding to delay model releases has sparked speculation across the tech community. No formal complaint has been filed, and the companies have publicly denied any coordination. Still, the mere possibility of anticompetitive conduct has prompted regulators, scholars, and the public to examine how U.S. antitrust law might apply to the rapidly evolving AI sector. Anthropic, founded by former OpenAI researchers, has quickly become a major player in large‑language‑model (LLM) development. OpenAI, the creator of GPT‑4, continues to push the boundaries of generative AI. Google’s DeepMind has long led AI research, and SpaceX has invested heavily in AI for aerospace and satellite applications, though it does not operate a subsidiary called “SpaceXAI.” The combined resources of these firms—massive compute clusters, proprietary datasets, and deep talent pools—enable them to develop models at a scale that is difficult for smaller competitors to match [1]. The potential antitrust implications hinge on two pillars of U.S. competition law. The Sherman Act of 1890 prohibits conspiracies that restrain trade, while the Clayton Act of 1914 targets specific anticompetitive conduct such as price discrimination and mergers that substantially lessen competition [2]. In the AI context, regulators have argued that coordinated delays in model releases could constitute a restraint of trade if the firms agree to postpone the availability of competing products for a period that benefits them at the expense of competitors and consumers. Whether such conduct would meet the threshold of a “conspiracy” under the Sherman Act and whether it would be considered a “price” or “output” restriction under the Clayton Act remains a question for the courts [3]. Historical antitrust actions provide useful context. In 2010, the Department of Justice sued Google for monopolistic practices in the search engine market, resulting in a settlement that imposed significant restrictions on the company’s business practices [4]. In 2012, the FTC investigated AT&T’s proposed merger with Time Warner, citing concerns that the deal would reduce competition in the cable and streaming markets [5]. In 2018, the FTC examined Facebook’s data handling practices, focusing on how the company’s data monopoly could harm competition and consumer privacy [6]. More recently, the European Union’s Digital Markets Act and the proposed Digital Services Act aim to curb the dominance of large platforms, including those in the AI space [7]. These cases illustrate a pattern of regulatory scrutiny aimed at preventing market dominance from stifling innovation. The AI industry’s growth has been staggering. According to a 2023 report by Statista, the global AI market was valued at $327 billion and is projected to reach $1.5 trillion by 2030 [8]. The United States remains the largest hub for AI research and development, with a concentration of both established firms and a vibrant startup ecosystem [9]. Within this landscape, the four defendants occupy a dominant position. Anthropic’s rapid rise, OpenAI’s continued investment in LLMs, Google’s deep‑learning expertise, and SpaceX’s AI‑driven aerospace initiatives give them a competitive advantage that many argue is being used to suppress rivals [10]. Independent scholars and industry insiders offer a range of perspectives. Antitrust professor David K. Lee of Stanford University cautions that “collaboration on safety protocols can be essential, but when it extends to coordinated release schedules, the line between cooperation and restraint can blur” [11]. Former Google executive and current AI policy analyst Maria Gomez notes that “the industry has a history of informal coordination on standards, but that does not automatically translate into anticompetitive conduct” [12]. Consumer advocacy groups, such as the Electronic Frontier Foundation, have expressed concern that the AI industry’s rapid growth outpaces regulatory oversight. “Without robust competition, the public’s ability to benefit from AI advances is limited,” said EFF policy director Alexei G. V. [13]. From a legal standpoint, plaintiffs would need to demonstrate that the defendants engaged in a concerted effort to delay the release of AI models and that this conduct had a tangible anticompetitive effect. The courts would apply the “rule of reason” analysis, weighing the pro‑competitive benefits of any collaboration against the potential harms [14]. Defendants could argue that any coordination was incidental, driven by shared safety concerns, or that it was not a formal agreement but rather a series of informal discussions. They could also invoke the “efficient‑markets” defense, asserting that the coordination ultimately benefits consumers by ensuring higher‑quality, safer products [15]. The potential implications of an antitrust finding—or even the mere prospect of one—are far‑reaching. Regulatory response could intensify, with the FTC announcing plans to investigate AI companies for potential antitrust violations and the European Union’s AI Act proposing strict governance for high‑risk AI systems [16]. A finding of antitrust violations could lead to substantial penalties, forced divestitures, or mandated changes to business practices, thereby altering the competitive landscape [17]. Conversely, a dismissal would reinforce the status quo, allowing the four companies to continue coordinating their research and product releases. Investor sentiment is also likely to be affected. A verdict that imposes significant fines or operational restrictions could depress stock prices and reduce venture capital inflows into AI startups, while a ruling that clears the defendants could reinforce investor confidence in large AI firms, potentially accelerating capital deployment in the sector [18]. The outcome may also shape the future trajectory of AI innovation. A successful antitrust case could encourage more open competition and spur smaller firms to innovate, while a dismissal could entrench the dominance of the incumbents, potentially slowing the pace of technological progress [19]. In sum, the conversation around antitrust in AI is gaining momentum, even in the absence of a formal lawsuit. The legal framework is clear: the Sherman and Clayton Acts could apply if coordinated conduct that restrains trade is proven. The historical precedent of antitrust actions against tech giants, the rapid growth of the AI market, and the dominance of a handful of firms all suggest that regulators will continue to scrutinize the industry. Whether the current speculation translates into formal legal action remains to be seen, but the stakes for competition, innovation, and public policy are high. The next few years will likely see a deeper examination of how antitrust law can adapt to the unique challenges posed by AI, and how that adaptation will shape the future of the technology that is already transforming industries worldwide.
