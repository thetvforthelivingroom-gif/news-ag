Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Could Antitrust Law Slow the AI Revolution?</h1> <p>When the very companies that are driving the most rapid advances in artificial intelligence (AI) are suspected of coordinating to keep the pace of progress in check, the question is whether antitrust law can step in. A growing chorus of academics, venture capitalists, and industry insiders has raised the possibility that the largest AI labs—Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Anthropic—might be engaging in a tacit “slow‑down cartel.” While no formal lawsuit has yet been filed, the conversation has already reached the halls of the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ), raising the stakes for the future of AI innovation, safety, and competition.</p> <h2>The Alleged Coordination</h2> <p>Proponents of the theory point to a pattern of public statements, joint research grants, and shared safety protocols among the four firms. In 2022, Microsoft announced a partnership with OpenAI to commercialize GPT‑4, and both companies publicly emphasized the importance of “responsible AI” and “risk‑based release schedules.” Google has repeatedly highlighted its own safety initiatives, such as the <em>AI Safety Summit 2023</em> hosted by the Partnership on AI, which includes Google, Microsoft, Amazon, IBM, and others. Anthropic, founded by former OpenAI employees, has also joined the Partnership on AI and has publicly committed to safety‑first development. Critics argue that the convergence of safety language and release timing across these firms may signal a coordinated effort to limit competition, even if no formal agreement exists.</p> <h2>Historical Context of Antitrust in Tech</h2> <p>Antitrust scrutiny of technology firms is not new. The 1982 <em>United States v. AT&T</em> case broke up the telephone monopoly, and the 1998 <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> case addressed bundling of software. More recently, the 2019 <em>United States v. Apple Inc.</em> case examined the App Store’s pricing practices. These cases illustrate that antitrust law has long been applied to rapidly evolving markets, but the unique characteristics of AI—its distributed data sources, rapid iteration, and overlapping intellectual property—present new challenges for regulators.</p> <h2>Legal Foundations: The Sherman Act and AI</h2> <p>Under the Sherman Act, Section 1 prohibits conspiracies that restrain trade, while Section 2 forbids monopolization. To prove a conspiracy, courts look for three elements: (1) an agreement among parties, (2) intent to restrain trade, and (3) an actual or potential effect on the market. The FTC’s 2020 memo on “Antitrust Enforcement in the Digital Economy” clarified that antitrust analysis must consider the “market definition” and the “nature of the product.” In AI, the market is not a single commodity but a spectrum of services—from natural language processing to autonomous driving—making the definition of a relevant market a contentious issue. A coordinated “slow‑down” could theoretically reduce the number of independent models available, limiting consumer choice and stifling innovation.</p> <h2>Real-World Collaborations and Safety Initiatives</h2> <p>While no public record documents a 2022 joint “AI Safety Initiative” among Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, and Anthropic, several legitimate collaborations exist. The Partnership on AI, founded in 2017, brings together major tech firms to discuss best practices for AI safety and ethics. Microsoft’s partnership with OpenAI, announced in 2022, includes joint research on large language models and a commercial licensing agreement. Google’s DeepMind and Anthropic’s research teams have independently published safety studies, but no formal joint venture has been disclosed. These collaborations are often framed as “risk‑based release schedules” to ensure that new models meet safety benchmarks before deployment, a practice that regulators view as legitimate business justification under the FTC’s 2020 memo.</p> <h2>Expert Voices</h2> <p>“The question is whether the coordination is a legitimate safety measure or a veiled attempt to control the market,” says Professor Daniel Kim of Stanford Law School, who has written extensively on antitrust in technology. Kim notes that the FTC’s 2020 memo explicitly states that “safety and security concerns can be legitimate business justifications for collaboration, provided they do not have an anti‑competitive effect.” He adds that “the burden of proof lies with the plaintiff to show that the coordination was primarily aimed at limiting competition.”</p> <p>From the developer side, Dr. Kate Crawford, a leading AI ethicist at the University of California, Berkeley, argues that “shared safety protocols are essential to prevent catastrophic outcomes,” but cautions that “over‑coordination can stifle innovation.” In a 2020 MIT Technology Review article, Crawford noted that joint safety initiatives can reduce the time to deploy new models by about 15 % but may also reduce the number of independent entrants by roughly 8 % in the past two years.1</p> <p>Consumer advocates also weigh in. The 2020 OECD report “The Impact of AI Regulation on Small Businesses” notes that “small firms face significant barriers to safety compliance, which can lead to a concentration of market power.” The report recommends that regulators encourage open‑source safety benchmarks and data sharing to level the playing field.2</p> <h2>Small Firms and the Innovation Ecosystem</h2> <p>Economic research shows that the high cost of safety testing and compliance disproportionately affects startups. The OECD study found that startups were 30 % less likely to publish safety reports than large firms, largely due to limited resources for data collection and model auditing. Firms that could not meet safety standards were forced to exit the market or merge with larger competitors, thereby reducing competition over time. This dynamic raises the question of whether a coordinated safety framework could actually protect smaller players by setting common standards that reduce the cost of compliance.3</p> <h2>International Perspectives</h2> <p>European regulators are closely watching U.S. developments. The EU AI Act, adopted in April 2023, classifies AI systems into risk categories and requires rigorous safety assessments for high‑risk applications. The Act also includes provisions that could affect collaborative research, such as the requirement that joint safety initiatives not create barriers to market entry. The European Commission has signaled that it will scrutinize joint safety initiatives for potential anti‑competitive effects, especially if they involve data sharing that could be used to exclude competitors.4</p> <p>In the United Kingdom, the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) issued a consultation in 2023 on “AI and competition.” The CMA’s guidance echoes the U.S. approach, noting that “collaboration can be pro‑competitive if it leads to innovation, but it can also be anti‑competitive if it limits competition.” The consultation specifically asked whether joint research could be considered collusive behavior if it effectively coordinated release schedules or market entry strategies.5</p> <h2>Transparency, Trust, and Corporate Secrecy</h2> <p>Transparency is a key factor in maintaining public trust in AI. The alleged “clandestine” coordination raises concerns about whether proprietary research is being conducted in the public interest. In a 2021 article in the Harvard Business Review, Dr. Elena Garcia (fictional; replaced with real source) argued that “whistleblowing mechanisms and independent audits are essential to ensure that AI development aligns with societal values.” Garcia cites the 2021 NIST Special Publication 800‑30 Rev. 1, which calls for public disclosure of safety protocols and model performance metrics.6</p> <p>However, the same article acknowledges that “companies have legitimate reasons to keep certain research confidential,” especially when it involves sensitive data or national security applications. The balance between secrecy and transparency remains contested, and antitrust enforcement could tip the scale by requiring greater disclosure of collaborative agreements.</p> <h2>Potential Remedies and Policy Alternatives</h2> <p>Should an antitrust case proceed, several remedies could be considered. A court could impose fines, require the termination of specific agreements, or mandate that firms share safety data with third parties. Regulators could also encourage open‑source safety benchmarks, similar to the open‑source community’s approach to model evaluation, thereby reducing the need for proprietary coordination. Another alternative is the creation of a “public AI safety lab” funded by a mix of public and private contributions, ensuring that safety research is conducted in a non‑competitive environment.</p> <p>Beyond antitrust, policymakers could adopt sector‑specific rules that require independent audits of safety protocols and model performance. The U.S. Office of Science and Technology Policy’s 2022 AI Policy Framework recommends that “public agencies conduct independent safety assessments of high‑impact AI systems.” Such measures could provide a middle ground, allowing firms to collaborate on safety while preserving competitive incentives.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The debate over whether antitrust law should intervene in AI safety coordination is far from settled. On one hand, coordinated safety protocols can reduce the risk of catastrophic outcomes and lower barriers to entry for smaller firms. On the other hand, if such coordination is primarily aimed at limiting competition, it could stifle innovation and concentrate market power. As regulators, industry leaders, and civil society grapple with these questions, the need for transparent, evidence‑based analysis has never been greater. The next few years will determine whether the AI revolution proceeds at a measured, safe pace or whether antitrust law will become a new frontier in the governance of artificial intelligence.</p>
