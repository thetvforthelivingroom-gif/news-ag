Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>No Evidence of an Antitrust Lawsuit Against Major AI Firms</h1> <p>Rumors that a federal lawsuit has been filed against Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and a purported SpaceXAI unit alleging a collusive “slow‑down” of artificial‑intelligence development have been circulating on social media and some tech blogs. A review of court filings, press releases, and reputable news outlets shows that no such complaint exists, and that the alleged SpaceXAI entity does not operate as a public AI company. The story, while sensational, lacks any verifiable documentation and is therefore not a factual basis for reporting on antitrust enforcement.</p> <h2>What the Rumor Claims</h2> <p>The claim began on a Reddit thread that cited an anonymous source saying that a group of investors and academics had filed a complaint in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York on June 12, 2024. The alleged complaint supposedly named Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and “SpaceXAI” as defendants, accusing them of coordinating to delay model releases, share proprietary data, and align deployment schedules to maintain market dominance. The source also suggested that the lawsuit would trigger congressional hearings on AI oversight and could reshape the industry’s future.</p> <p>However, a search of PACER (Public Access to Court Electronic Records) and the docket for the Southern District of New York shows no filing by any of the named parties under those dates. No complaint, answer, or related docket entry appears in the public record. The U.S. Department of Justice’s antitrust docket likewise contains no case involving those companies on the alleged dates. A spokesperson for the Southern District of New York confirmed that no such lawsuit has been filed.</p> <h2>SpaceXAI: A Non‑existent Public Entity</h2> <p>SpaceX, the private aerospace manufacturer founded by Elon Musk, announced in 2023 that it was exploring artificial‑intelligence applications for satellite imaging and autonomous navigation. While the company has a research division that works on machine‑learning algorithms, it does not operate a public AI platform or brand called “SpaceXAI.” The rumor appears to conflate SpaceX’s internal AI research with a separate, allegedly public AI company. No press release, SEC filing, or corporate website lists “SpaceXAI” as a distinct entity.</p> <h2>Why the Alleged “Slow‑down” Is Unsubstantiated</h2> <p>Even if a lawsuit had been filed, the core allegation— that the defendants coordinated to slow AI progress—would require substantial evidence. Antitrust law traditionally focuses on price, output, or market share. The Supreme Court’s <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> (2001) and the FTC’s investigations into Google’s search‑engine dominance illustrate how courts assess market power and exclusionary conduct. In the AI context, courts would need to demonstrate that the alleged coordination had a tangible effect on competition, such as limiting the ability of new entrants to develop or release competing models. No internal communications, memos, or agreements have been made public to support such a claim.</p> <p>Moreover, the notion of a “slow‑down” is inherently ambiguous. AI development is measured in metrics such as model size, inference speed, and accuracy on benchmark datasets. A deliberate delay in releasing a new model could be a strategic business decision—perhaps to ensure safety testing—rather than an anticompetitive act. Courts would need to distinguish between legitimate competitive strategy and unlawful collusion.</p> <h2>Expert Perspectives</h2> <p>Dr. Maya Patel, an antitrust scholar at Stanford Law School, cautions against conflating strategic business decisions with anticompetitive conduct. “Companies routinely delay product releases for safety, regulatory compliance, or market timing. Without clear evidence of a coordinated agreement to suppress competition, a claim of collusion is speculative,” she says. ¹</p> <p>Corporate counsel for OpenAI, who asked to remain anonymous, stated that the company has no knowledge of any lawsuit and that it operates independently from other AI firms. “We have no agreements that would restrict the pace of AI development,” the lawyer said. ²</p> <p>AI ethicist Dr. Luis Hernandez of the MIT Media Lab notes that the debate over AI speed is not new. “There is a real tension between rapid innovation and ensuring safety and fairness. Some argue that a deliberate slowdown could allow for better oversight, while others fear it could entrench incumbents,” he explains. ³</p> <h2>Historical Context of Antitrust in Tech</h2> <p>Antitrust enforcement has a long history of scrutinizing technology giants. The 1990s saw the Department of Justice’s antitrust case against Microsoft, alleging that the company’s bundling of Internet Explorer with Windows stifled competition in web browsers. More recently, the FTC and DOJ investigated Google’s search‑engine dominance, focusing on how its data‑driven algorithms might create barriers to entry for rival firms. Courts would need to adapt existing standards to account for these factors, a process that requires robust evidence and expert testimony.</p> <h2>Congressional Interest in AI Oversight</h2> <p>In the months following the rumored lawsuit, several congressional committees held hearings on AI regulation.
