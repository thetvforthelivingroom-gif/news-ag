Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>No lawsuit exists: Fact‑checking the claim that Google, OpenAI, Anthropic and a fictitious SpaceXAI conspired to slow AI progress</h1> <p>A viral post on social media last week alleged that a U.S. court had received a complaint accusing Google, OpenAI, Anthropic and a so‑called “SpaceXAI” of colluding to deliberately slow the pace of artificial‑intelligence (AI) development. The post cited a secret agreement dating back to 2021, coordinated release schedules, data‑sharing pacts and lobbying efforts that would “stifle competition” under the Sherman Act. It also claimed that an AI had solved the Riemann Hypothesis, a claim that mathematicians have debunked. A quick review of court filings, company press releases and reputable news outlets shows that none of these allegations are grounded in fact.</p> <p>The story illustrates how the AI boom has made misinformation easier to spread. In the absence of verifiable evidence, claims of collusion or sabotage can mislead investors, regulators and the public. Below we set the record straight, provide the legal and technological context that makes such allegations plausible yet unsubstantiated, and explain why accurate reporting matters in an era of rapid technological change.</p> <h2>1. The claim and why it fails</h2> <p>The central claim—that four major AI firms entered a clandestine consortium to slow progress—has no basis in any court docket. A search of PACER, the federal court filing system, yields no complaint filed by any plaintiff against Google, OpenAI, Anthropic or any entity named “SpaceXAI” in the last decade. Google’s AI division is known as Google AI; there is no publicly documented subsidiary called SpaceXAI, and SpaceX’s publicly announced AI initiatives are limited to the “Starlink” satellite network and a handful of research projects that are not commercialized as a separate entity <sup>[1]</sup>.</p> <p>The alleged “SpaceXAI” is likely a confusion with SpaceX’s internal AI research, which focuses on autonomous flight and satellite operations. SpaceX has not announced a commercial AI platform comparable to OpenAI’s GPT series or Google’s LaMDA. The absence of a formal business entity named SpaceXAI means that any lawsuit naming it would be legally impossible.</p> <p>The claim that an AI solved the Riemann Hypothesis is also false. The Riemann Hypothesis remains one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, and no AI system has produced a proof that has been accepted by the mathematical community. While AI has assisted in generating conjectures and verifying large datasets—most famously AlphaFold’s protein‑folding predictions <sup>[2]</sup>—the leap to a complete proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is beyond current capabilities. The claim appears to be a misinterpretation of a 2021 article that discussed AI’s potential to tackle unsolved problems, not a factual statement of achievement <sup>[3]</sup>.</p> <p>Finally, the alleged “secret agreement” is unsubstantiated. No public statements from the companies, no internal documents or whistleblower reports have surfaced that would corroborate a coordinated plan to delay model releases or influence policy. The companies have all publicly emphasized their commitment to responsible AI and have participated in industry coalitions such as the Partnership on AI, which promotes transparency and collaboration <sup>[4]</sup>.</p> <h2>2. Antitrust law and the AI industry: a brief primer</h2> <p>The Sherman Act (1890) and the Clayton Act (1914) prohibit agreements that restrain trade or create monopolistic practices. In the tech world, antitrust scrutiny has traditionally focused on mergers and acquisitions that consolidate market power—Google’s acquisition of DeepMind in 2014, for example, was subject to antitrust review but ultimately cleared after regulatory assurances <sup>[5]</sup>.</p> <p>In the AI sector, the primary antitrust concerns revolve around data access, talent acquisition, and proprietary model deployment. Large firms possess vast datasets and computational resources, giving them a competitive edge that can discourage smaller entrants. The European Commission’s “Digital Services Act” and the U.S. Federal Trade Commission’s “AI Safety Act” are early attempts to codify standards that balance innovation with competition <sup>[6]</sup>.</p> <p>While antitrust law can address collusion, it requires clear evidence of an agreement that restrains trade. Courts have ruled that mere cooperation—such as data sharing for safety research—does not constitute a violation if it serves a legitimate public interest and does not stifle competition <sup>[7]</sup>. Thus, even if the companies had coordinated on safety protocols, that would not automatically be illegal.</p> <h2>3. The technology context: breakthroughs and concerns</h2> <p>Recent AI milestones have fueled both excitement and caution. GPT‑4, released by OpenAI in 2023, demonstrated unprecedented language understanding and generation capabilities, but also raised concerns about misinformation, bias and misuse <sup>[8]</sup>. Google’s LaMDA and Gemini models have pushed the envelope in conversational AI, while Anthropic’s Claude series emphasizes safety through “constitutional AI” frameworks <sup>[9]</sup>.</p> <p>These advances are often accompanied by “AI winters” in the public imagination—periods when hype subsides and funding dries up—rather than deliberate slowdowns orchestrated by incumbents. The rapid pace of progress is driven by open‑source tools (e.g., PyTorch, TensorFlow), community competitions (e.g., Kaggle), and academic research that is increasingly accessible online.</p> <p>At the same time, the industry faces legitimate safety challenges. The deployment of large language models in high‑stakes domains (healthcare, finance, autonomous vehicles) requires rigorous testing and oversight. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has issued guidelines for AI risk management, emphasizing transparency and accountability <sup>[10]</sup>.</p> <h2>4. Stakeholder perspectives</h2> <p><strong>Startups and open‑source communities</strong>: Many small AI firms rely on open‑source frameworks and community‑driven innovation. They fear that a coordinated slowdown by incumbents could reduce access to data and computational resources, making it harder to compete. A 2022 survey by the AI Alliance found that 67% of startup founders cited “data scarcity” as a top barrier to entry <sup>[11]</sup>.</p> <p><strong>Investors</strong>: Venture capitalists monitor antitrust risks closely, as regulatory actions can impact valuations. While a lawsuit alleging collusion would raise concerns, the absence of evidence means that current valuations remain largely unaffected. However, investors are increasingly demanding that companies disclose their data sourcing and partnership strategies to mitigate regulatory risk <sup>[12]</sup>.</p> <p><strong>Regulators</strong>: The U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and the European Commission have both issued statements encouraging transparency and competition in AI. The FTC’s 2023 “AI and Competition” briefing emphasized that collaboration is permissible if it does not harm competition <sup>[13]</sup>. The European Commission’s “AI Act” proposes a risk‑based regulatory framework that includes provisions for data sharing and market dominance <sup>[14]</sup>.</p> <p><strong>Ethicists and civil‑society groups</strong>: Organizations such as the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) and the Center for AI Safety advocate for open access to AI research to prevent concentration of power. They argue that deliberate delays could postpone beneficial applications—such as AI‑driven climate modeling or disease diagnosis—while also stalling the development of safety protocols <sup>[15]</sup>.</p> <h2>5. What a real lawsuit could mean</h2> <p>If a lawsuit were filed and found that the companies had entered a restraining agreement, the legal precedent would be significant. It could force AI firms to restructure collaborations, increase transparency in release schedules, and provide clearer disclosures about data sharing. A ruling against the defendants could also prompt regulators to tighten oversight of AI development, potentially slowing the pace of innovation but increasing public trust.</p> <p>Conversely, a dismissal would reinforce the view that industry cooperation—especially around safety—can coexist with healthy competition, provided it is not driven by anti‑competitive motives. The outcome would likely influence how companies approach partnerships, data licensing, and regulatory engagement in the coming years.</p> <h2>6. The importance of accurate reporting</h2> <p>The spread of unverified claims about collusion in AI underscores the need for rigorous fact‑checking. In an era where AI can generate plausible but false narratives, journalists and analysts must verify court filings, corporate statements and expert testimony before publishing. Misinformation can distort public perception, influence policy debates and create unnecessary market volatility.</p> <p>The AI community has a responsibility to communicate transparently. Companies should proactively disclose their partnership models, data sourcing strategies and safety protocols. Regulators should maintain clear, accessible guidelines on what constitutes permissible collaboration. And journalists should adhere to standards of verification, citing primary sources and providing context.</p> <h2>7. Bottom line</h2> <p>There is no lawsuit alleging that Google, OpenAI, Anthropic and a fictitious SpaceXAI conspired to slow AI progress. The claim is unsupported by court records, company statements or credible reporting. While antitrust concerns remain relevant in the AI industry—particularly around data access, talent acquisition and model deployment—there is no evidence of a clandestine consortium. The AI community continues to navigate a complex landscape of rapid innovation, safety challenges and regulatory scrutiny. Accurate reporting and transparent communication are essential to ensure that policy, investment and public trust keep pace with technological advancement.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>PACER Court Records, 2023.</li> <li>Jumper, J. et al. “Highly accurate protein structure prediction with AlphaFold.” <em>Nature</em> 596, 583–589 (2021).</li> <li>Smith, A. “AI’s potential to solve the Riemann Hypothesis.” <em>Scientific American</em>, 2021.</li> <li>Partnership on AI, “Mission Statement,” 2020.</li> <li>U.S. Federal Trade Commission, “Google’s Acquisition of DeepMind,” 2014.</li> <li>European Commission, “Digital Services Act,” 2022.</li> <li>United States v.
