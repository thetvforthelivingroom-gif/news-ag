Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-23
In the last 47 years, Greenland and Antarctica have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice—an amount that would lift global sea levels by nearly 85 millimetres. That satellite‑derived loss, revealed just before COP29, underscores a rapid acceleration in ice‑sheet melt, amplifying the urgency for stronger emissions cuts and revised risk assessments.

What happened

Satellite gravimetry has quantified a cumulative loss of over 12 trillion tons of ice from the two polar ice sheets between 1997 and 2024, a figure that translates to an estimated 85 mm rise in global sea level. The loss averages about 1.8 mm per year, with Greenland contributing roughly 70 % and Antarctica about 30 % of the total [1]. These numbers represent a dramatic increase from earlier estimates that suggested a slower, more gradual decline. The data show a clear uptick in mass loss after 2000, coinciding with heightened global temperatures and intensified atmospheric and oceanic warming.

Background and context

The breakthrough in measuring ice‑sheet mass change comes from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) satellites, launched in 1997, and its successor, GRACE‑Follow‑On, which continued observations until 2017. GRACE measures subtle changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by shifting mass, allowing scientists to infer ice‑sheet weight loss directly. CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, complements GRACE by providing high‑resolution elevation data that helps disentangle surface changes from subsurface processes. Together, these missions surpass ground‑based and airborne surveys in spatial coverage and temporal continuity, offering a global perspective on ice dynamics that was previously unattainable [1].

Before the advent of satellite gravimetry, estimates of ice‑sheet loss relied on radar altimetry, ground‑based GPS, and airborne lidar, which, while precise locally, could not capture the full extent of mass changes across vast, remote regions. Consequently, earlier studies often underestimated the rate of loss, particularly in the rapidly changing southern hemisphere. The satellite data have revealed that the rate of ice‑sheet melt has accelerated since the early 2000s, a trend that aligns with the observed rise in atmospheric and oceanic temperatures.

The bigger picture

The drivers behind the accelerated ice loss are multifaceted. Anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions have warmed the atmosphere, raising surface temperatures and reducing snowfall over Greenland’s interior. In Antarctica, ocean heat transport—particularly from the Atlantic and Pacific—has warmed ice‑sheet margins, accelerating basal melting. Changes in atmospheric circulation, such as a weakening polar vortex, have also reduced snowfall and increased the frequency of warm air intrusions, further amplifying melt rates. These processes are interlinked, creating a feedback loop that hastens ice loss and sea‑level rise [1].

Policy implications are immediate. The 12 trillion‑ton figure arrives at a critical juncture, as nations prepare for COP29 and negotiate new emissions targets. The data suggest that current mitigation pathways may be insufficient to keep sea‑level rise within the 1.5 °C threshold, raising questions about the adequacy of existing pledges. Moreover, insurance and infrastructure risk models that historically under‑estimated ice‑sheet mass loss may need to be revised to account for the higher sea‑level rise projections. The gap between scientific evidence and policy action highlights a tension between precautionary measures and economic interests, especially in regions where coastal development is expanding rapidly [1].

What to watch

COP29 will be a pivotal moment for global climate governance. The satellite evidence of rapid ice loss provides a stark metric that could influence negotiations, potentially prompting higher emissions cuts and more ambitious adaptation plans. Observers will be watching whether the scientific community can translate these findings into concrete policy instruments that reflect the urgency of the situation.

In the coming years, new gravimetry missions—such as the proposed successors to GRACE‑Follow‑On—are slated for launch. These missions aim to improve spatial resolution and extend the time series, offering finer detail on regional mass changes and enabling better attribution of melt drivers. The scientific community will also seek independent corroboration from other satellite platforms and in‑situ measurements to reinforce confidence in the 12 trillion‑ton estimate.

Risk models used by insurers and planners will need to integrate the revised sea‑level rise figures. Updated models will likely shift the perceived vulnerability of low‑lying coastal areas, potentially altering development patterns and prompting investment in protective infrastructure. The pace at which these models adapt will be a key indicator of how effectively the private sector is responding to the new scientific insights.

In conclusion, the satellite‑derived loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice over the past 47 years is a clear signal of accelerating climate change. It underscores the need for immediate, aggressive emissions reductions and a reassessment of risk management strategies worldwide. The coming COP29 and future satellite missions will determine whether the scientific community can translate these findings into decisive global action.
