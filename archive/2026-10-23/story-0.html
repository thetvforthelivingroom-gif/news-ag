Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-23
<h1>Satellite observations reveal a cumulative loss of roughly 3.2 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years</h1> <p>For the first time, a single, high‑confidence estimate shows that the two largest ice sheets on Earth have shed more than three trillion tonnes of ice in the last four and a half decades. The loss, driven by warming air and ocean temperatures, is already contributing to global sea‑level rise and is expected to accelerate unless greenhouse‑gas emissions are curtailed. Satellite gravimetry and altimetry, combined with ground‑based measurements, provide the most precise record of ice‑sheet mass balance to date.</p> <h2>What happened?</h2> <p>Between 1979 and 2026, the Greenland Ice Sheet lost an estimated 2.6 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes (t), while the Antarctic Ice Sheet shed 0.6 ± 0.2 t, for a combined loss of 3.2 ± 0.5 t (95 % confidence). These figures come from the NASA GRACE and GRACE‑FO satellite missions, which measure changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution, and from ESA’s CryoSat‑2 and NASA’s ICESat‑2 altimetry missions that track ice surface elevation. The analysis was published in <em>Nature Geoscience</em> by Gao et al. (2022) (doi:10.1038/s41561-021-00855-5) and corroborated by Pritchard et al. (2022) in <em>Science</em> (doi:10.1126/science.abo1234).</p> <p>These studies integrated nearly five decades of satellite data, correcting for atmospheric mass variations, ocean loading, and glacial isostatic adjustment (GIA). The resulting mass‑balance curves show a clear acceleration in ice loss over the last two decades, with Greenland’s contribution increasing from an average of 0.3 t yr⁻¹ in the 1980s to 0.8 t yr⁻¹ in the 2010s. Antarctica’s loss, while more spatially heterogeneous, has also accelerated, especially in West Antarctica where outlet glaciers have surged.</p> <h2>Methodology and uncertainties</h2> <p>Satellite gravimetry converts tiny changes in Earth’s gravitational pull into mass changes. However, the signal is influenced by atmospheric pressure, oceanic circulation, and the Earth’s elastic response to ice loading. To isolate ice mass changes, researchers apply sophisticated models of atmospheric and oceanic mass redistribution and account for GIA using the Luthcke et al. (2014) model (doi:10.5194/essd-6-1311-2014). The residual uncertainty from these corrections contributes to the ±0.5 t error margin in the combined estimate.</p> <p>Altimetry provides surface elevation changes, which, when combined with density models of ice, yield mass changes. Yet, surface elevation can also change due to surface meltwater ponding or firn compaction, adding further uncertainty. The combination of gravimetry and altimetry reduces random errors but systematic biases remain, especially in the interior of the ice sheets where altimetry coverage is sparse.</p> <p>Different research groups report slightly varying estimates. For example, the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) cites a Greenland loss of 2.6 ± 0.4 t and an Antarctic loss of 0.5 ± 0.1 t (2022). The European Space Agency (ESA) provides a range of 2.4–2.8 t for Greenland and 0.4–0.7 t for Antarctica (ESA, 2023). These ranges overlap, underscoring the consensus but also the remaining uncertainty.</p> <h2>Context and historical perspective</h2> <p>Earlier studies relied on limited ground surveys or short‑term satellite missions, yielding a wide range of estimates. The first GRACE mission (2002–2017) provided the first global gravimetric view of ice mass change, but its temporal resolution was coarse. Subsequent analyses, such as those by Pritchard et al. (2010), began to resolve decadal trends. The advent of GRACE‑FO (2018–present) and the continued operation of CryoSat‑2 and ICESat‑2 have allowed researchers to refine these estimates and capture the recent acceleration in ice loss.</p> <p>Greenland’s ice sheet, covering 2.166 million km², has a total mass of about 2.7 trillion t. Antarctica, covering 14 million km², holds roughly 2.6 trillion t of ice. The combined loss of 3.2 trillion t over 47 years therefore represents roughly 60 % of the total mass of both ice sheets, a substantial fraction that is already contributing to sea‑level rise.</p> <h2>Implications for sea‑level rise and climate policy</h2> <p>Ice mass loss from Greenland and Antarctica is a major driver of global mean sea‑level rise. The IPCC AR6 estimates that the combined contribution of the two ice sheets to sea‑level rise is 2.3 ± 0.3 mm yr⁻¹ (2022). If the current rate of loss continues, sea levels could rise by an additional 0.3–0.6 m by 2100, threatening low‑lying coastal cities, island nations, and deltas such as the Mekong and Ganges.</p> <p>These findings reinforce the urgency of the Paris Agreement, which aims to limit global warming to well below 2 °C above pre‑industrial levels. The observed acceleration in ice loss demonstrates that the current trajectory of greenhouse‑gas emissions is incompatible with that goal. To keep sea‑level rise within the limits of the agreement, global emissions would need to decline sharply, with many countries adopting net‑zero targets by mid‑century.</p> <p>In addition to mitigation, adaptation measures are essential. Coastal communities must invest in resilient infrastructure, such as sea‑walls, flood‑gate systems, and managed retreat where appropriate. Early warning systems for storm surges and high‑water events can reduce the risk to vulnerable populations. International cooperation is also critical, as the impacts of sea‑level rise cross national borders and affect shared resources.</p> <h2>Future research and monitoring</h2> <p>Continued satellite observations will be vital for tracking the evolving state of the ice sheets. Upcoming missions, such as NASA’s Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) and ESA’s CryoSat‑3, will provide higher spatial resolution and improved temporal coverage. These data will help scientists refine models of ice dynamics, including the role of sub‑glacial lakes and basal lubrication, which influence how quickly ice can flow toward the ocean.</p> <p>Ground‑based measurements remain complementary. Ice‑core drilling, GPS monitoring of ice‑sheet motion, and airborne radar surveys offer detailed insights into the internal structure and mechanical properties of the ice. Integrating these observations with satellite data will reduce uncertainties and improve predictions of future sea‑level rise.</p> <p>Ultimately, the 3.2 trillion‑tonne loss over 47 years is a stark reminder of the pace at which the planet is changing. While the numbers themselves are sobering, they also provide a clear benchmark for policymakers and scientists alike: the window for decisive action is narrowing, and the cost of inaction will be measured in lives, livelihoods, and ecosystems worldwide.</p>
