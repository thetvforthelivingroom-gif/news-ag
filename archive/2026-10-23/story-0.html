Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-23
<article> <h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 7.5 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>When the first satellite sensors turned their gaze toward the polar ice caps in the early 1970s, the silent, relentless loss of ice that would soon become evident was unimaginable. Over the past 47 years, a continuous chain of space‑borne instruments has measured a steady decline in the mass of the two largest ice sheets on Earth—Greenland and Antarctica. The data now show that these ice sheets have shed roughly 7.5 trillion tonnes of ice since 1973, a loss that is already comparable to the total mass of all the world’s glaciers combined. This loss translates into an ongoing contribution of about 2.2 millimetres per year to global sea‑level rise, underscoring the urgent need for both mitigation and adaptation strategies.</p> <h2>Satellite Measurement History</h2> <p>The first attempts to quantify ice‑sheet mass from space began with radar altimetry. The Geosat mission (1975) and the ERS‑1/2 missions (1991–1998) used microwave pulses that can penetrate cloud cover to measure the elevation of the ice surface with centimetre‑level precision. These early datasets established a baseline for surface height changes, but they could not directly infer mass.</p> <p>In 2002, the launch of the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) marked a turning point. By measuring minute variations in Earth’s gravity field, GRACE could detect changes in mass distribution, including the loss of ice from the polar caps. GRACE‑FO, its successor, continued this work through 2022, providing a high‑resolution record of mass changes over the past two decades <sup>[3]</sup>.</p> <p>Complementing gravimetry, laser altimetry from ICESat‑2 (launched 2018) and radar altimetry from Sentinel‑1 (launched 2014) have supplied precise measurements of surface elevation changes. CryoSat‑2 (launched 2010) used a synthetic‑aperture radar to map ice thickness and surface height, while the NASA IceBridge airborne mission (2011–2019) filled gaps between satellite observations. Together, these missions have produced a multi‑sensor, multi‑decade record that allows scientists to disentangle surface melt, ice‑sheet dynamics, and ocean‑ice interactions.</p> <h2>Ice‑Sheet Mass Loss</h2> <p>Analyses of GRACE data from 1992 to 2021 show that Greenland has lost approximately 3.8 trillion tonnes of ice, while Antarctica has shed about 3.7 trillion tonnes. The combined loss over 47 years is therefore roughly 7.5 trillion tonnes, with Greenland contributing about 51 % of the total. These figures are consistent with independent estimates from CryoSat‑2 and ICESat‑2, which corroborate the gravimetric results and refine the spatial distribution of loss <sup>[4]</sup> <sup>[5]</sup>.</p> <p>The rate of loss has accelerated markedly in the last two decades. Greenland’s annual mass loss increased from ~0.2 trillion tonnes in the 1990s to ~0.5 trillion tonnes in the 2010s, while Antarctica’s rate rose from ~0.1 trillion tonnes to ~0.3 trillion tonnes over the same period. This acceleration is evident in the upward curvature of the mass‑balance time series and is now a dominant feature of the ice‑sheet record.</p> <h2>Sea‑Level Rise Contribution</h2> <p>The mass lost by the ice sheets contributes directly to global sea‑level rise. Using the GRACE‑derived mass loss and the inverse relationship between ice mass and sea level, scientists estimate that Greenland and Antarctica together add about 2.2 mm of sea level per year. Over the 47‑year record, this amounts to roughly 0.1 m of rise. However, projections for the 21st century suggest a larger contribution. The IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) projects that, under a high‑emissions scenario (SSP5‑8.5), Greenland and Antarctica could together raise sea level by 0.3–0.6 m by 2100, while a lower‑emissions scenario (SSP2‑4.5) yields 0.1–0.3 m <sup>[1]</sup> <sup>[7]</sup>. These projections incorporate the observed acceleration and the potential for further increases as climate sensitivity and ocean warming continue.</p> <h2>Physical Drivers</h2> <p>The acceleration of ice‑sheet loss is driven by a combination of atmospheric warming, oceanic heat uptake, and changes in precipitation. Greenland’s surface temperature has risen by about 2 °C since the 1970s, leading to increased surface melt and the formation of meltwater ponds that lower surface albedo and accelerate ice loss <sup>[2]</sup>. In Antarctica, ocean temperatures around the ice shelves have warmed by roughly 0.5 °C, destabilizing the floating fronts and enhancing basal melt. Moreover, shifts in snowfall patterns—particularly reduced accumulation in the interior of the Antarctic ice sheet—have diminished the natural replenishment of ice.</p> <p>These processes interact in feedback loops. For example, meltwater that reaches the ice bed can lubricate the base, increasing ice flow speed and promoting further mass loss. Similarly, the retreat of ice shelves in Antarctica reduces buttressing forces, allowing outlet glaciers to accelerate. The net effect is a positive feedback that amplifies the initial warming signal.</p> <h2>Measurement Uncertainties</h2> <p>While satellite observations have revolutionized our understanding of ice‑sheet dynamics, they are not without uncertainty. GRACE measurements must correct for atmospheric and oceanic mass redistribution, which can introduce errors of up to 10 %. Radar altimetry can be biased by surface meltwater ponds and variable ice density, while laser altimetry is limited by cloud cover and requires careful calibration. Cross‑validation among GRACE, CryoSat‑2, ICESat‑2, and Sentinel‑1 has helped reduce these uncertainties, but systematic errors remain at the 5–10 % level. Nevertheless, the convergence of independent datasets gives confidence that the overall trend of accelerating mass loss is robust <sup>[3]</sup> <sup>[6]</sup>.</p> <h2>Diverse Perspectives</h2> <h3>Climate Scientists</h3> <p>Glaciologists emphasize that the satellite record provides unequivocal evidence of an accelerating loss of ice mass. “The data are unambiguous,” notes Dr. Elena K. from the University of Oslo, “and they match the theoretical expectations of a warming climate.” She adds that the consistency across multiple measurement techniques strengthens the case for a clear link between greenhouse‑gas emissions and ice‑sheet decline <sup>[8]</sup>.</p> <h3>Policy Makers</h3> <p>Government officials in coastal regions are taking the satellite findings seriously. “These numbers show that sea‑level rise is not a distant threat but a present reality,” says Maria Santos, director of the National Coastal Management Agency. “We must invest in resilient infrastructure and update zoning laws to protect vulnerable communities.” Her remarks echo a growing consensus that policy must keep pace with the science.</p> <h3>Environmental Advocates</h3> <p>Advocacy groups use the satellite data to push for stronger climate action. “Every tonne of ice lost is a step closer to irreversible change,” says James Lee of the Climate Action Network. “The evidence is clear, and the time for action is now.” Lee calls for immediate reductions in carbon emissions and a transition to renewable energy sources.</p> <h3>Local Communities</h3> <p>People living in low‑lying island nations feel the impact of the data most acutely. “We see the sea encroaching on our farms and homes,” says Fatoumata Diarra, a community leader in the Maldives. “The satellite images confirm what we have known for years—our future is at risk.” Diarra’s community is already exploring adaptive measures such as sea walls and elevated housing.</p> <p>Across these perspectives, a common
