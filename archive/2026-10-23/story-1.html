Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-23
In March 2023, a coalition of civil‑society groups filed a federal antitrust complaint in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts. The lawsuit alleges that three of the most influential artificial‑intelligence (AI) firms—Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google—conspired to slow the pace of AI development. The complaint, brought by the Center for AI Safety and the AI Now Institute, claims that the defendants coordinated research timelines, shared proprietary data, and established a joint framework that, according to the plaintiffs, effectively throttled progress in the field. The plaintiffs argue that such coordination violates the Sherman Act, a federal law that prohibits agreements that restrain trade, and the Clayton Act, which addresses practices that could lead to anticompetitive outcomes. However, the filing contains no independent evidence—such as documents or whistle‑blower testimony—to substantiate the claim that a formal agreement existed. The defendants have denied any collusion, stating that their collaborations are purely voluntary and aimed at promoting safety and ethical standards. The case remains in its early stages, with the court yet to rule on the merits of the allegations or the admissibility of the evidence presented<sup>1</sup>. The lawsuit was filed by the Center for AI Safety and the AI Now Institute—organizations that have long advocated for transparent and accountable AI development. The complaint, titled “AI Slowdown Litigation,” claims that the defendants entered into a “secret agreement” to delay the release of certain AI capabilities, citing a series of emails and internal documents that the plaintiffs say were obtained through a whistle‑blower. The plaintiffs contend that the defendants’ coordination was designed to protect market dominance while sidestepping regulatory scrutiny. They argue that the alleged agreement constitutes a horizontal restraint that is per se illegal under the Sherman Act, and that it also violates the Clayton Act by preventing the entry of new competitors into the AI market<sup>2</sup>. The allegations raise a fundamental question: can collaborations that aim to promote safety and ethics be distinguished from anticompetitive conduct? The plaintiffs’ complaint frames the alleged coordination as a “joint framework” that deliberately slowed innovation. They point to a memorandum of understanding circulated among the defendants in 2021 that outlined shared research timelines and data‑sharing protocols. The plaintiffs argue that the memorandum, while framed as a safety initiative, effectively established a de facto cartel that limited the speed at which new AI models could be released. The defendants, however, have maintained that the agreements were purely voluntary and aimed at addressing shared safety challenges—such as mitigating bias, ensuring robustness, and preventing misuse. The legal landscape for antitrust enforcement in the technology sector is complex. The Sherman Act, enacted in 1890, prohibits contracts, combinations, or conspiracies that restrain trade. The Clayton Act, passed in 1914, complements the Sherman Act by addressing specific practices that could lead to anticompetitive outcomes, such as exclusive dealing and mergers that substantially lessen competition. In the context of AI, courts have had to grapple with how traditional antitrust principles apply to rapidly evolving technology markets. The landmark Microsoft case of 2000, which resulted in a consent decree that imposed restrictions on the company’s bundling practices, set a precedent for scrutinizing dominant firms’ market‑controlling behavior. More recently, the 2013 Google search and advertising cases underscored the willingness of regulators to intervene when a firm’s market dominance is perceived to stifle competition<sup>3</sup>. The AI slowdown lawsuit also taps into a broader debate about the role of industry‑driven safety initiatives. In 2019, the European Union adopted the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which imposed strict data‑handling requirements on companies that process personal data. The GDPR’s emphasis on transparency and accountability has influenced how AI firms approach data sharing. Similarly, the U.S. Congress has introduced several bills aimed at clarifying the scope of antitrust enforcement in the context of AI, including the “AI Accountability Act” (H.R. 1234) and the “AI Safety Act” (S. 5678). While these bills are still in the legislative process, they signal a growing recognition that AI development may require a new regulatory framework that balances innovation incentives with market fairness<sup>4</sup>. The lawsuit’s potential implications extend beyond the immediate parties. If the court determines that the alleged agreement constitutes a violation of antitrust law, it could set a precedent that would affect future collaborations across the AI sector. Regulatory bodies may respond by tightening guidelines for industry partnerships, potentially requiring disclosure of joint research agreements or imposing limits on data sharing. In the European Union, the European Commission’s “AI Act,” which was adopted in 2021, already imposes strict obligations on high‑risk AI systems, including requirements for transparency, human oversight, and risk assessment. The EU’s approach could influence U.S. lawmakers to adopt similar measures, thereby creating a more harmonized regulatory environment<sup>5</sup>. The lawsuit also has implications for funding and research partnerships. Venture capital firms may become more cautious about investing in firms that engage in large‑scale collaborations, fearing potential legal liabilities. Academic researchers may need to navigate a more complex web of institutional agreements and data‑sharing protocols, potentially slowing the pace of scientific discovery. The plaintiffs’ allegations could also prompt a reevaluation of the role of open‑source initiatives in AI development, as companies may seek to protect proprietary data and research timelines more aggressively. Stakeholder perspectives on the lawsuit are diverse. Investors are divided: some view the lawsuit as a risk that could slow the pace of technological breakthroughs and erode competitive advantage, while others see it as a necessary check on unchecked power that could ultimately benefit long‑term market stability. Civil‑society advocates and policy analysts are calling for greater transparency and independent oversight of AI collaborations. They argue that without clear regulatory frameworks, industry‑driven initiatives risk becoming vehicles for collusion. Regulatory bodies such as the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice have yet to issue formal statements on the case, but their silence has prompted speculation that they may be monitoring the proceedings closely. In the European Union, competition authorities have already begun to scrutinize AI firms for potential anticompetitive behavior, suggesting that the lawsuit could have cross‑border implications<sup>6</sup>. The public’s demand for transparency is also growing. High‑profile incidents involving AI bias and safety failures have eroded trust, leading to calls for more stringent oversight and accountability. The lawsuit, by spotlighting potential collusion, may reinforce the perception that AI firms are not fully transparent about their internal coordination, thereby fueling calls for regulatory reforms. Media coverage has already highlighted the case, with outlets such as Bloomberg Law, Reuters, and the Associated Press reporting on the lawsuit’s claims and the defendants’ responses. The narrative may evolve as new evidence emerges, and stakeholders—including researchers, investors, regulators, and civil‑society advocates—will need to stay informed about the lawsuit’s progress to understand how it will reshape the AI ecosystem<sup>7</sup>. In sum, the lawsuit against Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google is more than a legal dispute; it is a test case that could redefine the balance between collaborative innovation and antitrust enforcement in the rapidly evolving field of artificial intelligence. Its outcome will likely reverberate across industry practices, regulatory frameworks, and public trust in AI technologies. As the court navigates the complex interplay of antitrust law and emerging technology, the AI community will be watching closely, aware that the decisions made today could shape the trajectory of AI development for years to come.
