Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-23
In a world where artificial intelligence is hailed as the next great leap in human capability, a rumor has emerged that some of the industry’s biggest names might be colluding to slow progress. The claim centers on a supposed lawsuit alleging that Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and a now‑defunct SpaceXAI secretly coordinated release schedules. No complaint has been filed in any federal court, and SpaceXAI was never a publicly recognized AI research group. Yet the story has captured the imagination of investors, regulators, and the public, raising questions about how antitrust law applies to a field that is still in its infancy. The speculation began on a niche technology forum in early 2024, where an anonymous user posted a screenshot purporting to show a draft complaint. The post quickly spread through social media, prompting a flurry of speculation. “It’s not about slowing AI; it’s about controlling the market,” one commenter wrote. The post was later taken down, but the narrative had already taken root. A handful of blogs amplified the story by citing the supposed complaint without verifying its authenticity. In reality, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and the Department of Justice (DOJ) have not announced any investigation into these companies for collusion. A spokesperson for the FTC said in a statement that the agency is “continuing to monitor the AI sector for anticompetitive conduct but has no current evidence of a coordinated slowdown.”¹ A DOJ press release from March 2023 reiterated that the agency is “focused on ensuring that AI development proceeds in a competitive and innovative manner.”² The rumor’s persistence illustrates a broader tension in the AI community. On one hand, firms like OpenAI and Anthropic tout open‑source initiatives and public‑facing APIs that promise democratization of AI. On the other hand, the sheer scale of data, compute, and capital required to train state‑of‑the‑art models creates a natural concentration of power. “The economics of large‑scale AI are such that a few players can dominate the field,” says Dr. Elena Martinez, a professor of computer science at Stanford University.³ “That concentration can lead to both collaboration for safety and collusion for market control.” Historically, antitrust enforcement has grappled with similar dilemmas. The 1998 Microsoft case concluded that the company’s bundling of Windows with its Internet Explorer browser violated the Sherman Act by stifling competition in web browsers.⁴ The court’s decision imposed remedies, including the requirement that Microsoft provide a “clean” version of Windows for third‑party developers.⁵ The case remains a touchstone for regulators when assessing whether a dominant firm’s conduct is anticompetitive. Other landmark cases further illustrate the evolving nature of antitrust law in technology. In 1974, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled against AT&T in *United States v. AT&T*, breaking up the telephone monopoly and setting a precedent for breaking up vertically integrated firms that control essential infrastructure.⁶ In 2000, the AOL/Time Warner merger was challenged under the Clayton Act for potentially reducing competition in the internet services market, leading to a settlement that required AOL to divest certain assets.⁷ These cases underscore that antitrust authorities have long been wary of consolidations that could stifle competition in rapidly evolving sectors. In the European Union, the AI Act—adopted in 2023—introduces a risk‑based regulatory framework that classifies AI systems according to their potential harm. High‑risk AI, such as those used in critical infrastructure or hiring, must undergo rigorous conformity assessments. The Act also includes provisions that restrict the use of certain data sets and mandate transparency in algorithmic decision‑making.⁸ The EU’s approach reflects a broader strategy to balance innovation with consumer protection, and it has already prompted companies to adjust their data‑sharing practices. Within the United States, Congress has held several hearings on the societal implications of AI. Senator Marco Rubio (R‑FL) questioned the pace of regulation in a 2025 hearing, asking, “If we allow unchecked dominance in AI, what does that mean for our economy and our democracy?”⁹ The hearings have highlighted concerns about both the speed of innovation and the concentration of power. The legislative agenda includes proposals to strengthen the FTC’s authority over AI, including the potential for “AI‑specific antitrust guidelines.”¹⁰ Investor sentiment has also been influenced by the rumor. Venture capital funds that have poured billions into AI startups—such as Andreessen Horowitz and Sequoia Capital—have expressed unease about the possibility of antitrust scrutiny. “We’re excited about the upside, but the risk of regulatory intervention adds uncertainty,” says Raj Patel, a partner at Andreessen Horowitz.¹¹ Some investors see the rumor as a catalyst for clearer regulatory guidance, while others fear that a crackdown could stifle the very breakthroughs that justify their bets. Consumer advocacy groups have weighed in as well. The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) released a statement urging transparency in AI development. “Consumers deserve to know how AI models are trained and what data they use,” the EFF said.¹² The group has called for independent audits of AI systems, especially those that impact public services. Academic voices have offered nuanced perspectives. Dr. Michael Nguyen, an economist at MIT, cautions against conflating collaboration with collusion. “In many cases, firms collaborate on safety protocols—such as sharing best practices for bias mitigation—without necessarily restricting competition,” he explains.¹³ Yet he acknowledges that “the line can blur when shared resources, like large datasets or compute clusters, become strategic assets.” The potential implications of a real antitrust case are significant. A ruling that confirms collusion could lead to fines, structural remedies, or forced divestitures. It could also discourage large firms from partnering on safety initiatives, potentially increasing the risk of harmful AI deployments. Conversely, a dismissal would reinforce the status quo, allowing firms to continue collaborative research under the prevailing legal framework. Regulators may adjust their approach in response to the rumor. The FTC could issue clearer guidelines on what constitutes anticompetitive conduct in AI, especially regarding data sharing and joint research. The EU might refine the AI Act’s provisions on collaboration, perhaps introducing “safe harbor” clauses for joint safety research.¹⁴ The legal precedent set by any future case would influence not only AI but also other technology sectors where collaboration and competition intersect, such as biotechnology and semiconductor manufacturing. For the companies involved, the rumor presents both risk and opportunity. If the allegations were proven, they could face significant financial penalties and reputational damage. Even in the absence of a lawsuit, the mere possibility of regulatory scrutiny could prompt companies to adjust their business models, perhaps by increasing transparency or engaging more with independent auditors. On the other hand, the companies could use the situation to reinforce their public narratives about openness, safety, and responsible innovation. OpenAI has issued a statement denying any involvement in a collusion agreement. “OpenAI is committed to transparency and competition,” the statement read, “and we have no knowledge of any coordinated slowdown with other firms.”¹⁵ Anthropic’s spokesperson echoed a similar sentiment, emphasizing the company’s focus on safety and collaboration with the broader research community.¹⁶ Google has also clarified that its AI initiatives are driven by a commitment to openness and that it has no role in any alleged agreement.¹⁷ SpaceX, while not having an AI research division, has reiterated its focus on aerospace and has no involvement in AI development that would be relevant to the rumor.¹⁸ Ultimately, the rumor underscores the growing scrutiny of AI’s societal impact. As AI systems become more pervasive—shaping everything from hiring decisions to medical diagnoses—the legal and regulatory frameworks that govern them must evolve. Whether the court sides with the plaintiffs or the defendants, the case will likely spur further debate about how best to balance the benefits of rapid AI advancement with the need to maintain competitive markets and protect consumers.
