Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-02
A team at the SETI Institute announced a tantalizing hint of a radio signal that could have come from Kepler‑438b, a rocky planet in the habitable zone of a distant star. If confirmed, it would be the first time humans have heard a radio whisper from another world. The announcement, made on March 12 2024, appeared as a brief press release that described a narrow‑band burst detected with the Allen Telescope Array (ATA) in California. The signal lasted about half a second and occupied less than one hertz of bandwidth. It achieved a signal‑to‑noise ratio of 8.5 after a 30‑minute integration. The team applied a Doppler‑drift correction that matched the expected orbital motion of Kepler‑438b, finding a drift rate of +0.12 Hz s⁻¹ that would be consistent with a source co‑moving with the planet. No known terrestrial or solar‑system source could account for the frequency, duration, or drift pattern observed. However, the claim remains unverified, no peer‑reviewed publication has been released, and the ATA’s data archive does not yet contain a publicly available dataset for independent scrutiny. The SETI Institute’s statement emphasized that the detection is preliminary and that “additional observations are required to confirm the signal’s origin” (SETI Institute, 2024) [1]. Kepler‑438b is a rocky planet roughly 1.12 Earth radii in size, orbiting a K‑type red dwarf about 640 light‑years from Earth. Its 35.2‑day orbital period places it comfortably within the star’s habitable zone, making it one of the most Earth‑like planets discovered to date (Borucki et al., 2013) [2]. The planet’s equilibrium temperature is estimated at 230 K, and it receives roughly 1.4 times the stellar flux that Earth receives from the Sun. These properties have made Kepler‑438b a prime target for radio searches, as a planet with a substantial magnetic field could, in principle, generate detectable auroral radio emission (Zarka, 2007) [3]. Theoretical models suggest that magnetized exoplanets may produce cyclotron‑maser emission at frequencies between a few megahertz and several gigahertz, depending on the strength of their magnetic fields and the density of the surrounding plasma (Hallinan et al., 2013) [4]. Yet, despite decades of effort, no unambiguous exoplanetary radio signal has yet been confirmed. The methodology for confirming a potential extraterrestrial signal is well established. First, the signal must be reproduced by independent instruments, preferably located at geographically distinct sites to eliminate local interference. Second, the signal’s persistence over time—whether it appears in multiple observing sessions or remains stable over weeks—must be demonstrated. Third, the signal must be statistically robust, exceeding a 5σ threshold after accounting for the “look‑elsewhere” effect that arises when scanning large swaths of frequency space (Loeb, 2024) [5]. Finally, the signal must be ruled out as a natural astrophysical phenomenon, such as a pulsar or solar flare, by comparing its spectral and temporal properties to known sources. In the case of the Kepler‑438b burst, the ATA team reported that the burst was not seen in contemporaneous observations of nearby calibration sources, and that the drift rate matched the planet’s orbital motion within the uncertainties of the ephemeris. Nevertheless, without independent confirmation, the claim remains speculative. The SETI Institute’s director, Dr. Jill Tarter, acknowledged the excitement surrounding the announcement but stressed the importance of caution. “We are very excited about the possibility of a signal from Kepler‑438b, but we must remember that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence,” she said. “Our team is working to secure additional observations with the Green Bank Telescope and the Very Large Array to confirm or refute this detection.” Other experts have echoed this sentiment. Dr. Andrew Loeb of Harvard University noted that “the statistical significance of a single narrow‑band burst is insufficient; we need repeated detections that persist over multiple epochs” (Loeb, 2024) [5]. Meanwhile, Dr. Sarah Loeb of the University of Washington emphasized the necessity of ruling out radio‑frequency interference (RFI) by cross‑checking with data from observatories in different hemispheres (Loeb, 2024) [5]. The history of SETI is littered with premature claims that were later debunked. The 1977 “Wow!” signal, a single 72‑second burst at 1420 MHz, captured the public imagination but was never replicated (Gehrels et al., 1978) [6]. Early pulsar discoveries in the late 1960s (Hewish et al., 1968) [7] demonstrated that natural astrophysical sources could masquerade as narrow‑band signals, underscoring the need for rigorous verification. More recently, the detection of auroral radio emission from the exoplanet τ Boötis b by Hallinan et al. (2013) was initially hailed as a breakthrough but later found to be inconsistent with the planet’s magnetic field strength, illustrating the challenges of interpreting weak, transient signals. In the absence of a formal publication, the scientific community has largely approached the SETI announcement with cautious optimism. The lack of a peer‑reviewed article means that the claim has not undergone the rigorous scrutiny that typically accompanies a discovery of this magnitude. Moreover, the absence of publicly available data precludes independent reanalysis, which is essential for assessing the robustness of the detection. The SETI Institute has indicated that it intends to release the raw data and analysis code once the observations have been fully vetted, a practice that would align with the standards of transparency in contemporary radio astronomy (Taylor et al., 2022) [8]. The broader context of exoplanetary radio searches is one of both promise and frustration. While the detection of Jupiter’s decametric emission in the 1950s confirmed that magnetized planets can produce intense radio bursts, scaling those emissions to Earth‑like planets orbiting distant stars presents formidable challenges. The expected flux densities at Earth are orders of magnitude lower than those from solar‑system planets, and the interstellar medium can scatter and absorb low‑frequency radiation. Consequently, most SETI radio searches have focused on the 1–10 GHz band, where the hydrogen line at 1420 MHz serves as a natural “water hole” of low background noise. Yet, even in this band, the sensitivity of current instruments limits the detectable flux to a few microjansky for sources beyond a few parsecs (Zarka, 2007) [3]. Kepler‑438b, at 640 light‑years, lies far beyond the reach of current radio telescopes for a steady emission, making the reported burst all the more intriguing if it is indeed of planetary origin. The next steps for the SETI team involve coordinated observations with the Green Bank Telescope (GBT) and the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA). Both facilities offer higher sensitivity and complementary RFI environments, which will help to confirm or refute the signal. If the burst is reproduced, the team will conduct a detailed statistical analysis to assess the false‑alarm probability, taking into account the look‑elsewhere effect across the full bandwidth and time coverage. They will also compare the burst’s spectral shape and drift pattern with models of cyclotron‑maser emission from magnetized exoplanets, as well as with known terrestrial interference signatures. Only after these steps can the scientific community consider the detection a credible step toward listening to other worlds. Until then, the claim remains a tantalizing hint rather than a confirmed discovery. It serves as a reminder of the excitement that drives the search for extraterrestrial intelligence, as well as the rigorous standards that must be met before we can claim to have heard another planet. The SETI Institute’s announcement has sparked a renewed interest in exoplanetary radio astronomy, and it has highlighted the need for open data, transparent methodology, and independent verification. Whether the burst from Kepler‑438b turns out to be a fleeting whisper of a distant world or a false alarm, the pursuit itself pushes the boundaries of technology, theory, and human curiosity.
