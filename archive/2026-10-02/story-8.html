DR Congo rolls out Ebola vaccine for health workers as death toll rises

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-02
In the midst of the Democratic Republic of Congo’s worst Ebola outbreak in a decade, the government has begun vaccinating frontline health workers with the rVSV‑ZEBOV vaccine. This shift from reactive treatment to proactive protection comes as the death toll climbs and the epidemic spreads from remote forested areas into densely populated urban centres.

What happened

The DRC has entered a new phase of its ongoing Ebola epidemic. Health authorities announced that medical staff in North Kivu and Ituri provinces are receiving the rVSV‑ZEBOV vaccine, marking a strategic move from treating cases to preventing them. The decision follows a sharp rise in confirmed cases and deaths, with the national tally now exceeding a thousand fatalities—far higher than the 2018‑2020 outbreak. The outbreak’s spread into urban centres complicates containment and strains an already fragile health system [1].

Vaccination is being rolled out in phases, beginning with the most exposed workers. The Ministry of Health reports that the first batch of 50,000 doses—procured through a partnership with the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Global Vaccine Alliance—has arrived in Kinshasa. The plan is to vaccinate over 200,000 health personnel across the affected provinces within the next six weeks, underscoring the urgency of the situation [2].

Background and context

Ebola’s re‑emergence in the DRC is not the first severe outbreak the country has faced. The 2018‑2020 epidemic, which began in neighboring Uganda, claimed 1,400 lives and infected more than 2,300 people. Earlier efforts relied on contact tracing, isolation, and experimental therapeutics such as ZMapp and monoclonal antibodies. In contrast, the current outbreak benefits from a licensed vaccine, rVSV‑ZEBOV (also known as Ervebo), approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2019 after demonstrating 90 % protective efficacy in a large field trial in Guinea [3].

The vaccine’s mechanism—a weakened recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus that expresses the Ebola glycoprotein—allows rapid deployment and a single‑dose regimen. However, it must be stored between 2 °C and 8 °C, requiring a reliable cold‑chain infrastructure that is often lacking in rural Congolese settings. To address this, WHO has partnered with local NGOs to establish mobile refrigeration units and training programs for health workers in vaccine handling [4].

Supply constraints remain critical. WHO estimates that the global stockpile of rVSV‑ZEBOV is limited to approximately 400,000 doses, insufficient for the DRC’s projected needs. Consequently, the rollout prioritises health workers, who face the highest exposure risk. Remaining doses are earmarked for community outreach and potential future outbreaks in neighbouring provinces [5].

Diverse perspectives

Front‑line health workers report a mix of relief and caution. A nurse in Goma, who wished to remain anonymous, said, “We have been on the front lines for months, and the vaccine gives us a sense of protection that we didn’t have before.” She also noted concerns about side effects, echoing a broader hesitancy among some medical staff. “I’ve heard stories of headaches and fever after vaccination,” she said, “but I trust the data that shows it’s safe.” These sentiments reflect the delicate balance between urgent protection and fear of unknown adverse events, a tension documented in previous vaccine rollouts in the region [6].

Community members in rural villages respond more nuancedly. While some welcome the vaccine as a lifeline, others remain skeptical, citing mistrust of foreign interventions and past experiences with healthcare campaigns that failed to address local needs. A community leader in Beni remarked, “We need to be sure that this vaccine is not a tool to replace real care. We want doctors and supplies, not just shots.” This mistrust is compounded by political instability, with armed groups controlling parts of the affected provinces and disrupting supply chains. The Congolese government has worked closely with international partners to secure safe corridors for vaccine distribution, but logistical hurdles persist [7].

WHO emphasises that vaccinating health workers is a cornerstone of a broader containment strategy. “Health workers are the first line of defense,” said Dr. Maria van Kerkhove, WHO’s technical lead for Ebola. “Protecting them reduces the risk of secondary transmission and builds community confidence in the response.” WHO’s data from the 2018‑2020 outbreak showed that vaccinating health workers reduced the secondary attack rate by 60 %, underscoring the vaccine’s impact on outbreak dynamics [8].

Donor agencies, including the Global Fund and the United Nations Development Programme, have pledged additional funding to support the vaccine rollout and strengthen cold‑chain logistics. “We recognise that the vaccine is only part of the solution,” said a senior official from the Global Fund. “We must also invest in training, surveillance, and community engagement to ensure lasting impact.” These commitments are essential, given the limited supply of the vaccine and the need for sustained public health infrastructure improvements [9].

Manufacturers of rVSV‑ZEBOV have confirmed that the supply chain is expanding. Merck, the vaccine’s producer, stated that it is increasing production capacity to meet global demand, though the lead time for new batches remains several months. “We are working to scale up production while maintaining quality standards,” a spokesperson said. “The urgency of the situation is clear, and we are committed to supporting the response” [10].

What to watch

The success of the vaccine rollout will hinge on several factors. First, maintaining the cold chain in remote and conflict‑affected areas will determine how many health workers can be safely vaccinated. Second, community acceptance will play a crucial role; if mistrust remains high, the vaccine’s reach may be limited. Third, the global supply of rVSV‑ZEBOV must be replenished to prevent shortages that could stall the campaign.

In the long term, the DRC’s experience may influence global policy on outbreak preparedness. The shift from reactive treatment to proactive vaccination could become a standard approach for future Ebola outbreaks, especially in regions with weak health infrastructure. Moreover, data generated from this rollout—on vaccine efficacy, side‑effect profiles, and impact on transmission—will enrich the scientific literature and inform future vaccine development.

The political landscape will also shape the response’s trajectory. Continued instability and armed conflict could disrupt logistics and erode public trust. Conversely, sustained political will and international cooperation could strengthen health systems and improve resilience against future epidemics.

In conclusion, the rollout of the rVSV‑ZEBOV vaccine to health workers in the DRC marks a pivotal moment in the fight against Ebola. By prioritising those on the front lines, the government and WHO aim to curb transmission, protect lives, and ultimately bring the outbreak under control. The coming weeks will test the efficacy of this strategy and the capacity of international partners to support a comprehensive, community‑centered response.
