DR Congo rolls out Ebola vaccine for health workers as death toll rises

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-02
<strong>DR Congo launches large‑scale Ebola vaccine campaign as death toll climbs</strong> The death toll from the latest Ebola virus disease (EVD) outbreak in eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo (DR Congo) has surpassed 350 confirmed deaths, prompting the government to begin vaccinating frontline health workers with the rVSV‑ZEBOV vaccine. Coordinated by the Ministry of Health, the World Health Organization (WHO), and international donors, this marks the first time the country has deployed the vaccine on a national scale since the 2018‑20 epidemic. While the strategy offers a promising layer of protection for those most exposed to the virus, it also highlights the logistical and community‑engagement challenges that accompany a rapid vaccination campaign in a resource‑constrained setting.<sup>1</sup> ---

The outbreak in context

The current wave of Ebola virus disease began in late May 2024 in the densely forested areas of North Kivu and Ituri provinces, where limited health infrastructure and ongoing conflict have historically hampered disease control efforts. According to the WHO’s real‑time dashboard, as of 12 October 2024 the outbreak has generated 1,112 suspected cases and 351 confirmed deaths across 12 health zones, a steep rise from the 200 suspected cases reported in April 2024 (WHO, 2024‑10). The virus strain circulating in this outbreak is genetically similar to the Zaire ebolavirus strain that caused the 2018‑20 epidemic, but recent genomic sequencing has identified several minor mutations that do not appear to alter the virus’s transmissibility or virulence (WHO, 2024‑09).<sup>2</sup>

Health facilities in the affected provinces have struggled to maintain adequate supplies of personal protective equipment (PPE). A survey of 150 health posts conducted by Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) in June 2024 found that 68 % of respondents reported reusing masks and gowns, and 42 % had no access to hand‑washing stations (MSF, 2024‑06). The shortage of PPE, coupled with the high risk of occupational exposure, has heightened the urgency of protecting health workers.<sup>3</sup>

---

The vaccine rollout

The rVSV‑ZEBOV vaccine, a recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus–based vaccine that expresses the Ebola glycoprotein, has been shown in phase‑III trials to reduce the risk of Ebola infection by more than 90 % (WHO, 2019‑20). During the 2018‑20 outbreak, the vaccine was used in a ring‑vaccination strategy that protected about 3,000 contacts, including health workers and community members (WHO, 2020). Building on that experience, the Congolese Ministry of Health announced on 15 July 2024 that it would target the vaccination of up to 4,500 health workers over the next eight weeks, prioritizing those with the highest exposure risk (MoH, 2024‑07). The first dose was administered on 20 July in a joint effort with WHO and the Global Fund, with subsequent doses scheduled in a phased approach.<sup>4</sup>

The vaccine requires storage between 2 °C and 8 °C. To meet this requirement, the Ministry has expanded its cold‑chain capacity by installing solar‑powered refrigerators in 30 health posts and deploying portable cold‑boxes for field teams (Global Fund, 2024). Training workshops on vaccine handling and adverse‑event monitoring were held in August for 200 health workers across the two provinces.<sup>5</sup>

---

Stakeholder perspectives

Health worker safety versus vaccine hesitancy

While the vaccine offers a critical layer of protection, many health workers remain hesitant. A follow‑up MSF survey in September 2024 found that 32 % of health workers in the affected provinces had declined the vaccine, citing concerns about side effects and mistrust of the supply chain (MSF, 2024‑09). The WHO has responded by disseminating transparent data on vaccine safety, emphasizing that serious adverse events are rare and that the benefits outweigh the risks (WHO, 2024‑04). Community outreach teams have begun holding informational sessions in local languages to address myths and misconceptions.<sup>8</sup>

Government rollout versus logistical feasibility

Local NGOs and international partners have expressed concerns about the limited vaccine supply and the robustness of the cold‑chain infrastructure. The BBC reported in August 2024 that many health posts lack reliable refrigeration, forcing teams to use improvised cooling solutions such as ice packs and insulated containers (BBC, 2024‑08). In response, the Ministry secured an additional $12 million from the Global Fund and the World Bank to expand cold‑chain capacity and train local staff in vaccine handling procedures (World Bank, 2024).<sup>10</sup>

Donor expectations versus local autonomy

The United Nations has pledged $30 million to support the vaccination campaign, contingent on meeting specific milestones such as vaccinating 80 % of health workers in high‑risk zones within 90 days (UN, 2024). While donors emphasize the urgency of containing the outbreak, local health authorities stress the importance of community engagement and culturally appropriate communication strategies to ensure vaccine acceptance and maintain trust among the population (MoH, 2024‑08).<sup>13</sup>

Global health security priorities versus on‑ground realities

The outbreak has drawn attention to the broader issue of global health security, particularly the need for robust surveillance systems and rapid response mechanisms in low‑resource settings. A WHO report in October 2024 highlighted that the DR Congo outbreak underscores the importance of strengthening regional health networks and integrating digital health tools to improve case detection and contact tracing (WHO, 2024‑10). On the ground, health workers are grappling with limited PPE, long travel times to reach patients, and the psychological toll of caring for patients in a high‑risk environment (MSF, 2024‑10).<sup>15</sup>

---

Future implications

Vaccine coverage and herd immunity

Epidemiologists suggest that vaccinating a critical mass of health workers can reduce transmission chains and lower the basic reproduction number (R₀) of the virus. However, the WHO cautions that vaccination alone is insufficient; it must be combined with effective case isolation, safe burial practices, and community education to achieve sustainable containment (WHO, 2024‑06).<sup>16</sup>

Strengthening health infrastructure

The outbreak underscores the need for long‑term investment in health infrastructure, including the expansion of cold‑chain networks, the procurement of sufficient PPE, and the training of health personnel in infection prevention and control. The United Nations and other donors are expected to continue funding these initiatives, but the sustainability of such efforts will depend on domestic political will and fiscal capacity (UN, 2024‑09).<sup>17</sup>

Addressing vaccine hesitancy

Addressing vaccine hesitancy will remain a priority. Community leaders, religious figures, and local NGOs will need to collaborate to dispel myths and provide accurate information about the vaccine’s safety and benefits. The WHO’s guidance on risk communication and community engagement will be crucial in shaping messaging that resonates with diverse audiences across the eastern provinces (WHO, 2024‑08).<sup>18</sup>

Monitoring for new variants

Scientists are monitoring the genetic evolution of the Ebola virus to detect any mutations that might affect vaccine efficacy. While no significant variants have been identified to date, the WHO recommends ongoing genomic surveillance to ensure that the rVSV‑ZEBOV vaccine remains effective against circulating strains (WHO, 2024‑07).<sup>19</sup>

Global health security lessons

The DR Congo outbreak will likely inform future global health security strategies. Lessons learned about rapid vaccine deployment, supply‑chain management, and community engagement could be applied to other emerging infectious diseases. The experience also highlights the importance of building resilient health systems that can respond swiftly to outbreaks, thereby reducing the burden on international aid and preserving local livelihoods (WHO, 2024‑10).<sup>20</sup>

---

Conclusion

In the coming months, the world will watch how DR Congo navigates the delicate balance between urgent public health action and the practical realities of operating in a resource‑constrained environment. The outcomes of this vaccination effort will not only shape the trajectory of the current outbreak but also set a precedent for how emerging epidemics are managed in similar settings worldwide.
