Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-02
<h1>Scientists Report First Candidate Radio Detection from an Exoplanet, but Confirmation Still Pending</h1> <p>A team led by Dr. L. K. Miller of the University of Cambridge has announced the first tentative detection of radio emission from an exoplanet. The signal, observed at 150 MHz with the Low‑Frequency Array (LOFAR) and confirmed by the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT), appears to originate from the close‑in giant planet TOI‑1234 b, which orbits a G‑type star 300 light‑years away. While the result is exciting, it has not yet been independently verified and remains a candidate rather than a confirmed discovery. The claim underscores both the promise and the challenges of exoplanetary radio astronomy.</p> <h2>Background: From Jupiter to the Exoplanet Frontier</h2> <p>Radio emission from planets in our Solar System has been known for decades. Jupiter’s powerful decametric bursts, first recorded in the 1950s, have provided a wealth of information about its magnetic field, auroral processes, and interaction with the solar wind (Zarka 1998). In contrast, attempts to detect similar emission from exoplanets have largely yielded upper limits. The 2015 LOFAR campaign set a 3‑σ limit of < 10 mJy on the hot Jupiter HD 189733 b at 150 MHz (G. D. B. et al. 2015). Subsequent observations with the Very Large Array (VLA) and GMRT have produced comparable constraints (Zarka et al. 2017; Obermeier et al. 2018).</p> <p>These efforts have been guided by theoretical models that predict cyclotron maser emission from magnetized planets interacting with stellar winds (Zarka 2007). The emission frequency is set by the planet’s magnetic field strength, \(f_{\rm c}=2.8\,B\) MHz (B in gauss). For a Jupiter‑like field of ~14 G, the peak emission would lie near 40 MHz, well below the 150 MHz band used in most campaigns. However, for close‑in hot Jupiters, stronger magnetic fields and enhanced stellar wind pressures could shift the emission to higher frequencies (Grießmeier et al. 2007).</p> <h2>The Candidate Detection</h2> <p>Miller et al. (2025) report that during a 12‑month monitoring program with LOFAR, they identified a narrowband signal centered at 150 MHz that persisted for several hours each observing night. The signal’s flux density varied between 5 and 12 mJy, with a quasi‑periodic modulation matching the 12.3‑hour orbital period of TOI‑1234 b (Miller et al. 2025). The team performed a series of null tests, observing nearby stars of similar spectral type and distance, and found no comparable emission. A follow‑up observation with GMRT, using a different array configuration and independent calibration pipeline, recovered a signal with a 4‑σ significance at the same frequency and modulation period (Miller et al. 2025).</p> <p>Although the detection is intriguing, Miller et al. emphasize that the signal could still arise from an unrecognized terrestrial radio frequency interference (RFI) source or an unexpected stellar phenomenon. They call for independent confirmation with additional facilities, such as the Murchison Widefield Array (MWA) and the upcoming Square Kilometre Array (SKA) pathfinders. The authors also note that the signal’s spectral width (~1 MHz) and temporal stability are consistent with cyclotron maser emission, but alternative explanations cannot yet be ruled out (Miller et al. 2025).</p> <h2>Methodological Advances and Challenges</h2> <p>Detecting exoplanetary radio emission is technically demanding. The signals are expected to be faint (< 10 mJy) and narrowband, often buried beneath the much brighter stellar radio output and terrestrial RFI. The Miller team employed several advanced techniques: real‑time RFI excision using machine‑learning classifiers (B. S. H. et al. 2024), phased‑array beamforming to increase sensitivity, and cross‑correlation between independent telescopes to reject local interference (B. S. H. et al. 2024). They also used a novel “null‑field” strategy, observing a control field offset by 1° to monitor background noise levels in real time (B. S. H. et al. 2024).</p> <p>Despite these innovations, systematic uncertainties remain. The absolute flux calibration at low frequencies can vary by 10–20 % due to ionospheric effects (S. A. M. et al. 2023). Moreover, the intrinsic variability of the host star’s radio emission, driven by magnetic activity cycles, can mimic planetary signatures if not properly modeled (Khodachenko et al. 2007). The authors therefore advocate for simultaneous optical and ultraviolet monitoring to constrain stellar activity during radio observations (Miller et al. 2025).</p> <h2>Implications for Exoplanet Science</h2> <p>If confirmed, the detection would open a new window onto the magnetic environments of exoplanets. Planetary magnetic fields play a crucial role in protecting atmospheres from stellar winds and in shaping space weather conditions that could influence habitability. Measuring the field strength of a hot Jupiter would also test models of planetary dynamos and interior structure. Furthermore, radio observations could provide an independent method for determining orbital parameters and planetary masses, complementing transit and radial‑velocity techniques.</p> <h2>Next Steps and Future Observations</h2> <p>To establish the planetary origin of the signal, astronomers plan to observe TOI‑1234 b with a range of instruments that operate at different frequencies and have distinct RFI environments. The MWA, which observes at 80–300 MHz, will test whether the emission extends to lower frequencies. The SKA pathfinders, with their unprecedented sensitivity, will search for similar signals from other hot Jupiters and from smaller, potentially Earth‑size planets. In addition, coordinated campaigns that combine radio, optical, and ultraviolet data will help disentangle stellar activity from planetary emission.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The tentative detection of radio emission from TOI‑1234 b represents a milestone in exoplanet research, but it also highlights the need for rigorous confirmation. Continued observations with multiple facilities, coupled with improved calibration techniques, will determine whether the signal truly originates from a magnetized exoplanet. Until then, the discovery remains a promising candidate that could, if verified, usher in a new era of exoplanetary radio astronomy.</p>
