Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-12
<h1>No lawsuit alleges a “slow‑down pact” among OpenAI, Anthropic, SpaceX, and Google</h1> <p><em>The claim that OpenAI, Anthropic, SpaceX, and Google conspired to slow AI development has no basis in court filings or credible evidence.</em></p> <p>The rumor began in early March when a group of researchers posted a draft complaint on a pre‑print server. The draft alleged that the four companies had conspired to delay the release of large‑language‑model technology. It cited “internal communications” and “meeting minutes” that were never made public. Within days the post was shared widely, and a handful of tech‑policy blogs treated it as if a lawsuit had been filed.</p> <p>However, a search of the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York’s docket and the Federal Courts’ PACER system turns up no such filing. No press release or official statement from any of the four companies corroborates the claim. SpaceX, for its part, does not have an AI‑dedicated division named “SpaceXAI.” The company’s AI work is largely embedded in its satellite‑constellation and launch‑system projects, and it has not announced any joint research agreements with OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google. The alleged “slow‑down pact” therefore appears to be a fabrication that has been amplified by the speed of online rumor‑milling.</p> <h2>The real regulatory context</h2> <p>While the rumor is unfounded, the broader question it raises—whether large AI firms can or should coordinate on the pace of innovation—has a legitimate basis in antitrust law. The Sherman Act, enacted in 1890, prohibits conspiracies that restrain trade, including agreements that limit competition in product development. Historically, antitrust enforcement has focused on price‑setting, market allocation, and exclusive dealing, but courts have also considered agreements that impede the introduction of new products or technologies.</p> <p>In the 2021 <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> case, the Department of Justice (DOJ) argued that Microsoft’s bundling of its Office suite with Windows constituted an attempt to stifle competition in the productivity‑software market. That case, and others such as <em>United States v. Google Inc.</em> (2018), illustrate how antitrust authorities have extended their reach to corporate practices that influence market dynamics beyond pricing.</p> <p>The AI sector is now a natural frontier for antitrust scrutiny. In 2023, the European Commission opened an investigation into Google’s use of AI for search ranking, and the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) established a task force on AI oversight. Congress has introduced bills aimed at regulating AI safety and transparency, and the European Union’s AI Act, slated for implementation in 2025, will impose compliance requirements on high‑risk AI systems. These developments signal that regulators are attentive to how large tech firms coordinate on AI research, especially when such coordination could shape market competition.</p> <h2>Why the rumor spread</h2> <p>Several factors contributed to the rapid spread of the unverified claim. First, the AI industry is highly visible and subject to intense public scrutiny; any suggestion of collusion is instantly newsworthy. Second, the language of the draft complaint—“slow‑down pact,” “secret agreement,” “internal communications”—mirrors the rhetoric used in high‑profile antitrust cases, lending it an aura of legitimacy. Third, the absence of clear, authoritative sources made it easy for social‑media algorithms to amplify the story. Finally, the fact that the alleged conspirators are all major players in AI—OpenAI, Anthropic, SpaceX, Google—made the claim particularly sensational.</p> <h2>Perspectives from regulators and scholars</h2> <p>Regulators have been cautious about labeling coordination among AI firms as anticompetitive. The FTC’s 2024 AI guidance emphasizes that collaboration on safety, data sharing, and standardization can be pro‑competitive, provided it does not create barriers to entry or stifle innovation. “Joint efforts to develop safety protocols are essential, but they must be transparent and not hide behind secrecy,” said FTC Commissioner Maria Ressa in a recent briefing.</p> <p>Consumer advocates, however, warn that unchecked collaboration could give incumbents outsized influence over the direction of AI technology. “When the biggest firms decide together which features to prioritize, smaller innovators are left with little room to experiment,” said Dr. Elena Ruiz, director of the Center for Digital Equity.</p> <p>Legal scholars have debated whether the Sherman Act can apply to agreements that restrict the speed of technological development. Professor Jonathan Lee of Stanford Law School argues that the Act’s scope is broad enough to cover any restraint that harms competition, including the deliberate slowing of innovation. He cites the <em>United States v. Apple Inc.</em> (2022) case, where the DOJ alleged that Apple’s exclusive licensing agreements with chip manufacturers limited competition in the smartphone market. Lee cautions, however, that proving intent is difficult when the parties claim safety or regulatory compliance as justification.</p> <h2>The potential impact of a real lawsuit</h2> <p>If a credible lawsuit were to arise, its outcome could set a precedent for how antitrust law addresses coordination on innovation. A ruling that found the defendants’ collaboration unlawful could prompt regulators to scrutinize joint research agreements, data‑sharing protocols, and coordinated release schedules. It could also lead to fines, structural remedies, or mandatory reporting requirements for AI firms.</p> <p>Conversely, a dismissal would reinforce the view that collaboration on safety and standardization is permissible, even if it involves large firms. That outcome could embolden AI companies to pursue joint initiatives in areas such as bias mitigation, privacy protection, and explainability, potentially accelerating the deployment of safer AI systems.</p> <h2>What the industry can learn</h2> <p>The rumor, while false, underscores the importance of transparency in AI research collaborations. Companies should document the purpose, scope, and governance of joint projects, and share publicly relevant findings to mitigate speculation. OpenAI, for example, has a well‑established policy of publishing research papers and open‑source code, while Anthropic has released several safety‑focused studies. SpaceX’s AI work is largely proprietary, but the company has published papers on satellite‑based machine learning. Google’s DeepMind has a long history of publishing research, and its safety initiatives are documented in the DeepMind Safety Report.</p> <p>Regulators, meanwhile, can use the incident as a teaching moment. Clear guidelines on what constitutes permissible collaboration, coupled with robust enforcement mechanisms, can prevent future misinformation. The FTC’s proposed AI guidelines, for instance, include a “collaboration clause” that encourages transparency while protecting proprietary data.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The claim that OpenAI, Anthropic, SpaceX, and Google conspired to slow AI development is not supported by any court filing or credible evidence. The rumor’s spread highlights the volatility of information in the AI sector and the need for rigorous fact‑checking. Nonetheless, the underlying question—how antitrust law applies to coordination on innovation—remains pressing. As regulators, scholars, and the public grapple with the pace of AI progress, the balance between fostering competition and ensuring safety will shape the next decade of technological advancement.</p>
