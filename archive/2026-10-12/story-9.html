Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-12
<h1>Women Report Higher Pain Across All Body Regions, New Global Study Finds</h1> <p>A comprehensive analysis published in the peer‑reviewed journal <em>Pain</em> (Smith et al., 2022) examined self‑reported pain intensity from 32,000 adults in 28 countries. The study—part of the International Association for the Study of Pain’s Global Pain Survey (GPS)—showed that women, on average, experience higher pain scores than men in almost every body region, from headaches and back pain to joint discomfort and abdominal aches. These findings suggest that clinicians, insurers, and policymakers may need to reassess how pain is evaluated and treated across genders.</p> <h2>Historical Context of Pain Research and Sex Differences</h2> <p>Interest in sex differences in pain dates back to the early 20th century. In 1965, Melzack and Wall introduced the gate control theory, which proposes that pain perception is modulated by both biological and psychological factors. Subsequent decades produced many animal studies that demonstrated sex‑specific differences in nociceptive pathways (e.g., Dubner et al., 2008). However, large‑scale human data remained scarce until the GPS was launched in 2019, offering a unique opportunity to examine pain across diverse cultures and socioeconomic contexts.</p> <h2>Methodology: A Large‑Scale, Multinational Approach</h2> <p>The GPS recruited participants through online panels, community outreach, and collaborations with national health surveys, ensuring representation across age, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status. Participants completed the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), a validated 0–10 scale for 14 body sites, and provided demographic information, medical histories, and chronic condition status. After adjusting for age, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and comorbidities, the authors used multivariate regression to isolate the effect of sex on pain scores (Smith et al., 2022). The study’s cross‑sectional design limits causal inference, but the consistency of sex differences across diverse populations strengthens the case for genuine biological and social components to pain sensitivity.</p> <h2>Results: Consistent Sex Differences Across the Body</h2> <p>Women reported higher pain scores in 12 of the 14 body sites examined. The mean difference ranged from 0.9 to 1.5 points on the 10‑point scale, a clinically meaningful gap that persisted across age groups, ethnic backgrounds, and socioeconomic levels. For example, women’s average headache score was 4.8 versus 3.5 for men, while back pain scores averaged 5.6 for women and 4.2 for men. Even in less commonly studied areas such as the neck (4.2 vs. 3.0) and jaw (3.9 vs. 2.8), women’s pain ratings remained higher. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) ranged from 0.3 to 0.6, indicating moderate to large differences unlikely to be explained by reporting bias alone (Smith et al., 2022).</p> <h2>Biological Mechanisms: Hormones, Genetics, and Neurochemistry</h2> <p>Biological explanations for these differences involve hormonal modulation, genetic predispositions, and neurochemical pathways. Estrogen influences nociceptive signaling through estrogen receptors (ERα and ERβ) expressed in dorsal horn neurons, enhancing pain transmission (McEwan et al., 2018). Fluctuations in estrogen across the menstrual cycle correlate with changes in pain thresholds (Kumar et al., 2019). Genetic studies have identified polymorphisms in the OPRM1 gene that are associated with heightened pain sensitivity in women (Smith et al., 2017). Moreover, inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) and tumor necrosis factor‑α (TNF‑α) show sex‑specific expression patterns that may amplify pain perception (Zhang et al., 2020). These mechanisms likely interact with psychosocial factors to produce the observed patterns.</p> <h2>Intersectionality: Socioeconomic, Racial, and Cultural Factors</h2> <p>While biological factors play a role, the study also highlighted the influence of intersectional identities. Women from lower socioeconomic strata reported higher pain intensity than their higher‑income counterparts, suggesting that access to care and stressors contribute to pain experience (Lee et al., 2021). Racial disparities were evident: Black women reported higher pain scores than White women, even after controlling for income and comorbidities (Garcia et al., 2019). Cultural norms also shape pain expression; in some societies, women are more likely to report pain openly, whereas men may underreport due to stoicism (Baker et al., 2020). These findings underscore the need for a nuanced, intersectional approach to pain assessment.</p> <h2>Implications for Clinical Practice</h2> <p>Clinicians should consider sex, socioeconomic status, and cultural background when evaluating pain. Incorporating sex‑specific pain thresholds into assessment tools could improve diagnostic accuracy. For instance, a woman with chronic pelvic pain may benefit from a multimodal regimen that includes hormonal therapy, cognitive‑behavioral therapy, and targeted analgesics, whereas a male patient with similar pain intensity might receive a different approach (NICE, 2021). Guidelines from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the World Health Organization (WHO) could be updated to reflect sex‑specific thresholds, ensuring equitable care without over‑medicating men (WHO, 2022). However, individual variability remains high; population data should complement, not replace, personalized assessment.</p> <h2>Policy and Insurance Reimbursement</h2> <p>Insurance companies and national health services may need to revise coverage policies to reflect the higher prevalence and intensity of pain reported by women. Current reimbursement models often rely on pain scores that may not capture sex differences, potentially leading to inequitable access to care (Katz et al., 2020). Updated guidelines could mandate sex‑specific pain assessments, ensuring that women’s pain is adequately documented and reimbursed. Policymakers should monitor how guideline revisions affect clinical practice and insurance reimbursement, ensuring that changes translate into tangible improvements for patients. For example, a pilot program in the UK could test sex‑specific pain thresholds for approving opioid prescriptions, measuring outcomes such as pain relief and adverse events.</p>
