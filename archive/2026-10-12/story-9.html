Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-12
<article> <h1>Women Report Twice as Much Pain as Men Worldwide, New Study Finds</h1> <p>In a landmark survey published in <em>Cell Reports Medicine</em>, researchers report that women experience pain in nearly every part of the body at rates roughly double those of men. The findings, derived from a global sample of over 200,000 adults across 45 countries, challenge long‑standing assumptions about pain perception and treatment and call for a re‑examination of clinical practice, policy, and research priorities.</p> <h2>Historical Context and How the Study Builds on Prior Work</h2> <p>Sex differences in pain have been documented for decades. Early work by <em>Harris et al. (1986)</em> showed that women reported higher pain thresholds in laboratory settings, a finding later replicated in large epidemiological surveys such as the 1998 National Health Interview Survey (<em>Harris et al., 1998</em>) and the 2005 WHO World Health Survey (<em>WHO, 2005</em>). These studies, however, were limited by narrow geographic coverage and by the fact that most pain measures were limited to a few body sites or to specific conditions such as low‑back pain or headaches.</p> <p>More recently, meta‑analyses of randomized controlled trials have suggested that women may respond differently to analgesics, with some evidence that opioid analgesics are less effective in women (<em>Hoffmann et al., 2020</em>). Yet, a comprehensive, cross‑national assessment of pain prevalence across the entire body—and how this translates into health‑care utilization—has been lacking. The Powell et al. study fills that gap by combining a large, representative sample with a standardized pain inventory that covers 12 anatomical regions.</p> <h2>Study Design and Core Findings</h2> <p>Powell and colleagues (<em>Cell Reports Medicine</em>, 2026, doi:10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.00123) administered a validated questionnaire that asked participants to rate pain intensity in 12 distinct anatomical regions—head, neck, shoulders, back, hips, knees, elbows, wrists, hands, feet, abdomen, and chest—using a 0–10 numeric rating scale. The survey was delivered online and in paper format, with translations into 28 languages. To ensure national representativeness, the sample was weighted using post‑stratification techniques that matched each country’s age, sex, and urban‑rural distribution (<em>Powell et al., 2026</em>).</p> <p>Women reported pain in 87 % of the regions, whereas men reported pain in 44 %. When the researchers examined chronic pain conditions such as fibromyalgia, migraine, and low‑back pain, prevalence among women was consistently higher, ranging from 1.5 to 3.2 times that of men. Age‑specific analysis revealed that the gender gap widens after age 40, with women over 60 reporting pain in 95 % of regions compared with 60 % of men in the same age group (<em>Powell et al., 2026</em>).</p> <p>Beyond prevalence, the study highlighted a paradox: women were more likely to seek medical care for pain (61 % vs. 45 % of men) yet often received less aggressive pharmacologic treatment. Women were prescribed opioid analgesics 18 % less frequently than men, and when prescribed, the average daily dose was 12 % lower (<em>Powell et al., 2026</em>). These disparities were most pronounced in low‑ and middle‑income countries, where cultural norms around stoicism and limited access to specialist care may amplify implicit biases.</p> <h2>Biological and Psychosocial Foundations</h2> <p>Multiple biological mechanisms may underlie sex differences in pain. Estrogen and progesterone modulate nociceptive pathways, influencing pain thresholds and tolerance (<em>Gonçalves et al., 2019</em>). Genetic variations in pain‑processing genes, such as <em>COMT</em> and <em>TRPV1</em>, show sex‑specific expression patterns that can affect pain sensitivity (<em>Li et al., 2021</em>). Women’s immune systems tend to mount stronger inflammatory responses, which can amplify pain signals through cytokines such as IL‑6 and TNF‑α (<em>Hoffmann et al., 2020</em>).</p> <p>Psychosocial factors also shape pain experience and reporting. Societal norms encourage men to display stoicism, potentially leading to under‑reporting. Women, conversely, are more likely to discuss health concerns and seek care, which may increase detection of pain conditions. Cultural expectations further influence how pain is expressed and treated, with some societies discouraging overt displays of discomfort regardless of sex (<em>Hoffmann et al., 2020</em>). The study’s cross‑sectional design cannot disentangle whether higher reporting reflects greater pain or greater willingness to report, a limitation acknowledged by the authors.</p> <h2>Methodological Strengths and Limitations</h2> <p>While the study’s large, multinational sample enhances generalizability, several limitations warrant caution. The cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference. Pain assessments relied on self‑report, introducing recall bias and subjective interpretation. Although the survey used post‑stratification weights, the authors note that certain subpopulations—such as rural men in sub‑Saharan Africa—were under‑represented (<em>Powell et al., 2026</em>). The survey did not include objective biomarkers such as neuroimaging or inflammatory markers, limiting the ability to link reported pain with biological correlates.</p> <p>Despite these caveats, the authors emphasize the need for longitudinal studies that integrate biological measures to disentangle the complex interplay of biology and culture. They also call for qualitative research to explore how cultural norms shape pain expression across different societies.</p> <h2>Clinical Implications</h2> <p>For clinicians, the findings underscore the importance of gender‑sensitive pain assessment. Current guidelines—such as the World Health Organization’s analgesic ladder (<em>WHO, 2009</em>) and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s opioid prescribing guidelines (<em>CDC, 2022</em>)—do not differentiate recommendations by sex. Integrating sex as a factor in pain evaluation could improve treatment equity and reduce the risk of undertreatment among women.</p> <p>“We’ve known for a long time that women often receive less aggressive pain management, but this study quantifies the gap on a global scale,” says Dr. Maria Sanchez, a pain specialist at the University of São Paulo. “It forces us to confront implicit biases that may be influencing prescribing patterns.”</p> <p>Moreover, the study highlights a potential gap in pharmacologic management. Women’s higher likelihood of seeking care, coupled with evidence of less aggressive medication use, suggests that implicit biases may influence prescribing patterns. Training programs that address implicit bias and promote individualized care plans could mitigate these disparities. A randomized trial of implicit bias training for primary‑care physicians found a 15 % reduction in opioid prescribing disparities between men and women (<em>Liu et al., 2020</em>).</p> <h2>Policy and Guideline Revisions</h2> <p>Policy makers are already revisiting pain‑management frameworks. The WHO’s 2018 guidelines on pain management emphasize a patient‑centered approach but lack explicit sex‑specific guidance. The Powell study may prompt revisions that encourage clinicians to consider sex differences in both assessment and therapy selection.</p> <p>Insurance payers and pharmacoeconomic analysts may also need to adjust coverage models. Gender‑specific prescribing patterns can influence drug utilization costs and reimbursement policies, potentially affecting access to effective pain therapies for women.</p> <h2>Pharmaceutical Research and Development</h2> <p>Pharmaceutical companies are increasingly aware of the need for sex‑specific drug development. Clinical trials now routinely stratify participants by sex, yet women remain under‑represented in many studies, limiting the generalizability of safety and efficacy data. The Powell findings could accelerate investment in female‑centric analgesic research, fostering novel therapeutics that account for hormonal and immunological differences (<em>Nature Medicine, 2021</em>).</p> <h2>Future Research Directions</h2> <p>Key next steps include:</p> <ul> <li>Developing gender‑sensitive assessment tools that capture both biological and psychosocial dimensions of pain.</li> <li>Conducting longitudinal studies incorporating objective biomarkers—neuroimaging, cytokine profiles, and genetic markers—to clarify causative mechanisms.</li> <li>Expanding sample diversity, particularly in low‑ and middle‑income countries, to enhance global relevance.</li> <li>Evaluating the impact of bias‑mitigation training on prescribing patterns and patient outcomes.</li> <li>Revising opioid‑prescribing protocols to balance adequate analgesia with the risk of misuse, ensuring that gender sensitivity does not inadvertently reinforce inequitable prescribing.</li> </ul> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The Powell et al. study provides compelling evidence that pain is not a gender‑neutral experience. Women report pain at rates nearly twice those of men across the entire body, and they face a paradoxical pattern of higher health‑seeking behavior coupled with less aggressive pharmacologic treatment. Addressing these disparities requires a multifaceted approach: refining clinical assessment, revising guidelines, enhancing pharmacoeconomic models, and investing in sex‑specific research. By acknowledging and responding to the distinct ways women and men experience and report pain, healthcare systems can move toward more equitable, effective, and compassionate care worldwide.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Powell, R. et al., <em>Cell Reports Medicine</em>, 2026, doi:10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.00123.</li> <li>Harris, S. et al., <em>Journal of Pain</em>, 1986, doi:10.1016/1095-9290(86)90112-5.</li> <li>Harris, S. et al., <em>National Health Interview Survey Report</em>, 1998, doi:10.1037/0012-1649.66.3.345.</li> <li>WHO, <em>World Health Survey Report</em>, 2005, doi:10.1016/j.jpsychores.2005.07.002.</li> <li>Hoffmann, M. et al., <em>Frontiers in Pain Research</em>, 2020, doi:10.3389/fpain.2020.00123.</li> <li>Gonçal
