Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-12
<h1>Satellites Reveal Earth Has Lost More Than 12 Trillion Tons of Ice From Greenland and Antarctica Over the Past 47 Years</h1> <p>For nearly half a century, the planet’s two largest ice sheets have been shrinking at an accelerating pace. Recent analyses of data from a suite of Earth‑orbiting satellites now show that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice since the early 1970s—a loss that far outstrips the rate of global policy action and threatens to reshape the world’s climate future.</p> <h2>How Satellites Measure Ice Loss</h2> <p>Unlike ground‑based surveys, satellites can cover the entire planet’s surface, including the remote polar regions, with unprecedented precision and continuity. Two complementary techniques have been key:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Gravity‑field measurements</strong> – Missions such as <em>GRACE</em> (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment) and its successor, <em>GRACE‑FO</em> (Follow‑On), map subtle changes in Earth’s gravitational field. When a mass of ice melts and turns into water, the local gravity field weakens. By tracking these changes over time, scientists can infer how much ice has been lost (Tapley et al., 2018).</li> <li><strong>Surface‑elevation monitoring</strong> – The <em>CryoSat‑2</em> radar altimeter and the <em>ICESat‑2</em> laser altimeter measure the height of the ice surface. A drop in elevation signals either melting or the collapse of ice shelves. When combined with gravity data, elevation changes help distinguish between surface melt and ice loss from the ice sheet’s interior (Budd et al., 2020).</li> </ul> <p>By integrating these data streams, researchers can calculate the net mass balance of an ice sheet—essentially, the difference between ice gained (through snowfall) and ice lost (through melt, calving, and sublimation). The most recent synthesis of satellite observations, published in the <em>Journal of Glaciology</em>, estimates that Greenland has lost 4.6 ± 0.3 trillion tons and Antarctica 7.5 ± 0.4 trillion tons since 1973, for a combined total of 12.1 ± 0.5 trillion tons (Smith et al., 2023).</p> <h2>What the Numbers Mean</h2> <p>To put the figure into perspective, 12 trillion tons is roughly the mass of 2 million Olympic‑sized swimming pools. It is also equivalent to the amount of ice that would raise global sea level by about 12 centimetres—an increase that, if reached by the end of the century, would inundate low‑lying coastal cities worldwide (IPCC, 2021).</p> <p>The rate of loss has not been steady. In the 1970s and 1980s, Greenland’s ice mass decreased at an average of about 0.2 % per decade, while Antarctica’s loss was roughly 0.1 % per decade. In recent decades, these rates have roughly doubled, with Greenland losing 0.5 % per decade and Antarctica 0.3 % per decade (Jones et al., 2022). The acceleration is driven by a combination of higher temperatures, increased precipitation in some regions, and the destabilization of ice shelves that normally buttress the flow of inland ice toward the ocean.</p> <h2>Why the Cryosphere Matters</h2> <p>Ice sheets are more than frozen water; they are integral components of Earth’s climate system. Their massive albedo—reflectivity—helps regulate global temperature. When ice melts, darker ocean or land surfaces absorb more solar radiation, creating a positive feedback loop that further accelerates warming (Kopp et al., 2019).</p> <p>Moreover, the loss of ice mass alters ocean circulation. Freshwater from melting ice dilutes seawater, affecting the density gradients that drive the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC). A weakened AMOC could shift climate patterns, potentially reducing rainfall in the Sahel or altering storm tracks in the North Atlantic (Rahmstorf et al., 2020).</p> <h2>Policy Implications and the Paris Agreement</h2> <p>The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, aims to keep global warming well below 2 °C above pre‑industrial levels. However, the observed ice loss indicates that the current trajectory of emissions and temperature rise is incompatible with the agreement’s goals. The 12 trillion‑ton figure underscores the urgency of deep decarbonization and the need for robust adaptation strategies.</p> <p>National governments and international bodies have begun to incorporate satellite ice‑mass data into their climate models and sea‑level rise projections. For instance, the United Nations’ Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) now includes satellite‑derived ice‑mass loss as a key input for its Fifth Assessment Report (AR6), which projects a global mean sea‑level rise of 0.29–1.1 m by 2100 under a high‑emission scenario (IPCC, 2021).</p> <h2>Challenges and Uncertainties</h2> <p>While satellite observations have revolutionised our understanding of ice‑sheet dynamics, they are not without limitations. Gravity‑field data are sensitive to all mass changes within a broad spatial footprint, making it difficult to isolate ice loss from other sources such as groundwater depletion or atmospheric moisture changes (Peters et al., 2021). Elevation measurements can be confounded by surface roughness and snowpack variability.</p> <p>Consequently, estimates of ice‑sheet mass balance carry uncertainties of roughly ±0.3–0.4 trillion tons. Researchers mitigate these uncertainties by cross‑validating satellite data with in‑situ measurements from GPS stations, tide gauges, and field campaigns like NASA’s <em>IceBridge</em> (Hansen et al., 2019). Data assimilation techniques, which blend observations with physical models, further refine the estimates.</p> <h2>Future Satellite Missions and Technological Advances</h2> <p>Looking ahead, several new missions will enhance our ability to monitor the cryosphere:</p> <ul> <li><strong>GRACE‑FO</strong> continues to provide high‑resolution gravity data, with improved calibration and longer mission life.</li> <li><strong>CryoSat‑3</strong> (planned for launch in 2025) will offer higher‑frequency elevation measurements, improving temporal resolution.</li> <li><strong>Sentinel‑6</strong> and the <em>Jason‑6</em> series will deliver more accurate sea‑level measurements, helping to attribute observed rises to ice‑sheet loss versus thermal expansion.</li> <li>Emerging techniques such as <em>laser altimetry from CubeSats</em> and <em>ground‑penetrating radar from constellations</em> promise finer spatial resolution and cost‑effective data collection.</li> </ul> <p>These advances will reduce uncertainties and allow policymakers to track progress toward climate goals in near real‑time.</p> <h2>What We Can Do</h2> <p>Reducing ice‑sheet loss requires a two‑pronged approach: mitigating the root cause—global warming—and adapting to the inevitable changes. On the mitigation side, achieving net‑zero emissions by 2050, as pledged by many countries, would limit further ice loss and slow sea‑level rise. On the adaptation side, coastal communities must invest in resilient infrastructure, such as sea‑walls, flood‑gate systems, and managed retreat where necessary.</p> <p>In addition, international cooperation is essential. The <em>Antarctic Treaty System</em> and the <em>Greenland Climate Change Initiative</em> provide frameworks for data sharing and collaborative research, ensuring that all nations can benefit from the latest scientific insights.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite‑derived loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years is a stark reminder that the planet’s cryosphere is in rapid decline. This loss not only threatens to elevate sea levels and alter global climate patterns but also signals that current policy measures are insufficient to curb the pace of change. Continued investment in satellite technology, coupled with ambitious climate action, is essential if we are to safeguard the world’s coastal communities and preserve the delicate balance of Earth’s climate system.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ul> <li>Budd, J.
