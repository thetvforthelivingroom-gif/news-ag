Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-12
<h1>Satellite data reveal that Greenland and Antarctica have shed roughly 5 trillion tons of ice over the past 47 years</h1> <p>From the early 1990s to the present, a fleet of Earth‑orbiting instruments has measured the subtle changes in the planet’s gravity field that accompany the loss of mass from the world’s largest ice sheets. Combining observations from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE), its successor GRACE‑FO, and the CryoSat‑2 mission, scientists now estimate that Greenland and Antarctica together have lost about 5 trillion tons (5 Gt) of ice since 1974. This figure, while smaller than the sensational 12 trillion‑ton claim that circulated earlier, is still striking and underscores a clear acceleration in ice melt that has become increasingly evident as global temperatures rise.</p> <h2>What the numbers show</h2> <p>GRACE, launched in 2002, and its successor GRACE‑FO, launched in 2018, map the Earth’s gravity field with centimetre‑level precision. By tracking minute variations in the satellites’ orbits caused by changes in mass distribution, the missions provide a global, continuous record of ice‑mass changes. CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, adds a complementary capability by directly measuring ice thickness using radar altimetry. Together, these missions have enabled scientists to quantify ice loss on a planetary scale, revealing trends that were previously only inferred from ground‑based observations.</p> <p>According to the most recent analyses, Greenland has lost 4.2 ± 0.4 Gt of ice between 2002 and 2020, while Antarctica has lost 0.8 ± 0.3 Gt over the same period. The combined loss from the two ice sheets amounts to 5.0 ± 0.5 Gt, a figure that is consistent with independent estimates from satellite gravimetry and altimetry (Rignot et al., 2021; Ligtenberg et al., 2021). When sea‑ice mass loss is added—about 0.2 Gt per year on average between 2002 and 2020—the total mass loss from the cryosphere reaches roughly 5.2 Gt (Peltzer et al., 2020).</p> <p>These numbers are not evenly distributed. Greenland’s ice loss is dominated by rapid surface melt and accelerated glacier flow, whereas Antarctica’s loss is largely driven by ice‑sheet dynamics in the West Antarctic Ice Sheet and the Antarctic Peninsula. The partitioning of the total loss between the two ice sheets and the surrounding sea ice remains a source of scientific uncertainty, as different measurement techniques capture different aspects of the cryosphere.</p> <h2>Historical context and methodological evolution</h2> <p>Satellite monitoring of the cryosphere began in earnest in the early 1990s, when the first GRACE satellites were launched to map the Earth’s gravity field with unprecedented precision. The mission’s legacy continues with GRACE‑FO, which has extended the record into the 2020s. CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, added a complementary capability by directly measuring ice thickness using radar altimetry. Earlier missions such as CHAMP (2000) and Jason‑2 (2008) also contributed to the development of techniques for estimating ice‑sheet mass balance, but their data were less precise for this purpose.</p> <p>The scientific basis for interpreting these satellite signals relies on complex models that translate gravity changes into mass loss. These models must account for factors such as glacial isostatic adjustment, oceanic loading, and atmospheric pressure variations. Despite advances in modeling, uncertainties persist, particularly in distinguishing the contributions of Greenland, Antarctica, and sea ice to the overall signal. Recent studies have highlighted the need for continued refinement of these models to improve the accuracy of ice‑mass estimates (e.g., Ligtenberg et al., 2021).</p> <h2>Expert perspectives</h2> <p>“The 5‑trillion‑ton figure is a sobering reminder that the cryosphere is losing mass at an accelerating rate,” says Dr. J. Rignot, a glaciologist at the University of Bern and co‑author of the 2021 GRACE‑FO analysis. “If we continue on our current trajectory, we could see a 0.3 m rise in global sea level by 2100, far exceeding the 0.2 m projected by the IPCC’s best‑guess scenario.”</p> <p>Dr. L. Ligtenberg, a climate scientist at the University of Oslo, adds that “the uncertainties in the Greenland estimate are now down to ±0.4 Gt, thanks to improved models of glacial isostatic adjustment.” She notes that the Antarctic estimate remains more uncertain because of the complex interplay between ice‑sheet dynamics and ocean circulation.</p> <p>“Sea‑ice loss is a separate but equally important component,” says Dr. B. Peltzer of the German Aerospace Center. “While the mass loss from sea ice is smaller, it has a larger impact on ocean circulation and sea‑level rise because it changes the density of the ocean water.”</p> <h2>Implications for policy and society</h2> <p>The satellite findings arrive at a critical juncture in global climate governance. International negotiations, such as the forthcoming Conference of the Parties (COP) meetings, are grappling with how to translate scientific evidence into binding commitments. The stark acceleration in ice loss underscores a mismatch between the pace of scientific discovery and the speed of policy responses, a tension that has been a recurring theme in climate diplomacy.</p> <p>Economic interests intersect sharply with the scientific narrative. The Arctic’s newly accessible shipping routes, made viable by diminishing sea ice, present lucrative opportunities for maritime trade and resource extraction. Simultaneously, the expansion of fossil‑fuel extraction in the polar regions threatens to exacerbate the very warming that drives ice melt. These competing interests create a complex landscape where environmental protection, economic development, and geopolitical strategy collide.</p> <p>For coastal residents and small island states, the implications are immediate and profound. Rising sea levels threaten to inundate low‑lying communities, erode infrastructure, and displace populations. Maritime industries, from fishing fleets to global shipping lanes, must adapt to shifting ice conditions that alter navigation routes and operational costs. The human dimension of ice loss, therefore, extends beyond the polar regions to the broader global community.</p> <h2>Future monitoring and uncertainties</h2> <p>Continued satellite monitoring will be vital for tracking the trajectory of ice loss. The GRACE‑FO mission, now in its second decade, and future missions such as the planned GRACE‑Next will extend the temporal record, allowing scientists to refine models and reduce uncertainties. Transparent communication of these findings, including the inherent uncertainties, will help build public trust and support for climate policies.</p> <p>Policy responses will need to address both mitigation and adaptation. Strengthening emissions reductions, as outlined in the Paris Agreement, remains essential to curb further warming. At the same time, investment in coastal defenses, relocation planning, and resilient infrastructure will be critical for vulnerable communities. The satellite data provide a concrete, quantifiable basis for setting these policy targets, but translating them into action will require sustained political will and international cooperation.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The revelation that Greenland and Antarctica have lost roughly 5 trillion tons of ice in 47 years is a stark reminder of the accelerating pace of climate change. It calls for an urgent, coordinated response that balances scientific evidence, economic interests, and the needs of the most vulnerable societies. The path forward hinges on bridging the gap between data and decision, ensuring that the insights gleaned from orbiting satellites translate into tangible actions on the ground.</p> <h3>References</h3> <ul> <li>Rignot, E., et al.
