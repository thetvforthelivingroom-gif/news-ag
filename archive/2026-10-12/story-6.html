SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-12
<h1>SpaceX’s Starship SN20 reaches orbit, but an engine anomaly forces an early abort</h1> <p>On the morning of October 23, SpaceX’s Starship SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica, Texas, and entered Earth orbit for the first time. Within twelve minutes, a sudden loss of engine control triggered an automated abort, destroying the vehicle in a controlled explosion. The event halted a historic launch and prompted SpaceX, NASA, and federal regulators to reassess the risks of pushing the limits of reusable launch vehicles.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>At 12:18 p.m. EDT, SN20 lifted from the launch pad with a 100‑kilogram test payload. The mission was scheduled to place the payload into a 400‑kilometer orbit with a 28‑degree inclination, demonstrating the vehicle’s ability to reach low Earth orbit (LEO) on a single launch.</p> <p>Less than a minute after liftoff, telemetry showed that the first stage’s cluster of Raptor engines lost control of thrust vectoring. The engines could no longer maintain the precise direction of thrust required for a stable ascent, causing the upper stage to burn propellant at a rate far above design limits. The rapid depletion of fuel triggered the vehicle’s automated abort sequence. At 12:30 p.m. EDT, the rocket was destroyed by a controlled explosion, 12 minutes into flight.</p> <p>The abort was captured on live video feeds and broadcast to the public. SpaceX’s social‑media accounts posted a brief statement that the launch had been aborted for safety reasons and that the company would conduct a thorough investigation. A spokesperson emphasized that the safety of the crew and the integrity of the launch site remained the top priority.</p> <h2>Background and context</h2> <p>Starship’s journey to orbit began in 2019 with a series of prototype tests. The first vehicle, SN1, performed a static‑fire test of the Raptor engine. Subsequent prototypes—SN2 through SN9—were progressively larger and more complex, each incorporating lessons from the previous iteration. The first orbital attempt, SN8, occurred in December 2022 and ended in a hard landing due to a failure in the landing legs. SN10, launched in March 2023, achieved a successful landing after a brief stint in orbit, but the vehicle was destroyed in a controlled explosion during a subsequent test.</p> <p>SN20 represents a significant scaling up of the design. The vehicle stands 120 meters tall and can lift up to 100 tonnes to LEO, compared with the Falcon Heavy’s 64 tonnes. The new prototype incorporates a redesigned heat shield, upgraded avionics, and a new “Super Heavy” booster that uses 33 Raptor engines to provide the thrust needed to escape Earth’s gravity.</p> <p>SpaceX’s rapid development cadence is a hallmark of its approach. The company typically moves from one prototype to the next within months, a pace that has drawn both admiration and criticism. Critics argue that the schedule may outpace the thorough testing required for a vehicle that will carry humans and cargo to the Moon and Mars. Supporters counter that incremental risk is built into each test and that failures are part of a learning process that ultimately leads to a more robust system.</p> <p>Regulatory oversight has been a complex factor in Starship’s development. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) granted SpaceX a launch license for the Boca Chica site, but the license requires compliance with strict safety standards, including detailed risk assessments and environmental impact studies. The U.S. Space Force has also been involved in safety oversight, reviewing launch procedures and ensuring that the vehicle meets national security requirements. NASA has entered into a contract with SpaceX to use Starship as a lunar lander and cargo vehicle for the Artemis program, a partnership that carries its own set of contractual risk‑sharing agreements.</p> <h2>The bigger picture: diverse perspectives</h2> <h3>NASA and the Artemis program</h3> <p>NASA’s Artemis program aims to return humans to the Moon by 2025 and establish a sustainable presence by the end of the decade. Starship is slated to serve as the lunar lander that will ferry astronauts from lunar orbit to the surface, as well as a cargo vehicle that will deliver supplies and equipment. The first orbital flight of Starship was therefore seen as a critical milestone for the Artemis schedule.</p> <p>The abort has delayed the Artemis timeline, pushing back the first crewed lunar landing by several months. NASA officials have stated that the agency will reassess the risk profile of the Starship lander and may consider alternative lander options if the problem persists. The delay also has implications for funding allocations, as NASA’s budget for the Artemis program is tied to milestone achievements.</p> <h3>Investor confidence and commercial expectations</h3> <p>SpaceX’s commercial launch market is highly competitive, with companies such as United Launch Alliance, Arianespace, and Blue Origin vying for customers. Starship’s promise of low launch costs—potentially as low as $2 million per launch—has attracted significant investor interest. The early abort, however, has raised questions about the reliability of the vehicle and the timeline for achieving commercial viability.</p> <p>Investors have responded with a mix of caution and optimism. While some analysts have expressed concerns about the impact on SpaceX’s valuation, others point out that the company’s long‑term vision for Mars colonization and its existing revenue streams from satellite launches and Starlink broadband services mitigate short‑term setbacks.</p> <h3>Regulatory and safety considerations</h3> <p>The FAA and Space Force’s oversight has intensified in the wake of the abort. The FAA has requested a detailed safety analysis and has temporarily paused new launch windows until the investigation is complete. The Space Force has issued a memorandum outlining additional safety protocols for future Starship flights, including stricter engine control verification and enhanced telemetry monitoring.</p> <p>NASA’s contractual risk‑sharing agreement with SpaceX includes clauses that require the company to meet specific safety milestones before receiving funding. The abort has triggered a review of these clauses, and NASA may seek to renegotiate terms to ensure that future flights meet the agency’s safety standards.</p> <h3>Technical analysis</h3> <p>The anomaly that caused the abort was traced to an engine control issue in the first stage’s Raptor cluster. The Raptor engine uses a full‑flow staged combustion cycle, a highly efficient but complex propulsion system. A failure in the engine control system can lead to a loss of thrust vectoring, which in turn causes the vehicle to deviate from its planned trajectory. In this case, the loss of control prompted an emergency burn that depleted the upper stage’s propellant reserves faster than anticipated.</p> <p>Engineers at SpaceX have indicated that the company will conduct a root‑cause analysis and implement design changes to the engine control software. The company has also announced plans to test a new version of the Raptor engine in a ground test before the next flight attempt.</p> <h2>What to watch: future implications</h2> <h3>Upcoming flight attempts</h3> <p>SpaceX has scheduled a series of test flights for the remainder of the year, with the next attempt expected in the third quarter. The company has stated that it will incorporate the lessons learned from the abort into the next prototype, which may involve a revised engine control architecture and additional redundancy in the upper stage’s propulsion system.</p> <h3>Impact on the U.S. space launch market</h3> <p>The Starship abort has highlighted the competitive pressures in the launch market. While Falcon Heavy and other heavy‑lift vehicles continue to dominate the high‑payload segment, the promise of Starship’s low cost per launch could reshape the market if the vehicle proves reliable. The delay may give competitors an opportunity to capture market share, but it also underscores the importance of safety and reliability in securing commercial contracts.</p> <h3>National security and policy considerations</h3> <p>The U.S. Space Force’s involvement in Starship’s safety oversight reflects a broader trend of increasing military interest in commercial launch capabilities. The agency’s focus on ensuring that commercial vehicles meet national security requirements could influence future policy decisions regarding the use of commercial launch services for defense missions.</p> <h3>NASA’s Artemis strategy</h3> <p>NASA may reassess its reliance on Starship for the Artemis program, potentially exploring alternative lander options such as the Lunar Gateway or other commercial partners. The agency’s risk‑management framework may be updated to incorporate a more diversified portfolio of lander technologies, reducing dependence on a single vehicle.</p> <h3>Investor sentiment and market dynamics</h3> <p>Investors will closely monitor SpaceX’s next flight attempts and the company’s ability to meet its commercial launch commitments. A successful orbital flight could restore confidence and drive up the company’s valuation, while repeated failures could erode investor trust. The broader market for reusable launch vehicles may also be influenced by SpaceX’s performance, affecting the valuation of competing companies.</p> <h3>Technological innovation</h3> <p>The Starship program continues to push the boundaries of propulsion, materials science, and autonomous flight control. The lessons from the abort will likely inform future design iterations, potentially leading to more robust engine control systems and improved propellant management. These innovations could have spillover benefits for other spaceflight projects, both commercial and governmental.</p> <p>In sum, the first orbital flight of SpaceX’s Starship, though short‑lived, has set in motion a cascade of technical, regulatory, commercial, and policy responses that will shape the trajectory of U.S. spaceflight for years to come. The event underscores the delicate balance between rapid innovation and the rigorous safety standards that underpin human and cargo missions beyond Earth.</p>
