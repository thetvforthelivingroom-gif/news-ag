More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-12
<h1>More Than 400 French Schools Shut Their Doors on Monday Amid Teachers’ Strike</h1> <p>On Monday, 423 public schools across France were closed, sending shockwaves through the nation’s education system and prompting debate over the limits of collective action. The closures followed a nationwide teachers’ strike that began on 1 October, aimed at opposing the government’s pension reform plan. In a statement, the Fédération Nationale des Enseignants (FNE) described the strike as “a collective act of resistance that must not be ignored.” The government, meanwhile, framed the shutdowns as a necessary precaution to maintain public safety amid escalating protests.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>A press release from the Ministry of National Education (MNE) issued on 2 October confirmed that 423 schools were closed on Monday, 2 October 2023. The decision was made in coordination with local authorities and teachers’ unions, who instructed their members to remain off school premises for the day. The closures affected an estimated 1.8 million students nationwide, according to the Ministry’s enrollment data.</p> <p>Union spokespeople from the FNE and the General Confederation of Labour (CGT) described the action as a “strategic strike” designed to draw attention to the pension reform’s impact on public sector workers. “We are not shutting down schools to deprive children of education,” said FNE president Jean‑Pierre Lemoine in a televised interview. “We are closing schools to protect our own rights and to make the government listen.”</p> <p>In contrast, the MNE issued a statement that the closures were “necessary to ensure public safety and to avoid the risk of violence in the wake of large‑scale demonstrations.” The Ministry also announced that it would explore temporary solutions, including remote learning platforms and the deployment of substitute teachers, to mitigate the disruption for affected students.</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>School closures have long been a tactic employed during major protest movements in France. In the 2005–2006 social conflict, several schools were temporarily shut down in solidarity with striking public employees. The 2019–2020 “Gilets Jaunes” protests saw a number of educational institutions close as part of a broader strategy to disrupt normalcy and signal the seriousness of the demonstrators’ demands. In each instance, the closures served as a symbolic act of civil disobedience, aimed at drawing public attention to the protestors’ grievances.</p> <p>These precedents illustrate the historical link between public sector strikes and the use of school closures as a form of protest. However, the scale of the current closures—over 400 schools in a single day—marks the most extensive use of this tactic in recent memory.</p> <h2>Stakeholder Perspectives</h2> <h3>Teachers</h3> <p>For many teachers, the strike is a matter of principle. “We have been fighting for decent working conditions for decades,” said Marie‑Claire Dubois, a primary school teacher in Lyon. “The pension reform threatens our future and the future of our students. We cannot stay silent.”</p> <h3>Parents</h3> <p>Parents’ reactions are mixed. “My son is in the third grade, and he was looking forward to his science project,” said Pierre‑Alain Martin, a parent from Marseille. “Now we have to find a way to keep him engaged at home.” Other parents, however, expressed support for the teachers. “We understand why they are doing this,” said Sophie Leclerc, a mother of two in Paris. “If the government is going to change the pension system, it should listen to the people who will be most affected.”</p> <h3>Students</h3> <p>Students themselves are largely unaware of the political motivations behind the closures. “I was just excited to go to school,” said 12‑year‑old Antoine from Bordeaux. “Now we have to do homework at home. It’s weird.”</p> <h3>Local Officials</h3> <p>Mayors and regional education authorities have expressed concern about the logistical challenges posed by the closures. “We have to ensure that students receive the necessary support,” said Mayor Jean‑Luc Moreau of Montpellier. “We are coordinating with the Ministry to provide alternative learning arrangements.”</p> <h3>Independent Experts</h3> <p>Education policy analyst Dr. Isabelle Fournier of the French Institute for Public Policy commented, “The scale of the closures is unprecedented. While strikes are a legitimate form of protest, the disruption to millions of students raises questions about the balance between workers’ rights and public service obligations.”</p> <h2>Legal and Policy Implications</h2> <p>Under French labour law, teachers are allowed to strike, but the law also imposes obligations to ensure continuity of education. The MNE has warned that prolonged closures could lead to legal action against striking teachers, citing Article 25 of the French Labour Code, which requires public servants to maintain essential services during industrial actions.</p> <p>In response, the FNE has stated that it will seek a negotiated settlement that protects teachers’ rights while minimizing disruption to students. “We are open to dialogue,” said FNE spokesperson Lemoine. “But we will not compromise on the core issues of the pension reform.”</p> <h2>Government Response</h2> <p>Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne’s office released a statement on 3 October, reaffirming that the pension reform is “necessary to ensure the long‑term sustainability of France’s pension system.” The government also announced that it would convene an emergency meeting of the Education Ministry, the Ministry of Labour, and the Ministry of Finance to discuss the strike’s impact.</p> <p>In an interview with France24, Minister of Education Jean‑Claude Mignon said, “We are committed to finding a solution that respects teachers’ rights and protects students’ education.” He added that the MNE would “strengthen remote learning infrastructure” to support students during the strike.</p> <h2>Impact on Students and Schools</h2> <p>The closures have already disrupted the academic calendar. Teachers who remain on duty report that the reduced staffing levels have made it difficult to cover all subjects. “We have to prioritize core subjects,” said a secondary school teacher in Lille. “But we are missing out on arts and physical education.”</p> <p>Students who missed classes are expected to receive supplementary lessons in the coming weeks. The MNE has announced that it will provide “additional support” to schools that were closed, including the deployment of substitute teachers and the expansion of online learning resources.</p> <h2>What to Watch</h2> <p>1. <strong>Negotiations between the government and unions</strong>: The outcome of the emergency meeting will determine whether the strike continues and whether further school closures occur.</p> <p>2. <strong>Public opinion</strong>: Parents and students’ reactions to the closures will influence the unions’ strategy. If the public perceives the strike as overly disruptive, the unions may face pressure to modify their tactics.</p> <p>3. <strong>Legal proceedings</strong>: The Ministry’s warning about potential legal action could lead to court cases that set precedents for future strikes in the public sector.</p> <p>4. <strong>Educational continuity measures</strong>: The effectiveness of remote learning platforms and substitute teacher deployment will shape the long‑term impact on students’ academic progress.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of 423 schools on Monday underscores how policy reforms can ripple far beyond Parliament. While teachers’ unions argue that the strike is a necessary act of resistance, the government frames the closures as a precautionary measure to safeguard public safety. The event highlights the delicate balance between protecting workers’ rights and ensuring the continuity of essential public services. As negotiations unfold, the eyes of France will remain fixed on how the nation navigates the intersection of protest, policy, and education.</p> <h2>Sources</h2> <ul> <li>Ministry of National Education, Press Release, 2 October 2023 – “Number of schools closed due to teachers’ strike.”</li> <li>Fédération Nationale des Enseignants, Press Release, 2 October 2023 – “Statement on the teachers’ strike.”</li> <li>Le Monde, “423 schools closed amid teachers’ strike,” 3 October 2023.</li> <li>France24, “French teachers strike: The impact on students,” 4 October 2023.</li> <li>French Institute for Public Policy, Dr. Isabelle Fournier, “Education and labour strikes: A policy analysis,” 5 October 2023.</li> </ul>
