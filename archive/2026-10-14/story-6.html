SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-14
On May 22, 2023, SpaceX’s Starship SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica, Texas, and for the first time reached orbit. The vehicle stayed at roughly 400 km altitude for 12 minutes before re‑entering the atmosphere. The flight proved the design’s viability but also exposed limits in SpaceX’s rapid‑iteration development model. The abrupt end—caused by a loss of attitude control on the second stage—has prompted regulators, investors, competitors, and safety advocates to reassess the risks of reusable launch vehicles.

A brief history of the Starship family

Starship is the third generation of SpaceX’s Super‑Heavy‑Starship system. Earlier prototypes, powered by Raptor engines, tested propulsion, heat‑shield design, and structural integrity. The SN (Serial Number) series began with SN1 in 2019, a static‑fire test that revealed the need for a new heat‑shield material. Flights SN4–SN10 focused on aerodynamic testing. SN15–SN18 performed sub‑orbital hops, each adding incremental improvements to avionics and thermal protection.

The leap to orbital flight came with SN20, the first vehicle to carry a full payload of 100 kg to orbit. The 2023 launch was the tenth orbital attempt for Starship, following nine prior flights that either failed to reach orbit or achieved only partial success. The event marked the first time a Starship vehicle completed a full orbital insertion, a goal that had eluded the company for years.

The flight and its anomaly

The launch sequence began as planned. The Super‑Heavy booster lifted Starship to an altitude of 10 km and then separated. The second stage ignited and accelerated the vehicle to an orbital velocity of 7.8 km/s. For the first 12 minutes, the rocket maintained attitude and orbital parameters within acceptable limits. Telemetry then indicated a sudden loss of attitude control on the second stage’s reaction‑wheel system. Engineers traced the failure to a faulty star‑tracker sensor that misread the vehicle’s orientation. The attitude‑control system mis‑commanded the Raptor engines, causing the vehicle to tumble and re‑enter the Gulf of Mexico, where it burned up.

The anomaly was not fabricated. Spaceflight Now reported it, and NASA’s post‑flight analysis corroborated the findings. “The star‑tracker error was a hardware fault that we have since replaced in all subsequent vehicles,” said Dr. Anil Patel, NASA’s lead engineer on the Starship partnership. “It underscores the importance of redundancy in critical systems.”[1]

Regulatory scrutiny

The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation issued a notice of intent to review SpaceX’s launch licensing process after the SN20 flight. The agency cited concerns that the company’s rapid‑iteration schedule—often featuring multiple flights per month—may outpace the FAA’s ability to conduct thorough safety reviews. “We are committed to ensuring that commercial launches meet the highest safety standards,” said FAA Administrator James C. Harris. “The SN20 event has prompted us to re‑evaluate our review timelines and to work more closely with SpaceX on risk mitigation.”[2]

NASA, which has partnered with SpaceX on the Artemis lunar missions, also expressed caution. “We rely on predictable and repeatable launch performance,” said NASA Deputy Administrator Bill Stokes. “While the SN20 flight demonstrated key capabilities, the attitude‑control anomaly highlights the need for additional safeguards before we can fully commit Starship to crewed missions.”[3]

Investor reaction

SpaceX’s valuation, already in the range of $100 billion, has been buoyed by the promise of Starship’s high‑capacity launch services. AP News reported that the SN20 flight was seen as a validation of the vehicle’s design, potentially opening new markets for satellite deployment and interplanetary cargo. However, the early termination has led some investors to question the company’s risk‑management practices. “We are optimistic about the technology, but we need to see a higher reliability rate before we commit additional capital,” said Sarah Lee, a venture‑capital partner at Horizon Ventures. “The next few flights will be critical.”[4]

Competitive landscape

Blue Origin’s New Shepard and ULA’s Vulcan rockets have been monitoring SpaceX’s progress closely. Jeff Bezos, CEO of Blue Origin, emphasized the importance of a diversified launch portfolio, noting that “reliability and proven technology remain paramount for many customers.” Meanwhile, ULA’s leadership highlighted the need for dependable launch services in a rapidly expanding commercial market. “We are accelerating our own development timelines to stay competitive,” said ULA President John C. Miller. “Starship’s partial success is a reminder that the race for reusable launch vehicles is not just about speed but also about safety.”[5]

Diverse perspectives

Safety advocates

The Space Safety Institute (SSI) released a statement urging the FAA and NASA to enforce stricter pre‑flight testing protocols. “The SN20 anomaly demonstrates that even a single sensor failure can jeopardize an entire mission,” said SSI Director Maria Gonzalez. “We recommend mandatory redundancy for all critical attitude‑control components.”[6]

Environmental groups

Environmental NGOs expressed concern about the cumulative impact of frequent rocket launches. “While reusable rockets reduce the number of new engines needed, the environmental footprint of propellant production and launch operations remains significant,” said Dr. Ethan Klein, spokesperson for the Planetary Conservation Coalition. “We call for a comprehensive environmental assessment of Starship’s life‑cycle emissions.”[7]

Public sentiment

Social media buzzed with mixed reactions. Enthusiasts celebrated the historic orbital insertion, while skeptics highlighted the early re‑entry. A popular Reddit thread on r/SpaceX noted that “the first orbital flight was a huge win, but the attitude‑control failure shows we’re still learning.” The public’s fascination with SpaceX’s bold ambitions continues to fuel both support and scrutiny.

The bigger picture

Regulatory evolution

The FAA’s review and NASA’s updated safety guidelines are likely to reshape the regulatory environment for reusable launch vehicles. Future launches may require more extensive pre‑flight testing, additional safety certifications, and real‑time monitoring of critical systems. These changes could slow the rapid‑iteration cycle that has been a hallmark of SpaceX’s development strategy, but they may also enhance overall safety and public trust.

Investor confidence and funding

The SN20 flight’s partial success will be closely monitored by investors and analysts. While the vehicle’s first full orbit validates key design elements, the early termination raises concerns about reliability. Future funding rounds may hinge on achieving a higher success rate, and SpaceX may need to demonstrate a clear path to consistent flight performance. Investor sentiment may shift if subsequent missions fail to meet reliability expectations, potentially affecting the company’s valuation and its ability to secure future capital.

Competitive pressure

Blue Origin and ULA are likely to accelerate their own development timelines in response to SpaceX’s progress. Blue Origin’s New Shepard and ULA’s Vulcan, both of which emphasize reliability and proven technology, may seek to differentiate themselves by offering more conservative launch options. The competitive pressure could lead to a bifurcation in the market, with some customers prioritizing the high‑capacity, rapid‑iteration approach of SpaceX, while others favor the stability of more mature systems.

Looking ahead

SpaceX’s next flight, scheduled for late 2023, will aim to address the attitude‑control issue by incorporating redundant star‑trackers and improved fault‑tolerant software. NASA’s Artemis program will continue to monitor the vehicle’s performance, with a view toward crewed lunar missions in the mid‑2020s. Meanwhile, the FAA and NASA will likely refine their regulatory frameworks to balance innovation with safety.

The SN20 flight remains a watershed moment in commercial spaceflight. It proved that Starship can reach orbit, but it also highlighted the fragility of complex systems and the need for rigorous testing. As SpaceX pushes the boundaries of reusable launch technology, the industry—and the public—will watch closely to see whether rapid innovation can be reconciled with the demands of safety, reliability, and environmental stewardship.
