More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-14
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut their doors, affecting roughly 200,000 students and 10,000 teachers. The closures are part of a nationwide strike over a pension reform that raises the retirement age from 62 to 64 and trims benefits for certain workers. The move turns classrooms into a battleground between the state and its most vocal workers, while families scramble for childcare and teachers face uncertainty about pay and safety.

The strike and the school shutdowns

The French Ministry of Education announced the closures in response to the protests that have already seen police block traffic, shut down public transport, and disrupted daily life nationwide. While the ministry has not confirmed an exact number, estimates place the figure at around 400 schools, spanning the entire country—from Paris to rural Brittany—and covering primary, middle, and high schools. Parents report that many affected schools are in densely populated urban areas where alternative childcare options are scarce, forcing last‑minute arrangements or reliance on family members who may themselves be working. Police presence at school entrances has been tense, with officers monitoring crowds to prevent protests from spilling into the school grounds.

Unions have long used school closures as a bargaining tool. Historically, strikes in 1968, 2010, and 2019 saw similar tactics. In each case, the closures were designed to create a tangible impact on everyday life, thereby increasing public pressure on policymakers. The current wave of strikes echoes that legacy, with unions arguing that the pension reform threatens the future of public‑sector workers and that the closures are a necessary escalation to bring the government to the negotiating table.

Legal and practical fallout

Legal experts question the legitimacy of shutting down schools as a form of protest. While the French constitution guarantees the right to education, the law does not explicitly authorize the closure of public schools in response to civil unrest. Some scholars argue that the closures violate the principle of “education as a public good,” while others contend that the state can temporarily suspend school operations if deemed necessary for safety. The Ministry of Education has not yet issued a formal statement clarifying the legal basis for the closures, leaving teachers and parents in uncertainty.

The immediate practical fallout is already being felt. Many parents have had to arrange overnight childcare or have missed work because of the sudden closures, creating ripple effects on local businesses and the economy. Teachers, many of whom are union members, are uncertain about their pay and job security, as the closures mean they will not be working on the scheduled days. Some schools report that the police presence has made it difficult for staff to enter or exit the premises safely, raising concerns about the safety of both teachers and students. The closures also risk delaying the academic calendar, potentially pushing back exams and graduation ceremonies, with long‑term implications for students’ academic trajectories.

The pension reform at the heart of the protests

The reform is a sweeping overhaul of France’s public pension system. The government’s proposal raises the retirement age from 62 to 64 and introduces new rules that would reduce pension benefits for certain groups of workers. The reforms are justified on the basis of demographic changes and fiscal sustainability. According to the Ministry of Finance, the aging population and the rising number of retirees relative to workers threaten the solvency of the pension system, and the reforms are necessary to ensure its long‑term viability. Critics argue that the reforms disproportionately affect low‑income workers and undermine the social contract that has been a cornerstone of French society for decades.

Unions such as the National Confederation of Labour (CFDT), the General Confederation of Labour (CGT), and the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) have all called for a halt to the pension reforms, citing concerns about workers’ rights and the social safety net. In addition to school closures, unions have organized large‑scale strikes that have shut down public transport, blocked major highways, and disrupted the operations of national institutions such as the French National Assembly and the European Parliament. Their strategy is to create a crisis that forces the government to negotiate, drawing on France’s history of using public‑sector strikes as a means of political leverage.

The broader context

France is preparing for the next presidential election, and the government’s handling of the pension reforms could have significant electoral ramifications. Public opinion is divided: a segment supports the reforms as necessary for fiscal responsibility, while a larger portion is frustrated by the protests’ disruptive tactics. The closures of schools add a new dimension to the debate, as families confront the immediate consequences of the policy dispute. The unions’ use of schools as a bargaining chip amplifies the stakes, turning a policy debate into a daily reality for millions of French citizens.

Voices on the ground

From the perspective of teachers, the closures are a source of both frustration and solidarity. Many teachers express concern about their pay, job security, and the safety of their students. Some report that police presence at school entrances has made it difficult for staff to enter or exit the premises safely. Others see the closures as an opportunity to rally support for their cause, using the disruption to draw attention to the broader issues of pension reform and workers’ rights.

Parents grapple with the practical implications of the closures. Many must find alternative childcare arrangements, often at a high cost or with limited availability. The closures have highlighted the fragility of the childcare system in France, as many families rely on public schools for after‑school care. The resulting financial strain and logistical challenges underscore the broader impact of the protests on everyday life.

What to watch

The school closures are likely to have a lasting impact on the French education system and the broader political landscape. Immediate fallout includes childcare gaps, potential delays to the academic calendar, and safety concerns for staff amid police presence. The unions’ strategy of using school closures as a bargaining tool could influence future labor disputes, setting a precedent for how public‑sector workers negotiate with the government. The political stakes are high, as the government’s handling of the pension reforms could affect its standing ahead of the next presidential election.

In the weeks ahead, the government will need to address the legal debate surrounding the closures and provide clear communication to parents, teachers, and the public. The unions will likely continue to press for a halt to the pension reforms, using the closures as a means of drawing attention to their cause. The French public will be watching closely to see how the government balances the need for fiscal responsibility with the practical realities of maintaining public services during a period of intense dissent. The outcome of this crisis will shape the future of France’s pension system, its public‑sector labor relations, and the resilience of its education system in the face of political turmoil.
