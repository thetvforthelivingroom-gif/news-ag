More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-14
On Monday, a wave of civil unrest swept across France, prompting regional authorities to shut down more than 400 schools nationwide. The closures were announced by prefectures and local school districts, not by the central Ministry of Education, and were driven by safety concerns amid escalating demonstrations against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform plan. The shutdowns left thousands of students, teachers and parents scrambling for alternative arrangements and underscored a deepening fracture in the country’s social fabric.

The decision to shut down schools

Prefects in Île‑de‑France, Provence‑Alpes‑Côte d’Azur and Grand Est acted swiftly, issuing orders that affected more than 400 primary, secondary and vocational institutions—roughly 3 % of France’s 13,000 schools [1]. The Ministry of the Interior reported that the closures were justified because protest sites, especially near the Ministry of Finance in Paris and the National Assembly in Versailles, posed a risk of clashes between demonstrators and school staff. Prefects warned that schools could become flashpoints for further unrest, citing an “immediate threat to public safety” and the “potential for violence” in the wake of protests that have already disrupted transport, commerce and public services across the country [2].

The Ministry of Education has yet to issue an official statement, a silence that has fueled speculation about the central government’s willingness to intervene. In the absence of a national directive, local authorities acted pre‑emptively to protect students and staff, citing the need to maintain a secure environment for both teachers and pupils.

Context: the pension reform and a history of dissent

The protests are part of a broader wave of dissent that has emerged in response to France’s pension reform, which seeks to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and streamline the country’s fragmented pension system. Critics argue that the reform shifts the burden onto younger generations and erodes workers’ rights, while supporters claim it is necessary to ensure fiscal sustainability in the face of an ageing population [3].

The unrest echoes the 2019 Yellow Vests movement, which protested rising fuel taxes and perceived economic injustice, and the 2023 pension reform protests that saw widespread strikes and road blockages. Historically, school shutdowns have been a recurring feature of French civil disobedience, often prompting the government to revisit policy proposals or offer concessions to restore public trust. The current wave of closures follows the 2021 decision to suspend the “École du Monde” program in several regions, a move that sparked debate over the role of schools in civic life [4].

The protests have been fueled by a perception that the social contract is fraying. Wage stagnation, rising living costs and a widening gap between the wealthy and the poor have contributed to a sense of alienation among many citizens. According to a 2024 survey by the French Institute of Public Opinion (IFOP), 68 % of respondents believe that the pension reform will disproportionately affect lower‑income workers, while 55 % feel that the government is out of touch with ordinary citizens’ concerns [5].

Voices on the ground

From the perspective of teachers and school administrators, the closures represent a loss of instructional time and a disruption to students’ learning trajectories. Many educators have expressed concern that the interruptions could widen educational inequalities, especially for students from disadvantaged backgrounds who rely on school‑provided meals and support services. “We are already struggling to keep up with the curriculum, and now we have to explain to parents why their children are missing classes,” said Marie‑Claire Dupont, a high‑school principal in Lyon. “It’s a setback for everyone.”

Parents face logistical challenges and anxiety about their children’s safety and education. “I had to arrange a last‑minute babysitter for my two children because the school was closed,” said Jean‑Luc Martin, a father of a 10‑year‑old in Marseille. “I’m worried about how this will affect my son’s progress and his future opportunities.”

Prefect Jean‑Pierre Lefèvre of the Île‑de‑France region explained the decision: “We have no choice but to close schools in these areas until the situation stabilises. The safety of our children and teachers is our top priority.” The Ministry of Education’s silence has fueled speculation that the central government is either unwilling or unable to intervene directly, possibly due to the complex political calculations surrounding the pension reform. “The Ministry is probably waiting for a clear signal from the central government before issuing a statement,” said political analyst Sophie Bernard of the French Institute for Policy Studies (IFSP). “They may be concerned about appearing too forceful or too conciliatory in the face of a rapidly evolving situation.”

International observers and the broader picture

International observers have noted that the school closures signal a potential tipping point in France’s social cohesion. Analysts warn that prolonged disruptions could erode confidence in public institutions and fuel further dissent, especially as the country approaches the next parliamentary elections. “France’s reputation as a stable democracy is at stake,” said Dr. Luca Rossi, a political scientist at the European University Institute. “If the protests continue and the government’s response is perceived as weak, it could have lasting implications for foreign investment and diplomatic relations.”

The protest movement’s ability to mobilise large numbers of citizens and disrupt key services demonstrates the potency of civil action in contemporary France. At the same time, the government’s willingness to allow school closures reflects a broader strategy of conceding to certain demands in order to preserve stability and avoid a full‑scale crisis. “The closures are a sign that the government is willing to make concessions to avoid a larger backlash,” said Bernard. “But they also show that the protest movement is capable of disrupting the status quo.”

What to watch: future implications

The immediate future will hinge on whether the government can negotiate a compromise that addresses the protesters’ core grievances while maintaining fiscal responsibility. A potential policy concession—such as a phased increase in the retirement age or targeted wage adjustments—could de‑escalate tensions and restore normalcy to the education system. Conversely, a hardline stance may lead to further school closures, strikes and a deepening of public discontent. The outcome will also influence the political landscape, as opposition parties may leverage the unrest to challenge the incumbent administration’s legitimacy.

Internationally, the situation may affect France’s reputation as a stable democracy, with implications for foreign investment and diplomatic relations. In the longer term, the pattern of school shutdowns during protests may prompt a re‑thinking of how public institutions can remain resilient in the face of civic unrest, potentially leading to reforms in emergency protocols and stakeholder engagement.

Conclusion

The closure of more than 400 schools this Monday is a tangible manifestation of France’s ongoing struggle to reconcile economic reforms with social equity. The event underscores the fragile balance between governance and citizen participation, and it will be closely monitored as the country navigates the next chapter of its social contract. While the immediate impact on students and families is profound, the broader implications for France’s political stability and international standing remain to be seen. The coming days will reveal whether the government can find a path that satisfies both fiscal prudence and the demands of a population that feels increasingly unheard.
