More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-14
<article> On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut their doors as protests over the government’s pension reform reach a new crescendo, forcing students, teachers and parents into an uncertain educational future while the state grapples with the balance between public safety and the right to learn. The closures, announced by the Ministry of Education, come amid a wave of demonstrations that have already disrupted transportation, public services and the economy, and raise questions about how civil disobedience can reshape public institutions.

What happened

The French Ministry of Education declared that a total of 400 to 500 schools would be closed on Monday, a figure that varies across reports but is consistently above 400 [2][3]. The decision was justified on “safety grounds” as the protests spread to cities such as Paris, Lyon, Marseille and Bordeaux, where demonstrators have blocked streets, set up barricades and occupied public spaces. The closures are not limited to primary or secondary schools; some higher‑education institutions and vocational training centers are also affected. While the Ministry has not yet issued a detailed reopening plan, it has urged parents to seek alternative arrangements and to stay informed through official channels.

Background and context

The unrest stems from President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform, which proposes raising the retirement age from 62 to 64, merging the 42 existing pension schemes into a single system, and imposing a points‑based system for calculating benefits. The reform, which was passed in the National Assembly in 2023, has been criticized by trade unions, political parties and civil society groups for eroding workers’ rights and favoring the wealthy. The opposition argues that the changes will disproportionately affect lower‑paid workers, public servants and those in physically demanding jobs, while the reform proponents claim it is necessary to ensure the long‑term sustainability of the pension system.

The protests began in late 2022 and have escalated into a nationwide movement that includes strikes, road blockades, and school closures. The government’s strategy has been to maintain the reform while attempting to quell violence and restore public order. The Ministry of Education’s decision to close schools is part of a broader pattern of state measures to control the protest environment, which also includes police presence and the use of tear gas in some areas.

Historically, France has a tradition of student activism and school closures as a form of protest. The 1968 student uprisings, which culminated in the May 1968 protests, saw widespread school and university shutdowns that played a pivotal role in shaping the country’s political landscape. The current wave of closures echoes that legacy, underscoring the enduring power of educational institutions as sites of dissent.

The bigger picture

Students and parents

For students, the closures mean a sudden disruption to their learning routine, a loss of access to teachers, and a potential setback in exam preparation. Parents express concern about the safety of their children, especially in areas where protests have turned violent. Some families are scrambling to find alternative childcare or to arrange for home‑based learning, while others are worried about the long‑term impact on academic progress.

Teachers and administrators

Teachers and school administrators face a complex dilemma: balancing professional obligations with personal safety. Many educators have voiced uncertainty about how to manage lesson plans, assessments, and student welfare when schools are closed. Some have reported that the Ministry has not provided clear guidance on how to conduct remote teaching or how to support students who may lack access to technology at home. The uncertainty has also sparked discussions within teachers’ unions about collective bargaining and the need for protective measures for staff.

Protestors

For protestors, school closures are a strategic tool to amplify their grievances. By targeting educational institutions, they aim to disrupt the daily life of the nation and draw attention to the perceived injustices of the pension reform. Protestors argue that the closures serve as a symbolic statement against the state’s policies and as a means to pressure the government into revisiting the reform.

Government and Ministry of Education

The government’s justification for the closures centers on safety concerns. The Ministry of Education has stated that the decision is “necessary to protect students and staff” in the face of escalating violence and that it will “reopen schools as soon as it is safe” [2]. However, the Ministry has not yet provided a detailed timeline or criteria for reopening, leading to criticism that the decision is opaque and lacks transparency. The lack of a clear policy on reopening has also raised questions about how the state will manage the educational continuity of students in the long term.

Public perception

The closures have sparked a debate about the legitimacy of state authority versus the right to protest. Some citizens view the government’s actions as a necessary measure to maintain order, while others see it as an overreach that infringes on the fundamental right to education. The situation has also prompted discussions about the role of civil disobedience in a democratic society and how it can influence policy change.

What to watch

Potential for further closures

The current wave of closures may be the beginning of a prolonged period of disruption. If protests intensify or spread to additional regions, the Ministry may decide to close more schools or extend the closures beyond Monday. The possibility of a sustained shutdown raises concerns about the cumulative impact on students’ academic performance and mental health.
