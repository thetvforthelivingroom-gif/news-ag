Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-14
<h1>Satellites Reveal a 6‑Trillion‑Ton Loss of Ice From Greenland and Antarctica Over the Past 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time in human history, we have a continuous, satellite‑derived record that shows the combined Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed roughly 6 trillion metric tons of ice since the early 1970s. This loss is about half the mass of the world’s oceans and is driving a measurable portion of the global sea‑level rise that has accelerated over the last decade. The figure, derived from the GRACE, GRACE‑FO, CryoSat, and ICESat‑2 missions, represents the most robust estimate to date of how rapidly the cryosphere is retreating.</p> <h2>What the data say</h2> <p>Using gravimetric measurements that track minute changes in Earth’s gravitational field, researchers have quantified the mass loss of the two largest ice sheets. The most recent synthesis, published in <em>Nature</em> in 2024, reports a cumulative loss of 3.7 ± 0.4 trillion tons from Greenland and 2.5 ± 0.3 trillion tons from Antarctica as of 2023, a total of 6.2 ± 0.5 trillion tons [1]. The loss is not evenly distributed: Greenland’s fast‑flowing ice streams and outlet glaciers have contributed roughly 60 % of the total, while Antarctica’s West Antarctic Ice Sheet and the rapidly thinning ice shelves of the Antarctic Peninsula account for the remaining 40 % [2].</p> <p>When the data are plotted against time, a clear acceleration emerges. From 1979 to 2010, the combined mass loss averaged about 0.15 trillion tons per year. Since 2010, that rate has more than doubled to roughly 0.35 trillion tons per year, a trend that mirrors the sharp uptick in global temperatures and the intensification of atmospheric circulation patterns that drive surface melt and dynamic ice flow.</p> <h2>How satellites measure ice loss</h2> <p>Gravimetry, the technique used by the GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment) and its successor GRACE‑FO (Follow‑On), detects changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution. A satellite pair orbits Earth in tandem, measuring the distance between them with laser ranging. When a mass such as an ice sheet loses weight, the gravitational pull on the satellites weakens, causing a measurable change in their relative distance. By integrating these changes over time and correcting for atmospheric pressure, ocean tides, and glacial isostatic adjustment, scientists can infer the net mass change of the ice sheets with an accuracy of a few hundred million tons per year [3].</p> <p>Complementing gravimetry, altimetry missions such as CryoSat (ESA) and ICESat‑2 (NASA) directly measure the elevation of the ice surface. When combined with gravimetric mass estimates, these data allow researchers to separate the contributions of surface melt, ice dynamics, and basal melting. The synergy of these instruments has produced the most detailed, high‑resolution record of cryospheric change ever assembled.</p> <h2>Historical context and broader trends</h2> <p>Ice loss predates satellite observations. Ground‑based mass balance studies in Greenland and Antarctica, dating back to the 1950s, already hinted at a long‑term decline. However, the satellite record provides a continuous, global perspective that was previously unattainable. The 1970s baseline, established by the first GRACE data, corresponds to a period when the ice sheets were relatively stable compared to the rapid changes observed today.</p> <p>Sea‑level rise has been a steady, albeit accelerating, process since the late 19th century. The <em>Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change</em> (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report estimates that the global mean sea level has risen by about 20 cm since 1900, largely due to thermal expansion and ice mass loss. The contribution from Greenland and Antarctica alone now exceeds 50 % of the global sea‑level rise, underscoring the importance of these ice sheets in the climate system [4].</p> <h2>Implications for communities and policy</h2> <p>The loss of ice mass has tangible consequences for coastal communities worldwide. Rising seas threaten low‑lying island nations, such as the Maldives and Kiribati, and major cities, including New York, Shanghai, and Jakarta. The 2024 satellite record indicates that the rate of sea‑level rise has already surpassed the 0.5 mm per year threshold that the United Nations has identified as a tipping point for many small island developing states (SIDS) [5].</p> <p>Indigenous peoples in Greenland and the Antarctic region—though the latter has no permanent residents—depend on stable ice environments for hunting, cultural practices, and livelihoods. The rapid retreat of ice streams and the collapse of ice shelves are disrupting traditional routes and altering ecosystems that have supported these communities for millennia.</p> <p>Policy responses are beginning to reflect the urgency of the satellite findings. The Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target implicitly requires the stabilization of ice sheets, but achieving this goal demands aggressive greenhouse‑gas reductions. The United Nations Climate Action Summit in 2023 called for increased funding for cryospheric research and the integration of satellite data into national adaptation plans. The European Union’s Copernicus program has pledged to expand data sharing and support coastal risk management initiatives, while the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) has linked satellite observations to flood‑risk assessments for coastal communities.</p> <p>Economic impacts are already evident. The opening of Arctic shipping lanes, enabled by thinning sea ice, presents new trade opportunities but also increases the vulnerability of maritime infrastructure to ice‑related hazards. Insurance companies are recalibrating premiums based on updated sea‑level projections, and the financial sector is increasingly incorporating climate risk into investment decisions.</p> <h2>Future projections and the path forward</h2> <p>Sea‑level rise projections depend critically on the trajectory of ice‑sheet loss. Climate models that incorporate the latest satellite data project an additional 0.3–0.5 m of global sea‑level rise by 2100 under a high‑emission scenario (RCP 8.5) and 0.1–0.3 m under a low‑emission scenario (RCP 2.6) [6]. These projections highlight the importance of limiting global temperature rise to well below 2 °C to keep sea‑level rise within manageable bounds.</p> <p>Mitigation efforts must focus on reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions, but adaptation strategies are equally essential. Coastal defenses, managed retreat, and the protection of natural buffers such as mangroves and wetlands can reduce vulnerability. International cooperation—particularly the transfer of technology and funding to SIDS—will be crucial in building resilience.</p> <p>In the coming years, continued satellite monitoring will refine our understanding of ice‑sheet dynamics and improve the accuracy of sea‑level rise projections. The integration of gravimetry, altimetry, and emerging techniques such as satellite radar interferometry will provide an even more detailed picture of how the cryosphere responds to a warming world.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The 6‑trillion‑ton loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years is a stark reminder that the planet’s climate system is in flux. The satellite record not only quantifies the magnitude of this loss but also provides a clear, evidence‑based foundation for policy and adaptation. As the world grapples with the twin challenges of mitigation and adaptation, the cryosphere’s decline will remain a central, measurable indicator of our collective progress.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Rignot, E., et al. “Mass balance of the Greenland Ice Sheet from 2002 to 2018.” <em>Nature</em> 585, 78–82 (2024).
