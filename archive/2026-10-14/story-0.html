Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-14
Satellite measurements now show that the planet has shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica in the last 47 years—a loss that could lift global sea levels by roughly three centimetres. This figure, drawn from a decade‑long series of gravity‑based and laser‑altimetry observations, is the most comprehensive, satellite‑verified estimate of polar ice loss to date. It provides policymakers with a concrete metric to gauge the pace of change and the urgency of mitigation efforts.

The science behind the numbers

The 12‑trillion‑ton loss is the cumulative result of mass changes measured by the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO, as well as laser‑altimetry missions CryoSat and ICESat‑2. These satellites track variations in Earth’s gravitational field and surface elevation, allowing scientists to calculate how much ice has melted or been displaced over time [T2]. Since GRACE’s launch in 2002, the precision of these measurements has improved, giving a clearer picture of ice dynamics across the planet’s two largest ice sheets [T2].

The loss is split between Greenland and Antarctica, with Greenland contributing about 8 trillion tons and Antarctica about 4 trillion tons over the 47‑year period. The figures represent both surface melt and ice‑sheet flow into the ocean, as the satellites capture changes in mass balance rather than just surface water. The data indicate that the rate of loss has accelerated in recent decades, with the most rapid periods occurring in the 2010s and early 2020s [T2].

Translating 12 trillion tons into sea‑level rise gives roughly 3 centimetres worldwide. This estimate comes from dividing the total volume of ice lost (converted from mass using ice density) by the surface area of the world’s oceans. The figure aligns closely with the upper end of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) projections for the 21st century, underscoring the significance of the satellite record [T2].

How the satellites work together

GRACE, launched in 2002, was the first mission to map Earth’s gravity field with the precision needed to detect subtle changes in ice mass. Its twin, GRACE‑FO, continued the mission after the original satellites ran out of fuel in 2017, ensuring continuity of the data record [T2]. CryoSat, launched by the European Space Agency in 2010, uses radar altimetry to measure ice‑sheet elevation, while NASA’s ICESat‑2, launched in 2018, employs laser altimetry for higher‑resolution surface height data [T2].

These instruments complement one another: gravity measurements capture mass changes regardless of surface elevation, while altimetry provides direct surface height changes. Together, they allow scientists to separate the contributions of surface melt, ice‑sheet dynamics, and snow compaction to the overall mass loss. The combination has been essential for refining estimates of how rapidly the polar ice sheets are contributing to sea‑level rise [T2].

The 47‑year timespan covers the period from the early 1970s, when ice‑sheet observations were largely ground‑based, to the present, when satellite data dominate. Earlier estimates, based on limited field measurements and early satellite data, had suggested a lower total loss. The new satellite record reveals that the ice sheets have been losing mass more rapidly than previously thought, largely because of increased melt from warmer air and more dynamic ice‑sheet flow [T2].

Implications for people and policy

The satellite data have immediate implications for coastal communities, infrastructure, and global shipping. A 3‑centimetre rise in sea level may seem modest, but it translates into higher storm surges, increased erosion, and more frequent flooding in low‑lying areas. The data also affect the economics of coastal insurance and the planning of sea‑defence structures, as insurers and governments reassess risk profiles in light of the new mass‑loss figures [T3].

From a geopolitical standpoint, the loss of polar ice opens new shipping routes, such as the Northwest Passage and the Trans‑Arctic routes, potentially reshaping global trade patterns. It also raises the prospect of resource extraction in previously inaccessible areas, sparking discussions about governance, environmental protection, and indigenous rights. The satellite record provides a factual basis for these debates, ensuring that policy decisions are grounded in the most accurate scientific data available [T3].

Policy makers have responded to the new data by revisiting national pledges under the Paris Agreement. Several countries have updated their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) to reflect the accelerated ice loss, acknowledging that the current trajectory would exceed the 1.5 °C target if unmitigated. The satellite evidence is therefore a critical lever in the upcoming post‑COP negotiations, offering a concrete metric that can be used to hold governments accountable [T2].

However, uncertainties remain. Ice‑density assumptions, snow compaction rates, and the separation of dynamic flow from surface melt introduce error margins into the mass‑loss estimates. Regional variations also mean that global averages can mask significant differences between Greenland and Antarctica, or between different sectors of Antarctica itself. Scientists emphasize the need for higher‑resolution regional studies to refine the global picture and to inform local adaptation strategies [T2].

Looking ahead

Looking forward, the continuation of GRACE‑FO and the planned launch of its successor, GRACE‑FO‑2, will be crucial for maintaining a continuous record of ice mass changes. NASA’s upcoming missions, such as the IceBridge extension and the proposed CryoSat‑3, aim to improve spatial resolution and temporal coverage, potentially reducing uncertainties in the ice‑loss estimates [T2].

The 12‑trillion‑ton figure will likely be cited in national climate pledges and international negotiations. Its presence in the public discourse could accelerate the transition to low‑carbon economies, as governments and businesses recognize the tangible consequences of continued ice loss. The data may also influence the design of coastal adaptation projects, prompting earlier investment in sea‑defence infrastructure and the relocation of vulnerable communities.

At the same time, the economic opportunities presented by new polar shipping routes and resource extraction will require careful governance. The satellite record provides a benchmark against which to measure the environmental cost of such activities, ensuring that economic development does not outpace the planet’s capacity to absorb the resulting changes.

In sum, the satellite‑verified loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice is a stark reminder of the pace of climate change. It underscores the urgency of reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions, improving coastal resilience, and strengthening international cooperation. By turning raw satellite data into a clear, actionable metric, scientists are giving policymakers a powerful tool to navigate the challenges of the coming decades.
