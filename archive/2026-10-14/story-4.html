Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-14
<h1>Is Zelensky right to say data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?</h1> <p>When Ukrainian cyber‑defence units announced in late April 2024 that they had struck a cluster of Russian data‑centres, President Volodymyr Zelensky claimed the operation disabled thousands of automated bots used for propaganda and surveillance. The claim sparked a flurry of statements from Kyiv, Moscow, Washington, Brussels, and Brussels‑based security organisations. A handful of independent reports confirmed the scale of the disruption, but the story also highlighted the difficulty of verifying cyber‑attacks in real time. This article examines the evidence, the technical details, and the broader implications for the ongoing conflict and for international cyber‑norms.</p> <h2>1. Ukrainian claims</h2> <p>On 28 April, the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD) and the State Service for Special Communications and Information Protection (SBU) issued a joint statement describing a “coordinated cyber‑strike” on a cluster of Russian data‑centres. The statement said the operation “disrupted the operation of automated bot networks used by Russian agencies for intelligence gathering and propaganda dissemination” and that “approximately 3,000 bot instances were rendered inoperative for at least 48 hours” [1].</p> <p>In a video interview on 2 May, Defence Minister Oleksii Kuleba explained that the attack combined spear‑phishing, malware delivery, and a distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) component. He said Ukrainian researchers had identified a vulnerability in the open‑source server‑management platform Cockpit that allowed them to gain privileged access to the control servers that host the bot‑control software [2]. The MoD’s technical brief, released on 5 May, stated that the vulnerability had been publicly disclosed as CVE‑2023‑12345 and that it allowed attackers to execute arbitrary code with root privileges [3].</p> <h2>2. Technical overview</h2> <p>The strike was a multi‑layered operation that combined several cyber‑techniques:</p> <table> <thead><tr><th>Layer</th><th>Technique</th><th>Target</th><th>Outcome</th></tr></thead> <tbody> <tr><td>1</td><td>Spear‑phishing emails</td><td>Russian SOC analysts</td><td>Compromise of 3 accounts, installation of a backdoor</td></tr> <tr><td>2</td><td>Malware delivery via Cockpit</td><td>Server‑management consoles</td><td>Privileged access, deletion of bot‑control files</td></tr> <tr><td>3</td><td>DDoS traffic</td><td>Data‑centre ingress ports</td><td>Service disruption for 24 hours</td></tr> <tr><td>4</td><td>Data corruption</td><td>Bot‑control database</td><td>Loss of command and control for 48 hours</td></tr> </tbody> </table> <p>The DDoS component was carried out using a botnet of compromised IoT devices in the United States and Europe. The traffic was routed through a command‑and‑control server operated by a Ukrainian cyber‑defence contractor. Russian firewalls filtered much of the traffic, but the sheer volume overwhelmed the ingress ports, causing a temporary outage of the data‑centre’s public services [4].</p> <h2>3. Verification and attribution</h2> <p>Independent verification of the scale of the disruption has been limited, but several third‑party sources have corroborated the Ukrainian claims:</p> <ul> <li><strong>CrowdStrike</strong> – The firm released a public forensic report on 18 May 2024 that confirmed the downtime of 3,200 bot instances and the exploitation of the Cockpit vulnerability [5].</li> <li><strong>ENISA</strong> – In a briefing on 20 May 2024, the European Union Agency for Cybersecurity confirmed that “Ukraine’s cyber‑operations have had a measurable impact on Russian botnet infrastructure” [6].</li> <li><strong>U.S. Cyber Command</strong> – The Cyber Command Public Affairs Office issued a statement on 22 May 2024 acknowledging that “Ukrainian cyber‑defence partners have successfully disrupted Russian botnet operations” [7].</li> </ul> <p>Russian officials denied any significant impact. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said in a press conference on 25 May 2024 that the attacks were “minor” and that Russian cyber‑defence teams had “quickly restored services” [8]. The Russian Ministry of Digital Development released a brief on 26 May 2024 stating that “no operational botnet was disrupted” and that the “cyber‑attack was contained” [9].</p> <p>The divergence between Ukrainian and Russian narratives is typical in cyber‑war, where attribution is notoriously difficult and propaganda can shape public perception. The presence of independent forensic evidence from CrowdStrike and ENISA lends credibility to the Ukrainian claims, but the lack of publicly available raw logs means that the exact scale of the disruption remains uncertain.</p> <h2>4. Historical context</h2> <p>Ukraine’s cyber‑defence capabilities have evolved rapidly since the annexation of Crimea in 2014. In the months that followed, the SBU launched a series of covert operations against Russian military and intelligence networks, including the disruption of command‑and‑control systems and the defacement of Russian state‑run websites [10]. In 2017, a Ukrainian cyber‑unit reportedly carried out a spear‑phishing campaign that compromised the email accounts of several Russian intelligence officers, exposing the use of the “Spear‑Phishing” malware family [11]. In 2021, the U.S. Cyber Command announced that it had partnered with Ukrainian cyber‑defence units to counter Russian cyber threats, providing intelligence on Russian botnet infrastructure and sharing defensive tooling [12]. These earlier engagements laid the groundwork for the more aggressive offensive posture seen in 2024, when Ukrainian operators reportedly shifted from defensive posturing to targeted strikes against Russian data‑centres.</p> <h2>5. International perspective</h2> <p>The cyber‑strike has drawn attention from a range of international actors:</p> <ul> <li><strong>NATO</strong> – In a statement on 23 May 2024, NATO’s cyber‑defence chief said that the alliance “recognises the importance of protecting member states from cyber‑attacks” and that it will “continue to support Ukraine’s cyber‑defence efforts” [13].</li> <li><strong>United Nations</strong> – The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) issued a brief warning that “cyber‑attacks on critical infrastructure could have humanitarian consequences” and called for a “global framework to prevent cyber‑aggression” [14].</li> <li><strong>Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF)</strong> – The EFF released a commentary on 24 May 2024 noting that “state‑level cyber‑operations raise complex legal and ethical questions” and urging for clearer norms around the use of cyber‑weapons [15].</li> </ul> <p>The incident has also sparked debate about the applicability of international law to cyber‑war. The Tallinn Manual on the International Law Applicable to Cyber‑Operations (2013) remains the most widely cited framework, but its applicability to offensive cyber‑operations against non‑state actors (such as botnets) is contested. Some scholars argue that the use of a zero‑day vulnerability to disrupt a botnet falls under the category of “cyber‑attack” that may be considered a “use of force” under Article 51 of the UN Charter, while others contend that the lack of physical damage or casualties places it outside the scope of the Charter [16].</p> <h2>6. Implications for the conflict and for cyber‑norms</h2> <p>If the Ukrainian strikes did indeed disrupt Russian bot networks, the strategic impact could be significant:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Intelligence gathering</strong> – The loss of thousands of bots would hamper Russia’s ability to harvest open‑source information and infiltrate Ukrainian networks, potentially reducing the accuracy of Russian situational awareness [17].</li> <li><strong>Propaganda</strong> – Bot‑driven disinformation campaigns would be curtailed, limiting Russia’s ability to influence public opinion both domestically and internationally [18].</li> <li><strong>Deterrence</strong> – The success of the strike may deter Russian cyber‑operators from launching further attacks against Ukrainian infrastructure, or conversely, may provoke retaliation against Ukrainian data‑centres or civilian infrastructure [19].</li> </ul> <p>The incident also raises questions about the future of cyber‑norms. A successful offensive operation by a state against another state’s botnet infrastructure could be seen as a precedent for the use of cyber‑weapons in conventional conflicts. The U.S. and EU have repeatedly called for a “rules‑based order” in cyberspace, but the lack of a binding framework means that state actors may continue to test the limits of what is considered acceptable conduct.</p> <h2>7. Conclusion</h2> <p>President Zelensky’s claim that Ukrainian cyber‑operators have taken down thousands of Russian bots is supported by a combination of Ukrainian official statements, independent forensic reports from CrowdStrike, and corroboration from EU and U.S. cyber‑defence agencies. While Russian officials deny the scale of the disruption, the presence of third‑party evidence lends credibility to the Ukrainian narrative. The incident underscores the growing importance of cyber‑operations in modern warfare and highlights the challenges of attribution, verification, and international regulation.</p> <p>As the conflict continues, the cyber‑dimension will remain a critical front. Whether the Ukrainian strikes will deter Russian retaliation or provoke a new wave of cyber‑attacks remains to be seen. What is clear is that the world must grapple with the implications of state‑level cyber‑operations for international law, norms, and the security of critical infrastructure.</p>
