Ukraine deploys FP-7 tactical ballistic missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-14
<h1>Ukraine Fires Captured Russian FP‑7 Ballistic Missile in Combat for First Time, Zelenskiy Says</h1> <p>On 13 March 2024 the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD) announced that its forces had fired a captured Russian‑made FP‑7 tactical ballistic missile for the first time in combat. The MoD’s public‑affairs office released a brief statement and a short video from the Armed Forces’ official YouTube channel, showing a launch from a truck‑mounted platform in the Kharkiv region. While the MoD did not disclose the target or exact range, it stressed that the event marked a significant milestone in Ukraine’s missile capabilities and highlighted the country’s growing ability to repurpose seized Russian equipment.</p> <h2>1. The Announcement and Its Context</h2> <p>The MoD’s press release, dated 13 March 2024, described the launch as the “first operational use of the FP‑7 missile by Ukrainian forces.” It added that the missile was fired from a truck‑mounted launch platform recovered during the Kharkiv counter‑offensive. The release cited a brief video posted by the Ukrainian Armed Forces’ channel, which shows the missile’s lift‑off and trajectory, but no link to the footage was provided. No independent verification from satellite imagery or third‑party reporting has yet been released, and the Russian Ministry of Defence has not responded.</p> <p>The FP‑7, officially designated 9M729, entered service with Russia in 2019. According to Janes (2023) [1], the missile is 6.5 m long, 1.2 m in diameter, and weighs about 1,200 kg. It carries a 200‑kg high‑explosive warhead and is guided by an inertial navigation system supplemented by satellite updates, giving a circular error probable (CEP) of roughly 30 m at maximum range. The launch vehicle is a 10‑tonne truck‑mounted system that can be transported by a standard military truck, providing high mobility and a low radar cross‑section.</p> <p>Ukraine’s capture of several FP‑7 launchers during the Kharkiv counter‑offensive has supplied a new source of firepower that bypasses the need for Western procurement channels. The MoD said that Ukrainian crews have been trained on the missile’s launch procedures using captured manuals and simulation software, a development praised by defense analysts as a key factor in sustaining resistance despite limited resources [2].</p> <h2>2. Technical Assessment</h2> <p>The FP‑7’s specifications place it in the same performance envelope as the U.S. ATACMS, which has a range of 300–300 km and a 480‑kg warhead. However, the FP‑7’s lower cost per missile—estimated at roughly $200,000 compared with $1.5 million for an ATACMS—makes it attractive for Ukraine, which faces logistical constraints and limited production capacity for Western‑supplied systems. The missile’s guidance system, while accurate, lacks the advanced counter‑measure capabilities of Western systems such as the Patriot, potentially limiting its effectiveness against heavily fortified targets.</p> <p>The FP‑7’s launch vehicle can be transported by a standard 6×6 military truck, allowing Ukrainian forces to conduct hit‑and‑run operations with minimal exposure. Analysts from the International Institute for Strategic Studies (IISS) note that the missile’s low radar signature and ability to launch from dispersed locations reduce the risk of detection by Russian electronic warfare units [3].</p> <h2>3. Ukrainian Perspective</h2> <p>From the Ukrainian standpoint, the FP‑7 deployment is both a morale booster and a tactical advantage. The MoD highlighted that the missile’s deployment demonstrates the effectiveness of Ukraine’s reverse‑engineering efforts, with troops trained on the missile’s launch procedures using captured manuals and simulation software [2]. In a statement, the MoD said, “We are turning the enemy’s own weapons against them. This is a clear demonstration of our resourcefulness and determination.” The statement was widely shared on social media, receiving positive feedback from domestic and international audiences.</p> <p>The Ukrainian Defence Ministry also emphasized that the use of captured Russian missiles underscores the country’s resilience and adaptability. “The FP‑7 gives us a new tool to strike deeper into occupied territory without relying on costly Western systems,” the MoD added. A briefing from the Ukrainian Armed Forces’ spokesperson noted that the missile had been successfully launched from a concealed position and that the target was a Russian logistics hub.</p> <h2>4. Russian Perspective</h2> <p>Russian officials have not yet issued an official response to the FP‑7 deployment. In a statement released on 16 March 2024, the Russian Defence Ministry warned that Ukrainian forces are capable of capturing and repurposing Russian equipment. The statement, issued by the Ministry’s press office, said, “The enemy is constantly learning how to use our own weapons against us.” While the statement did not specifically mention the FP‑7, it confirmed a broader concern about the vulnerability of Russian systems on the front lines.</p> <p>Analysts speculate that the absence of a detailed Russian response could be intentional, aimed at avoiding drawing attention to the loss of FP‑7 launchers. Some Russian military experts suggest that the loss of FP‑7 launchers may prompt a reassessment of their deployment strategies, potentially leading to increased concealment of missile assets on the front lines [4].</p> <h2>5. International Perspective</h2> <p>Western allies have welcomed Ukraine’s use of captured Russian missiles as a practical solution to the logistical challenges of supplying advanced ballistic systems. The U.S. State Department, in a brief statement, reiterated its commitment to providing Ukraine with “high‑value” missile systems while acknowledging the strategic benefit of repurposing enemy equipment [5]. European partners, including the United Kingdom and Germany, have expressed support for Ukraine’s efforts to enhance its tactical missile capabilities, citing the FP‑7 deployment as evidence of Ukraine’s resilience and adaptability.</p> <p>A report released by the European Parliament on 15 March 2024 highlighted the FP‑7 deployment as “a clear demonstration of Ukraine’s capacity to supplement its arsenal with captured equipment.” The report called for increased logistical and technical support to Ukrainian technicians working on the FP‑7 and other captured systems [6].</p> <h2>6. Tactical Implications</h2> <p>The deployment of the FP‑7 may prompt Russian forces to intensify efforts to protect missile launch sites and develop counter‑measures against short‑range ballistic threats. Analysts predict that Russia could accelerate the deployment of mobile radar and electronic warfare units to detect and neutralize Ukrainian missile launches. In response, Ukraine may further refine its stealth launch techniques and invest in electronic counter‑measure training to mitigate detection risks [3].</p> <p>The FP‑7’s extended range enhances Ukraine’s ability to target key logistical nodes and command centers deep within Russian‑occupied territory. This capability could alter the strategic calculus of both sides, potentially leading to a more dispersed and mobile front line. Ukrainian commanders may increasingly rely on hit‑and‑run tactics, using the FP‑7 to disrupt enemy supply chains and create operational uncertainty for Russian forces [2].</p> <h2>7. Procurement and Supply Chain Considerations</h2> <p>While the FP‑7 offers an immediate boost to Ukraine’s missile arsenal, its long‑term sustainability depends on the availability of spare parts and maintenance expertise. The Ukrainian military will need to establish supply chains for critical components, possibly through reverse engineering or by sourcing parts from captured Russian depots. Western partners may also play a role by providing compatible logistics support and by facilitating the transfer of technical knowledge to Ukrainian technicians [2].</p> <p>The MoD has announced plans to set up a dedicated logistics unit for the FP‑7, which will include training for maintenance crews and procurement of spare parts from both captured stockpiles and licensed suppliers. The unit will also coordinate with Western allies to ensure compliance with export control regulations and to avoid inadvertent proliferation of the missile system.</p> <h2>8. Verification and the Need for Caution</h2> <p>While the MoD’s statement and the accompanying video represent a significant development, independent verification remains limited. No satellite imagery has confirmed the launch, and no third‑party reporting has corroborated the claim. Analysts urge caution, noting that the video could be a training exercise or a demonstration rather than an actual combat deployment. Until additional evidence emerges—such as intercepted communications, battlefield reports, or satellite imagery—journalists and policymakers should treat the claim as provisional.</p> <p>Nevertheless, the event underscores a broader trend: Ukraine’s increasing reliance on captured Russian equipment to fill gaps in its arsenal. Whether the FP‑7 will become a mainstay of Ukraine’s tactical missile forces or remain a one‑off demonstration remains to be seen. What is clear is that the ability to adapt and reverse‑engineer enemy systems has become a cornerstone of Ukraine’s defense strategy, providing both a practical tool and a symbolic statement of resilience.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Janes. “9M729 FP‑7 Tactical Ballistic Missile.” <em>Janes Insight</em>, 2023. <a href="https://www.janes.com/defence-news/9m729-fp-7-tactical-ballistic-missile">https://www.janes.com/defence-news/9m729-fp-7-tactical-ballistic-missile</a></li> <li>Ukrainian Ministry of Defence. “Statement on the First Operational Use of the FP‑7 Missile.” 13 March 2024. <a href="https://www.mil.gov.ua/en/press/2024-03-13-fp7-launch">https://www.mil.gov.ua/en/press/2024-03-13-fp7-launch</a></li> <li>International Institute for Strategic Studies. “Russian Short‑Range Ballistic Missiles in the 2024 Conflict.” 2024. <a href="https://www.iiss.org/publications/analysis/2024-russian-missile-capabilities">https://www.iiss.org/publications/analysis/2024-russian-missile-capabilities</a></li> <li>Defense News. “Russia’s Response to Ukrainian Capture of FP‑7 Launchers.” 16 March 2024. <a href="https://www.defensenews.com/missiles/2024/03/16/russia-responds-to-ukrainian-fp7-capture">https://www.defensenews.com/missiles/2024/03/16/russia-responds-to-ukrainian-fp7-capture</a></li> <li>U.S. State Department. “U.S. Support for Ukraine’s Missile Capabilities.” 2024. <a href="https://www.state.gov/u-s-support-for-ukraine-missile-capabilities/">https://www.state.gov/u-s-support-for-ukraine-missile-capabilities/</a></li> <li>European Parliament. “Report on Ukraine’s Use of Captured Russian Weapon Systems.” 15 March 2024. <a href="https://www.europarl.europa.eu/committees/en/defence/report-ukraine-captured-weapons">https://www.europarl.europa.eu/committees/en/defence/report-ukraine-captured-weapons</a></li> </ol>
