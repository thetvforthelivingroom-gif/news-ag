Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-17
Over the last 47 years, satellites have documented the loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica. This unprecedented loss is already raising sea levels, reshaping weather patterns, and forcing governments to confront the urgency of climate action.

What happened

Satellite gravimetry missions began in the early 1970s and have provided the first continuous, global record of the mass of the world’s ice sheets. By tracking minute changes in Earth’s gravity field, these instruments infer how much ice has disappeared or accumulated over time. Recent analyses of data from NASA’s GRACE and GRACE‑Follow‑On missions, combined with ESA’s CryoSat, have quantified a cumulative loss exceeding 12 trillion tons (about 12 000 Gt) of ice from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets over the past 47 years. This figure represents the largest loss of ice ever measured by satellite and underscores the accelerating pace of ice melt in the planet’s polar regions.

The loss is not evenly distributed. Greenland’s ice sheet has contributed the bulk of the measured mass loss, with its large surface area and thickness making it especially vulnerable to rising temperatures. Antarctica, while larger in area, has experienced more variable loss. Some sectors have gained ice, while others—particularly the West Antarctic Ice Sheet—have been losing mass at an accelerating rate. Together, these two sheets account for roughly 70 % of the global ice mass, so the 12 trillion‑ton figure translates to a significant fraction of the total ice reservoir.

Background and context

The satellite record began with the launch of the first Earth‑observation satellites in the 1970s, which focused on atmospheric and oceanic measurements. It was not until the 1990s that dedicated gravimetric missions such as GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment) began to provide the precision needed to detect changes in ice mass. Over the past three decades, the technology has matured, allowing scientists to separate the gravitational signature of ice loss from other mass changes, such as groundwater depletion or oceanic redistribution.

The data reveal a clear trend: the rate of ice loss has accelerated over time. Early satellite observations in the 1990s indicated a modest annual loss of a few hundred gigatons. By the 2010s, the rate had increased to roughly 280 Gt per year in Greenland alone, a figure that has continued to climb. In Antarctica, the loss rate has been more variable, but recent years have seen a marked increase in the mass loss from the West Antarctic Ice Sheet. These trends align with the broader pattern of global warming, as rising temperatures melt surface snow and destabilize ice shelves that buttress the flow of ice into the ocean.

The 12 trillion‑ton loss is not merely a number; it has tangible consequences. Each gigaton of ice that melts ultimately contributes to sea‑level rise. The cumulative loss from Greenland and Antarctica has already raised global sea levels by several centimeters, a change that has profound implications for coastal communities, infrastructure, and ecosystems. Moreover, the loss of ice alters ocean circulation patterns, which can influence weather extremes, such as heatwaves and heavy precipitation events, across the globe.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Scientific consensus

The scientific community widely agrees that the observed ice loss is driven primarily by anthropogenic climate change. Multiple peer‑reviewed studies have linked rising atmospheric temperatures to increased surface melt and accelerated ice flow. The satellite record provides a robust, independent confirmation of these findings, offering a global perspective that ground‑based measurements alone cannot achieve.

Policy implications

The magnitude of the ice loss has become a key metric in international climate negotiations. Policymakers use satellite data to assess progress toward national commitments, such as net‑zero targets, and to evaluate the effectiveness of mitigation strategies. The evidence of accelerating ice loss underscores the urgency of reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions and has informed the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) discussions leading up to the COP28 conference.

Economic considerations

The loss of ice has immediate economic costs. Rising sea levels threaten coastal infrastructure—including ports, airports, and residential areas—necessitating costly adaptation measures such as sea‑walls and managed retreat. At the same time, the potential for increased freshwater availability from melting ice has prompted discussions about harnessing this resource, though the long‑term sustainability of such approaches remains uncertain.

Skepticism and debate

While the consensus is strong, a minority of skeptics question the interpretation of satellite data, citing potential calibration errors, atmospheric interference, and assumptions about ice density. Some critics argue that natural variability could account for a portion of the observed changes. However, the convergence of multiple satellite missions and the consistency of the data with independent ground‑based observations reduce the likelihood that the findings are artifacts of measurement error.

Natural variability versus human influence

Climate scientists emphasize that the long‑term trend of ice loss cannot be explained by natural cycles alone. While short‑term fluctuations—such as El Niño or volcanic eruptions—can temporarily influence ice melt rates, the persistent upward trajectory over nearly five decades aligns with the steady increase in global temperatures driven by fossil‑fuel combustion. This distinction is critical for policymakers, as it frames the issue as a human‑caused crisis rather than a natural fluctuation.

What to watch (future implications)

Continued monitoring

The satellite record will continue to be refined as new missions launch and existing instruments are upgraded. NASA’s GRACE‑Follow‑On, for example, will provide higher temporal resolution, allowing scientists to detect rapid changes in ice dynamics that may precede large discharges into the ocean. ESA’s upcoming missions will further enhance spatial resolution, offering finer detail on the processes that govern ice loss.

Integration with climate models

The satellite data feed into climate models that project future sea‑level rise and regional climate impacts. As models incorporate more accurate ice‑sheet dynamics, policymakers will have better tools to plan adaptation strategies, such as infrastructure design, zoning regulations, and emergency response planning.

Policy momentum

The evidence of accelerating ice loss is likely to influence the outcomes of the upcoming COP28 conference. Nations may feel increased pressure to strengthen their emission‑reduction pledges, secure climate finance for adaptation, and invest in research and development of low‑carbon technologies. The data also provide a benchmark against which the effectiveness of mitigation policies can be measured.

Public engagement

The visualizations of ice loss—from satellite imagery to time‑lapse videos—have become powerful tools for communicating the urgency of climate action. As the public becomes more aware of the tangible impacts of ice melt, there may be greater support for policies that curb emissions and protect vulnerable communities.

Economic transition

The loss of ice also signals a broader shift in the global energy landscape. As the world moves away from fossil fuels to mitigate further ice loss, investment in renewable energy, energy efficiency, and carbon‑capture technologies will accelerate. This transition presents both challenges and opportunities for economies worldwide, reshaping labor markets, trade patterns, and geopolitical dynamics.

---

The satellite record of more than 12 trillion tons of ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica over 47 years offers a concrete, measurable testament to the accelerating pace of climate change. It underscores the urgency of international cooperation, robust policy action, and sustained scientific inquiry to safeguard the planet’s future.
