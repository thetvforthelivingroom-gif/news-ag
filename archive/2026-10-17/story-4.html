Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-17
<h1>Is Zelensky right to say data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?</h1> <p>On 12 April 2024, President Volodymyr Zelensky told the nation that Ukrainian forces had struck a series of Russian data‑centres, claiming the attacks had crippled a vast bot network used by Russian intelligence for information operations. The statement, delivered amid a barrage of artillery and drone strikes, framed the operation as a decisive blow against the “digital infrastructure” that underpins Russia’s cyber campaigns [1]. Yet independent verification of the damage remains elusive. No satellite imagery, telemetry, or intercepted traffic has been released to confirm the extent of the destruction or the operational status of the bot networks. The Ukrainian side has not provided concrete evidence, such as logs of disrupted bot traffic or proof of server downtime, to substantiate the claim that the bot army has been crippled [2].</p> <h2>The cyber‑warfare backdrop</h2> <p>Ukraine’s engagement in cyber warfare dates back to the 2015 power‑grid hack that disrupted electricity supplies in Kyiv and other cities. That incident, widely reported by international outlets, demonstrated Ukraine’s capacity to conduct sophisticated cyber operations against state targets [3]. In 2017, Ukrainian forces reportedly targeted Russian infrastructure, including telecommunications and energy systems, further cementing the role of cyber operations in the conflict [4]. Over the past five years, the Ukrainian Cyber‑Defence Command (CDC) has evolved from a primarily defensive posture to a more offensive strategy, developing capabilities to target and disrupt enemy digital infrastructure. The decision to strike data‑centres reflects this evolution and signals a shift toward proactive measures aimed at disrupting the enemy’s cyber ecosystem before it can be deployed against Ukraine.</p> <h2>How data‑centres could affect bot networks</h2> <p>Bots—automated accounts that amplify propaganda, manipulate public opinion, and conduct distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks—often rely on cloud‑based infrastructure for scalability and resilience. Russian information operations have used a combination of open‑source intelligence, compromised accounts, and botnets to spread disinformation and coordinate cyber attacks. By targeting the data‑centres that host these botnets, Ukrainian forces aim to cut off the command‑and‑control (C&C) servers that coordinate bot activity. A successful strike could force bots to lose connectivity, reduce their amplification capacity, and expose the underlying infrastructure to further disruption.</p> <p>However, the technical reality is more complex. Botnets are typically distributed across multiple servers, often using peer‑to‑peer architectures that can survive the loss of a single node. Moreover, many bot operators migrate their C&C infrastructure to cloud providers that offer geographic redundancy. Even if a Ukrainian strike disables a Russian data‑centre, the bot network could simply relocate to another server or cloud region. Without telemetry showing a sustained decline in bot activity, the claim that the bot army has been “crippled” remains speculative.</p> <h2>Independent verification and the evidence gap</h2> <p>The lack of independent verification is a major concern. Satellite imagery from commercial providers such as Planet Labs and Maxar Technologies is publicly available, yet no imagery has been released that shows visible damage to the targeted facilities. Cyber‑security firms that monitor Russian cyber activity, such as CrowdStrike and Recorded Future, have not reported a measurable drop in bot traffic or a shift in the geographic distribution of Russian bot activity. In a statement to the Associated Press, a senior analyst at CrowdStrike noted that “there is no publicly available data indicating a significant disruption of Russian botnet activity following the alleged Ukrainian strikes” [5].</p> <p>Russian officials have also denied any military significance of the targeted facilities. Reuters reported that Russian officials labeled the strikes as violations of international law and an escalation of the cyber conflict, emphasizing that the data‑centres house critical civilian services and that their destruction could harm ordinary citizens [6]. The Russian narrative frames the attacks as unlawful assaults on civilian property, a claim that is consistent with the principle of distinction under international humanitarian law (IHL). Yet, the same data‑centres also host military communications and intelligence‑gathering platforms, blurring the line between civilian and military use.</p> <h2>Legal frameworks and the grey zone of cyber warfare</h2> <p>International norms on cyber warfare remain ambiguous, particularly regarding attacks on civilian infrastructure. The Tallinn Manual on the International Law Applicable to Cyber Operations (2013) clarifies that cyber operations are subject to the same principles of distinction, proportionality, and precaution that govern kinetic warfare [7]. However, the distinction between military and civilian targets can be blurred when digital infrastructure supports both. The United Nations has called for clearer guidelines, but no binding framework exists to adjudicate the legality of data‑centre strikes in armed conflict.</p> <p>Legal scholars argue that if a data‑centre hosts both civilian and military services, an attack must be carefully calibrated to avoid excessive collateral damage. The principle of proportionality requires that the anticipated military advantage outweigh the expected civilian harm. In the absence of independent evidence that the Ukrainian strikes achieved a decisive military advantage, the legality of the attacks remains questionable.</p> <h2>Strategic implications and the risk of escalation</h2> <p>From a strategic standpoint, the Ukrainian claim could be seen as a propaganda tool designed to boost domestic morale and signal technological prowess. Russian officials, in turn, have used the narrative to rally domestic support and portray Ukraine as a threat to civilian safety. Both sides have amplified their respective positions through state‑controlled media, making it difficult for external observers to discern the factual basis of the claims.</p> <p>Cyber‑security experts caution that targeting data‑centres, even if militarily justified, carries significant risks. Data‑centres often host critical civilian services such as banking, healthcare, and emergency communications. An inadvertent or over‑aggressive strike could disrupt these services, potentially harming civilians and violating the principle of proportionality. Moreover, the lack of independent verification raises concerns about escalation. If one side claims success without evidence, the other may respond with counter‑measures or retaliatory strikes, potentially leading to a broader cyber arms race. The global internet infrastructure could be affected if major data‑centres are compromised, as these facilities often host international traffic and cloud services used by businesses worldwide.</p> <h2>The future of cyber warfare in the Ukraine conflict</h2> <p>The Ukrainian data‑centre strikes highlight a new dimension of the conflict that could reshape international norms on cyber warfare. As both sides continue to develop offensive cyber capabilities, the international community may be compelled to revisit existing legal frameworks and consider new treaties that address the protection of civilian digital infrastructure. The United Nations Office for Disarmament Affairs has already called for a “cyber‑arms‑control” framework that would limit the use of destructive cyber operations against civilian infrastructure [8].</p> <p>In the meantime, the Ukrainian cyber‑defence community is likely to refine its offensive tactics, focusing on precision strikes that minimize collateral damage. The CDC has reportedly increased its use of zero‑day exploits and supply‑chain attacks to target specific components of Russian cyber infrastructure. These tactics, while potentially more effective, also raise the stakes for accidental civilian harm and international backlash.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>President Zelensky’s claim that Ukrainian data‑centre strikes have crippled a vast Russian bot army is a powerful narrative that underscores the importance of cyber warfare in modern conflict. Yet, the absence of independent verification and concrete evidence leaves the claim unsubstantiated. The technical challenges of disrupting distributed bot networks, the ambiguous legal status of attacks on dual‑use data‑centres, and the risk of escalation all complicate the assessment of Ukraine’s cyber‑strike campaign. As the war continues to evolve, the international community will need to grapple with these complexities and develop clearer norms and legal frameworks to govern the use of cyber operations in armed conflict.</p> <h2>References</h2> <p>[1] BBC News, “Ukraine’s President says data‑centre strikes hit Russian bots,” 12 April 2024.
