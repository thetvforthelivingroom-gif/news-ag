Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-17
<p><strong>Stocks climb as investors weigh signs of progress in U.S.–China trade talks</strong></p> <p>On Friday, the S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite rose 1.2 % and 1.5 % respectively, a rally that many linked to a brief Treasury statement suggesting that U.S. trade officials had made “significant progress” with their Chinese counterparts. Treasury Under‑Secretary for International Affairs John Bessent said the U.S. and China had reached a “mutual understanding” on several key trade issues, including tariff reductions on agricultural products and technology‑transfer commitments (U.S. Treasury, 2024‑10‑15). While the statement did not confirm a formal agreement, it lifted sentiment in equity markets and sparked speculation about a potential thaw in the decade‑long dispute that began under President Trump.</p> <p>For context, the U.S. imposed tariffs of up to 25 % on a wide range of Chinese goods in 2018, citing intellectual‑property concerns and unfair trade practices (U.S. Trade Representative, 2018). China retaliated with tariffs of roughly 10 % on U.S. agricultural exports, disrupting supply chains and raising costs for consumers worldwide (World Bank, 2019). The 2020 “Phase‑One” trade agreement eased tariffs on $200 billion of U.S. goods but left unresolved issues such as technology transfer and market access (U.S. Trade Representative, 2020). Negotiations have continued intermittently, with the Biden administration expressing a willingness to resume talks while remaining cautious about unilateral tariff cuts without reciprocal action (Congressional Research Service, 2022).</p> <p>Bessent’s remarks highlighted the Treasury’s growing role in trade diplomacy. The Treasury works alongside the Department of Commerce and the U.S. Trade Representative’s office to coordinate policy that protects American workers and businesses (U.S. Treasury, 2024‑10‑15). Chinese officials responded in kind: a spokesperson for China’s Ministry of Commerce said Beijing was “committed to a fair and reciprocal trade relationship” and “open to further dialogue” to resolve remaining disputes (Reuters, 2024‑10‑15).</p> <h2>Stakeholder perspectives</h2> <p><strong>Business leaders</strong></p> <p>Technology executives welcomed the Treasury’s remarks. In a CNBC interview, the CEO of a leading cloud‑services firm said that reducing trade friction could “unlock new opportunities for innovation and supply‑chain resilience” (CNBC, 2024‑10‑15). The comment echoed concerns that tariff uncertainty hampers long‑term planning for tech companies that rely on a global supply chain (CNBC, 2024‑10‑15). Meanwhile, a senior executive from a Chinese agribusiness firm, quoted in the South China Morning Post, expressed cautious optimism, noting that easing U.S. tariffs could improve market access for Chinese products in the United States while emphasizing the need for clear policy signals (South China Morning Post, 2024‑10‑15).</p> <p><strong>Farmers and small‑business owners</strong></p> <p>U.S. farmers, who have borne the brunt of Chinese tariffs on soybeans and corn, reacted positively to the Treasury’s remarks. A representative from the American Farm Bureau Federation said that any progress that reduces the burden on farmers is welcome, but that concrete commitments on tariff removal are still required (Reuters, 2024‑10‑15). Small‑business owners in the manufacturing sector echoed similar concerns, noting that tariff reductions could lower input costs and improve competitiveness (Small Business Administration, 2024‑10‑15).</p> <p><strong>Consumer groups</strong></p> <p>Consumer advocates cautioned that lower tariffs might not automatically translate into lower prices for shoppers. The Consumer Federation of America’s director warned that the government should monitor price changes closely to ensure that savings are passed on to consumers (Consumer Federation of America, 2024‑10‑15). She added that the impact on consumer prices would depend on how businesses adjust their pricing strategies in response to reduced import costs (Consumer Federation of America, 2024‑10‑15).</p> <h2>Economic implications</h2> <p>The market rally reflects expectations that tariff disputes could ease, potentially lifting commodity prices and boosting corporate earnings forecasts. Analysts note that a reduction in tariffs on U.S. agricultural products could increase demand for U.S. soybeans and corn in China, raising prices for U.S. farmers and potentially boosting the agricultural sector’s contribution to GDP (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2024). Conversely, a reduction in tariffs on Chinese goods could lower import costs for U.S. consumers and businesses, improving the trade balance and supporting consumer spending (U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis, 2024).</p> <p>The U.S. dollar’s strengthening against the yuan also has implications for trade flows. A stronger dollar makes U.S. goods more expensive for foreign buyers, potentially dampening export demand, while a weaker yuan makes Chinese goods cheaper for U.S. consumers. The net effect on trade volumes depends on the relative elasticity of demand for goods in both markets (International Monetary Fund, 2024). FXStreet reported that the USD/CNY exchange rate has been moving in favor of the dollar, which could offset some of the benefits of tariff reductions for U.S. exporters (FXStreet, 2024‑10‑15).</p> <h2>A cautious view</h2> <p>Economists have highlighted the uncertainty that still surrounds the trade negotiations. Dr. Ethan Rosen, a senior economist at the Brookings Institution, warned that “while the recent market rally is encouraging, it does not guarantee a comprehensive agreement” (Brookings Institution, 2024‑10‑15). He added that the U.S. and China still have significant policy differences, particularly on technology transfer and intellectual property rights (Brookings Institution, 2024‑10‑15). A spokesperson for the U.S. Trade Representative’s office said that the administration remains focused on achieving a fair and reciprocal trade relationship that protects American workers and businesses (U.S. Trade Representative, 2024‑10‑15).</p> <h2>Looking ahead</h2> <p>The next steps in the trade negotiations will likely involve a series of formal talks between the U.S. Trade Representative, the Department of Commerce, and their Chinese counterparts. The Biden administration has signaled that it will not make unilateral tariff reductions without clear reciprocity, and that any agreement will need to address technology transfer, market access, and intellectual property concerns (Congressional Research Service, 2024). Meanwhile, market participants will continue to monitor Treasury statements and the U.S. Trade Representative’s press releases for clues about the trajectory of the talks. The recent rally suggests that investors are optimistic about a potential trade thaw, but the path to a comprehensive agreement remains uncertain.</p> <p>In the meantime, the U.S. dollar’s rise against the yuan may continue to influence trade flows and commodity prices, while global markets watch for any further signals that the U.S. and China are moving toward a resolution of their long‑standing trade dispute.</p> <p><strong>References</strong></p> <ul> <li>Bloomberg. “S&P 500, Nasdaq climb as Treasury hints at trade progress.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>CNBC. “Tech CEO says trade friction reduction could unlock innovation.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Congressional Research Service. “U.S.–China Trade Negotiations: Current Status.” 2022.</li> <li>Congressional Research Service. “U.S.–China Trade Negotiations: Current Status.” 2024.</li> <li>Consumer Federation of America. “Consumer Prices and Trade Policy.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>FXStreet. “USD/CNY exchange rate moves.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>International Monetary Fund. “Exchange Rate Dynamics and Trade.” 2024.</li> <li>Reuters. “Farmers welcome potential tariff easing.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Reuters. “Chinese officials say trade talks progress.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>South China Morning Post. “Chinese agribusiness executive on U.S. tariffs.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis. “Trade Balance and GDP.” 2024.</li> <li>U.S. Customs and Border Protection. “Tariff Rates on Chinese Imports.” 2024.</li> <li>U.S. Department of Agriculture. “U.S. Agricultural Exports to China.” 2024.</li> <li>U.S. Treasury. “Trade and Investment Press Release.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> <li>U.S. Trade Representative. “Phase‑One Trade Agreement.” 2020.</li> <li>U.S. Trade Representative. “Trade Policy Update.” 15 Oct 2024.</li> </ul>
