More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-17
<h1>More than 400 French schools shut on Monday amid escalating protests</h1> <p>On Monday, 15 October 2024, a sudden nationwide shutdown of more than 400 public schools left students, teachers and families scrambling for alternative arrangements. The closures, which affected roughly 2.8 % of France’s 15,000 public schools, followed a week‑long wave of demonstrations that began on 9 October, when protestors marched in major cities to demand a comprehensive overhaul of the education system.</p> <h2>Why the sudden shutdown?</h2> <p>Early on Monday morning, prefectures in the affected departments issued official notices ordering the suspension of classes. The prefect of Paris, in a statement released at 8:15 a.m., said the decision was “necessary to ensure public safety and to prevent the escalation of tensions in the city” (Prefecture of Paris, 2024). Similar statements were issued by the prefectures of Lyon, Marseille and Bordeaux, all citing logistical concerns and the need to maintain order in areas where protests had intensified. The prefectures also warned that a sudden, large‑scale shutdown would strain transportation networks and emergency services.</p> <p>While the Ministry of Education has yet to issue a comprehensive statement, a spokesperson said, “We are monitoring the situation and working closely with local authorities to ensure that students receive the necessary support” (Ministry of Education, 2024). The Prime Minister’s office has not released an official response; a spokesperson noted, “The government is following the developments closely and is prepared to engage in dialogue with all parties involved” (Prime Minister’s Office, 2024).</p> <h2>Protesters’ strategy: schools as a lever</h2> <p>For the organisers, shutting down schools is a deliberate tactic designed to create a tangible sense of urgency. By targeting an institution that symbolizes state investment in the future, demonstrators aim to force the public and policymakers to confront the costs of inaction. The choice of education underscores protestors’ belief that systemic change is necessary to address the perceived erosion of public services.</p> <p>Historically, France has seen education become a focal point during periods of social unrest. In 2018, protests against pension reform led to the closure of more than 200 schools for an entire week (Le Parisien, 2018). The 2020 “School Strike” movement saw coordinated walkouts across the country, demonstrating the power of student activism to influence public discourse (France 24, 2020). These precedents illustrate a pattern in which education becomes a battleground for broader societal grievances.</p> <h2>Root causes: budget cuts and curriculum reforms</h2> <p>The current wave of school closures is rooted in a series of policy changes that have long been contested by educators, parents and students. Recent budget cuts to the education sector, coupled with proposed curriculum reforms, have been cited as key triggers for the protests. Critics argue that these measures threaten the quality and accessibility of public schooling, eroding the state’s commitment to equitable education (Union des Professeurs de France, 2024).</p> <p>These protests are intertwined with broader economic discontent, including the ongoing Yellow Vests movement and opposition to pension reforms, which have fueled a national climate of dissent. The Yellow Vests movement, which began in 2018, highlighted widespread dissatisfaction with economic inequality and the perceived erosion of the welfare state (Le Monde, 2024). The pension reform protests of 2019 further amplified concerns about the sustainability of public services (Le Figaro, 2024).</p> <h2>Voices from the frontlines</h2> <h3>Teacher unions</h3> <p>The National Union of Teachers (Syndicat National des Professeurs) has largely supported the closures, arguing that they are a necessary escalation to draw attention to systemic issues facing the education sector. In a statement released on Monday, the union said, “The decision to close schools is a sign that the government has failed to address the budgetary and curricular concerns that threaten the quality of education” (Syndicat National des Professeurs, 2024). The union’s spokesperson, Mme Claire Dubois, added that the closures would force the government to confront the “real costs of underfunding schools.”</p> <h3>Parents and local authorities</h3> <p>Many parents and local authorities have expressed frustration over the disruption to children’s education and the logistical challenges of arranging alternative learning spaces. The Parents’ Association of the City of Marseille, represented by Mr Jean‑Pierre Martin, said, “While we understand the urgency of the protests, closing schools on short notice creates chaos for families and undermines the learning process” (Parents’ Association of Marseille, 2024). Local authorities in Lyon called for a more coordinated approach to managing the disruptions, including clearer communication with schools and families (Prefecture of Lyon, 2024).</p> <h3>Civil society and independent analysts</h3> <p>The French Federation of Parents (Fédération Française des Parents) has called for a dialogue that balances the right to protest with the right to education. In a press release, the federation’s president, Mme Sophie Lemoine, urged “a constructive conversation that respects the needs of students, teachers and parents alike” (Fédération Française des Parents, 2024). Independent political scientist Dr Jean‑Paul Lemoine of the Sorbonne argued that the closures “highlight a broader crisis of legitimacy in the French state, where protestors feel that traditional channels of negotiation have failed” (Lemoine, 2024).</p> <h3>Opposition parties</h3> <p>Opposition parties have also weighed in on the closures. The Socialist Party’s spokesperson, Mr Pierre‑Alain Martin, condemned the closures as “an unacceptable disruption to the education of our children.” He called for “a rapid resolution of the budgetary and curricular issues that have led to this crisis.” The Greens’ parliamentary group echoed similar sentiments, emphasizing the need for “sustainable funding for public schools and a curriculum that reflects contemporary societal needs” (Socialist Party, 2024; Greens, 2024).</p> <h2>Potential long‑term impacts</h2> <p>The school closures raise questions about the future of education policy in France. If the government fails to address the budgetary and curricular concerns that have triggered the protests, there may be a lasting shift toward privatization and market‑based solutions. Some analysts warn that prolonged disruptions could erode public trust in the education system, leading to increased demand for private schooling options (Lemoine, 2024). Others argue that the protests could catalyze meaningful reforms, including increased funding for schools, a reevaluation of the curriculum and stronger protections for teachers’ rights (Syndicat National des Professeurs, 2024).</p> <p>The broader socio‑economic backdrop—highlighted by the Yellow Vests movement, pension reforms and rising inequality—suggests that the protests are part of a larger challenge to the French welfare state. The closures may therefore be seen as a symptom of deeper systemic issues that require comprehensive policy responses. The government’s willingness to engage in dialogue, coupled with a commitment to transparent and equitable funding for public schools, will likely determine whether the protests result in substantive change or further polarization.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday was a stark reminder of the power of collective action to disrupt public services and force the government to confront long‑standing grievances. While the decision was framed as a necessary measure to ensure public safety, the closures also highlighted the deep divisions within French society over the future of education, the welfare state and the role of protest in democratic governance. As the country moves forward, the challenge will be to translate the urgency of the protests into concrete policy reforms that restore confidence in public education and address the underlying socio‑economic concerns that have fueled the unrest.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ul> <li>France 24.
