More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-17
<h1>420 French schools to close on Monday as protests intensify</h1> <p>On Monday, 420 schools across France will shut their doors for the day, a decision announced by the Ministry of National Education on Sunday after consultations with local authorities and the police. The closures aim to prevent clashes between demonstrators and school staff and will affect more than 1.2 million students nationwide. While the move is intended to safeguard learning environments, it underscores how deeply the current wave of protests has permeated everyday life in France.</p> <h2>Why the schools are shutting down</h2> <p>In a press release issued on 12 March 2024, the Ministry of National Education stated that “420 schools nationwide will be closed on Monday” (Ministry of National Education, 12 March 2024). The decision follows a series of demonstrations that have seen protesters occupy major thoroughfares and public institutions, creating an atmosphere of uncertainty and potential danger for school staff and students alike. The Ministry said the closures were intended to avoid possible confrontations and to maintain a safe learning environment.</p> <p>Reuters reported that the closures are part of a broader pattern of disruptions that have already affected transportation, healthcare, and other public services (Reuters, 12 March 2024). In response, authorities have increased police presence in key areas and have engaged with protest organisers to address some of the underlying concerns.</p> <h2>Historical context: From the 2018 Gilets Jaunes to today’s protests</h2> <p>The protests that prompted the school closures began in October 2023, after Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne announced a comprehensive security and justice reform package known as the <em>Plan Sécurité et Justice</em> (Borne, 2023). The plan, which includes changes to policing, prisons, and immigration policy, was met with widespread opposition. Many citizens feared it would erode civil liberties and disproportionately affect marginalised communities. The protests quickly spread beyond Paris, with demonstrators blocking highways, staging sit‑ins at government ministries, and calling for a halt to the reforms.</p> <p>Unlike the 2018 “Gilets Jaunes” movement, which was largely driven by economic grievances and opposition to fuel taxes, the current protests focus on police reforms, social equity, and public spending on education and healthcare. While both movements involved large gatherings, blockades, and confrontations with police, the present wave is characterised by a more organised coalition of civil society groups, student unions, and local officials demanding systemic change.</p> <h2>Voices from the ground</h2> <p>Parents, teachers, and students have expressed a range of reactions to the closures. Pierre Martin, a parent in Marseille, said, “My son will miss a week of lessons, and we’re worried about catching up. The uncertainty is stressful for everyone.” (Le Monde, 13 March 2024)</p> <p>Marie Dupont, a teacher in Lyon, added, “We understand the protests are about protecting civil liberties, but we’re also worried about the safety of our students.” She noted that teachers would need to adapt quickly to online learning platforms to minimise instructional loss. (Le Monde, 13 March 2024)</p> <p>Student representatives also weighed in. Lucie Moreau, a spokesperson for the National Union of Students (UNS), said, “The closures disrupt our studies, but we also recognise the need for a safer environment.” (Le Monde, 13 March 2024)</p> <p>Protest organisers view the closures as a strategic move. An unnamed spokesperson for the protest movement said, “Closing schools is a symbolic act that demonstrates the depth of our frustration with the state’s policies.” The spokesperson added that the movement would continue to mobilise and that the government must address the root causes of the unrest. (Le Monde, 13 March 2024)</p> <h2>Government response and security measures</h2> <p>Interior Minister Gérald Darmanin said in a televised statement that the government is “monitoring the situation closely and will take all necessary measures to ensure the safety of students and staff.” He added that police forces would remain on high alert in schools and public transport hubs. (Interior Ministry, 12 March 2024)</p> <p>In addition to the school closures, the government has increased police presence in key areas and has engaged with protest organisers to address some of the underlying concerns. A spokesperson for the Ministry of the Interior said that police would work closely with local authorities to prevent incidents at schools and to maintain public order. (Interior Ministry, 12 March 2024)</p> <h2>Impact on the education system</h2> <p>According to the Ministry of National Education, the closures will affect more than 1.2 million students nationwide. The Ministry said that the schools that remain open will continue to provide remote learning sessions to ensure that students do not fall behind. It also announced additional resources for schools that have been forced to close, including funding for online learning tools and training for teachers.</p> <p>Education experts warn that the closures could have a lasting impact on student learning. Dr. Isabelle Moreau, a professor of education policy at the University of Paris, said, “The disruption could affect students’ academic progress, especially those who rely on in‑person instruction to stay on track.” She added that the government must provide adequate support to mitigate the impact of these closures. (Le Monde, 13 March 2024)</p> <h2>Reaching out to marginalised communities</h2> <p>Many of the schools affected by the closures are located in low‑income areas where students already face a digital divide. The Ministry’s plan to provide remote learning resources will be crucial, but experts caution that without reliable internet access and devices, students in these communities risk falling further behind. The government has pledged to distribute laptops and subsidise internet plans to bridge this gap.</p> <p>Local officials have also called for targeted support. Mayor of Lyon, Anne Hidalgo, said, “We must ensure that the most vulnerable students are not left behind. The city will coordinate with the Ministry to deliver additional tutoring and counselling services.” (Le Parisien, 14 March 2024)</p> <h2>Stakeholder consultations and future steps</h2> <p>The Ministry of National Education has announced that it will hold a series of consultations with parents, teachers, and students to discuss how best to support learners during this period of disruption. Representatives from the Association des Parents d’Élèves (APE) and the National Union of Teachers (SNCT) will also be invited to share their perspectives.</p> <p>In parallel, the government is working on a broader dialogue with protest organisers to address the underlying grievances that sparked the demonstrations. The Interior Ministry has set up a task force to facilitate communication between the police, local authorities, and civil society groups.</p> <h2>Looking ahead</h2> <p>While the protests continue, the government remains committed to ensuring that students have access to quality education, even amid unprecedented disruptions. The Ministry’s plan to provide additional resources for remote learning, coupled with targeted support for marginalised communities, aims to mitigate the impact of the closures.</p> <p>At the same time, the government acknowledges that lasting solutions will require addressing the root causes of the unrest. As the protests evolve, the Ministry will monitor the situation closely and adjust its approach to ensure both safety and continuity of learning.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The decision to close 420 schools on Monday highlights the profound reach of France’s current protests. While the closures inconvenience thousands of students and families, they also underscore the seriousness of the situation and the need for a comprehensive solution that addresses the concerns of all stakeholders. As the protests continue, the government remains focused on safeguarding students’ education and working toward a more equitable society.</p>
