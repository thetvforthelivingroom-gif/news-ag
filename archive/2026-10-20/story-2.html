More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-20
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France were closed amid nationwide protests over the government’s pension reform, leaving roughly one million students without a place to learn. The closures spanned both urban and rural districts, sparking debate about the balance between civil protest and educational continuity. While the number of teachers affected is smaller than earlier estimates, the impact on the national education system is significant and will reverberate through the academic calendar.

What happened

The French Ministry of National Education announced that, in response to escalating protests, a network of more than 400 schools would close for the day. The decision, made on short notice, was communicated to local prefectures and school administrators, who then coordinated the shutdowns with police and transport authorities to ensure safety and order. The closures covered 13 of France’s 18 regions, including Île‑de‑France, Provence‑Alpes‑Côte d’Azur, and Brittany, and affected both primary and secondary institutions. Estimates suggest that about 1 million students and 8,000 teachers will miss instruction on Monday, a figure that represents roughly 0.4 % of the country’s total school network [2][4].

The shutdowns are part of a broader pattern of school closures that have historically been used as a lever in French civil unrest. In 2005, the “Yellow Vests” protests saw dozens of schools across the nation shut down in solidarity with demonstrators, while the 2016 pension reform protests also targeted educational institutions to amplify the protest’s visibility. The current closures echo those precedents, illustrating how schools have long been a symbolic front in the country’s socio‑political battles [2].

Background and context

The trigger for the Monday closures is the government’s pension‑reform bill, which critics argue disproportionately benefits older workers while neglecting the needs of younger generations. The bill proposes to raise the retirement age and adjust pension calculations in a way that many see as favoring those who have already contributed the most to the system. The perceived inequity has sparked widespread discontent, especially among young people who face higher unemployment rates and limited access to social services. The protests have been fueled by a sense that the pension reform is a continuation of a broader trend of austerity measures that have eroded social protections for the most vulnerable [2].

Mobilization of the protests has been heavily coordinated through social media platforms, where organizers share live updates, maps of protest locations, and calls to action. Hashtags such as #PensionReform and #SchoolStrike have trended across France, amplifying the movement’s reach. Local protest groups have worked in tandem with national unions to coordinate the timing of the school closures, ensuring that the message of dissent is broadcast across the country’s educational landscape [2].

While the Ministry of Education has issued statements affirming that the decision to close schools is a temporary measure aimed at maintaining order, it remains unclear whether the closures are mandated by the central government or voluntarily adopted by local administrators in response to the protests. Some prefectures say they are following a directive from the Ministry, while others claim they are acting independently to protect students and staff from potential violence. This ambiguity has led to confusion among parents and teachers, who are uncertain about the long‑term implications of the closures [2].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Students and parents

For students, the sudden loss of a day of instruction is a significant disruption. Many parents have expressed concern about the impact on learning continuity, especially for those preparing for critical exams such as the Baccalauréat. Some parents have taken to social media to voice their frustration, arguing that the closures undermine the educational system’s stability while the protests continue to target public services. Others, however, see the school shutdowns as a legitimate form of protest, supporting the demonstrators’ demands for a fairer pension system [2].

Teachers and school administrators

Teachers and school administrators occupy a complex position. On one hand, they are responsible for maintaining educational standards and ensuring that students receive the necessary instruction. On the other hand, many teachers sympathize with the protestors’ grievances, particularly those who have been affected by the pension reforms themselves. Some teachers have joined the protests, while others have remained on duty, citing professional obligations. Administrators report that the decision to close schools was made under pressure from local authorities and the Ministry, and that they are working to reschedule missed lessons in the coming weeks [2].

Protest organizers

Protest organizers view the school closures as a strategic move to increase the visibility of their cause. By shutting down institutions that are central to daily life, they aim to demonstrate the widespread impact of the pension reforms on all sectors of society. Organizers emphasize that the closures are temporary and that normal operations will resume once the protests subside. They also argue that the school closures are a form of civil disobedience that draws attention to the broader issue of inequality and youth unemployment [2].

Government officials

Government officials defend the pension‑reform bill as a necessary step to ensure the sustainability of France’s pension system. They argue that the reforms will ultimately benefit all generations by securing the financial stability of the pension fund. The Ministry of Education has stated that the school closures were a precautionary measure to prevent potential clashes between protestors and students. However, the government has also acknowledged that the protests have highlighted deep‑seated social grievances that require further dialogue and reform [2].

Quantitative impact

The closures affect approximately 400 schools out of the roughly 90,000 schools in France, representing about 0.4 % of the national network [4]. While this percentage may seem small, the impact on students and teachers is significant, translating to roughly 1 million students and 8,000 teachers missing a day of instruction. The Ministry projects that this will result in a loss of around 10 000 instructional hours across the country, potentially affecting the scheduling of exams and the overall academic calendar. The impact on exam schedules is still being assessed, but preliminary estimates suggest that the Baccalauréat and other key assessments may need to be rescheduled to accommodate the lost instructional time [2].

What to watch (future implications)

The immediate next step will be to monitor whether the school closures become a recurring tactic in the protest movement. If the closures persist, they could lead to a broader disruption of the educational system, potentially affecting student performance and the national exam cycle. The Ministry of Education will likely issue further guidance on how to manage missed lessons and reschedule exams, while the government may consider additional reforms to address the underlying grievances that have sparked the protests.

Another key development to watch is the response of teacher unions and school administrators. If teachers and administrators continue to support the protestors, it could signal a shift in the balance of power between the state and the education sector. Conversely, if the government can secure the cooperation of teachers and administrators, it may be able to mitigate the impact of the protests on the educational system.

Finally, the long‑term implications of the pension‑reform debate will likely shape the political landscape in France. The protests have already highlighted the growing divide between older and younger generations, and the government’s ability to address these concerns will be a crucial factor in shaping France’s social contract. The outcome of the current protests, and the potential for further school closures, will be closely watched by policymakers, educators, and citizens alike as they navigate the evolving dynamics of French society.

The Monday closures are a stark reminder that the fight over France’s pension system has moved beyond the halls of parliament and into the classrooms where the next generation is being shaped. How the country balances the need for social reform with the imperative to maintain educational continuity will be a defining challenge for the coming weeks. This episode underscores the power of collective action and the importance of dialogue between citizens, institutions, and the state in shaping a fair and resilient society.
