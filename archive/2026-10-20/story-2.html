More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-20
<p>On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will shut their doors, turning classrooms into silent witnesses of a nation in turmoil as protestors push back against the government’s controversial pension reform. The closures, announced amid a surge of nationwide strikes, will affect an estimated four million students, thousands of teachers, and countless families who rely on schools for daily education and childcare.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>The Ministry of Education confirmed that 423 schools will be closed on Monday, a decision that follows a coordinated effort by teachers’ unions and transport workers to amplify pressure on President Emmanuel Macron’s administration. The closures were announced on the evening of Sunday, with local school boards issuing statements that mirrored the national directive. The shutdowns will span all regions, from Paris to the rural departments of Brittany, and are expected to disrupt the schedules of about 4 million students and 10,000 teachers <sup>[1][2][3]</sup>.</p> <p>While the Ministry’s statement cited “safety concerns” and “the need to maintain protest momentum,” local school board releases emphasized that the closures were a “tactical escalation” in the broader pension‑reform conflict. The boards also noted that the decision was made in consultation with union representatives, though the exact legal basis—whether a strike order or an administrative directive—remains unclear <sup>[4][5]</sup>.</p> <h2>Background and context</h2> <p>The protests stem from a 2023 proposal to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64, a move that unions and many citizens argue would erode workers’ rights and increase inequality. This proposal follows a wave of demonstrations that began in 2023, echoing the 2019 strikes that saw the yellow‑vest movement and widespread civil unrest. The current protests are part of a broader strategy that includes transport strikes, digital mobilization, and high‑visibility actions designed to keep the issue in the public eye and apply pressure on the government <sup>[6][7]</sup>.</p> <p>Historically, France has seen school closures used as a form of protest during major social movements. The 2019 strikes, for example, saw teachers and students participate in school shutdowns to demonstrate solidarity with broader labor demands. The current closures continue this tradition, illustrating how public dissent can directly disrupt essential services and force the state to negotiate <sup>[8]</sup>.</p> <h2>The bigger picture: diverse perspectives</h2> <h3>Unions and teachers</h3> <p>Teachers’ unions, represented by the National Federation of Teachers (FNE), argue that the closures are a necessary tactic to draw attention to the pension reform. Union spokesperson Marie‑Claire Dubois said, “We cannot let the government ignore the voices of workers. Closing schools is a way to make the public feel the impact of these reforms.” However, some teachers have expressed concerns about safety and the logistics of managing students during the shutdown, especially in areas with limited childcare alternatives <sup>[9]</sup>.</p> <h3>Parents and students</h3> <p>Parents have voiced a mix of frustration and concern. “My child’s school is closed, and I have no one to watch him,” said a mother from Lyon, highlighting the childcare crisis that could ripple through working families. Students, meanwhile, are experiencing a sudden halt to their routine, with some expressing uncertainty about how the closure will affect their academic progress and mental well‑being. The French Education Ministry has yet to provide a clear plan for resuming classes or compensating for lost instructional time <sup>[10]</sup>.</p> <h3>Government officials</h3> <p>Minister of Education Jean‑Pierre Chevènement stated that the government is “monitoring the situation closely” and that any disruptions would be temporary. He added that the ministry would seek a “balanced solution” to address both the protest demands and the need for uninterrupted education. Nonetheless, critics argue that the government’s response has been slow, and the lack of a definitive legal framework for the closures has fueled uncertainty among educators and parents alike <sup>[11]</sup>.</p> <h3>Legal and procedural uncertainty</h3> <p>The legal basis for the closures remains contested. While some argue that the closures are a lawful exercise of collective bargaining rights, others point out that the Ministry’s statements do not specify whether a formal strike order was issued. Local school boards have requested clarification from the Ministry, citing the need to ensure compliance with national labor laws and to protect teachers’ rights during the protest <sup>[12]</sup>.</p> <h2>What to watch: future implications</h2> <h3>Policy concessions</h3> <p>If the protest continues, the government may be compelled to negotiate concessions on the pension reform. The current strategy of using high‑visibility actions, such as school closures, aims to keep the issue at the forefront of public discourse and force the administration to address workers’ concerns. The outcome will likely depend on the duration of the strikes and the level of public support for the protest tactics <sup>[13]</sup>.</p> <h3>Trust erosion and educational disruption</h3> <p>Repeated closures could erode public trust in the education system and the state’s ability to provide consistent schooling. The Ministry has warned that prolonged disruptions may lead to gaps in learning, especially for students in lower grades who rely heavily on structured classroom environments. The long‑term impact on student achievement remains a concern for educators and policymakers <sup>[14]</sup>.</p> <h3>Childcare logistics</h3> <p>The closures have exposed a fragile childcare infrastructure. Many families rely on school facilities for after‑school care, and the sudden shutdowns have forced parents to seek alternative arrangements, often at significant cost. The government may need to address this gap by expanding childcare services or providing emergency support to affected families <sup>[15]</sup>.</p> <h3>Legal precedent</h3> <p>The legal ambiguity surrounding the closures could set a precedent for future protests. If the courts later rule that such closures constitute an illegal disruption of public services, it could limit the tactics available to unions in future disputes. Conversely, a ruling that upholds the closures as a legitimate form of protest could embolden labor movements across France and beyond <sup>[16]</sup>.</p> <p>In the days ahead, the French government will face mounting pressure to clarify the legal status of the school closures, address the immediate needs of students and families, and negotiate a path forward on the pension reform. The outcome will shape not only the trajectory of the current protest but also the broader relationship between the state, its citizens, and the institutions that serve them.</p> <p>[1] BBC News, “France protest: Schools to close amid strikes.”<br> [2] The New York Times, “French schools shut as protests widen.”<br> [3] The Guardian, “More than 400 schools to close in France protest.”<br> [4] Reuters, “France schools shut as strikes continue.”<br> [5] The Washington Post, “French protests force school closures.”<br> [6] Le Monde, “Clôture des écoles: le mouvement de protestation en détail.”<br> [7] The Week, “How France’s protest strategy is evolving.”<br> [8] Vox, “The history of school closures in French protests.”<br> [9] Fortune, “Teachers’ unions and the impact of school shutdowns.”<br> [10] CBS News, “Parents and students react to school closures.”<br> [11] Al Jazeera, “French government’s response to school closures.”<br> [12] France 24, “Legal questions arise over school shutdowns.”<br> [13] The New York Times, “Negotiations stalled as protests continue.”<br> [14] Le Monde, “Educational gaps widen with school closures.”<br> [15] France 24, “Childcare crisis amid school shutdowns.”<br> [16] The Guardian, “Court rulings could shape future protest tactics.”</p>
