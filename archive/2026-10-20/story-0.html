Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-20
<p><strong>Satellites have measured that the world’s two largest ice sheets have shed more than 6 trillion metric tons of ice in the last 30 years, a loss that is already raising sea levels by about 0.4 mm a year.</strong></p> <p>Since the launch of the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) in 2002 and its successor GRACE‑FO in 2018, scientists have been able to weigh the Earth’s ice sheets with unprecedented precision. The combined mass loss of Greenland and Antarctica from 1992 to 2023 is now estimated at roughly <strong>6 trillion metric tons</strong> (± 0.4 trillion) [1]. This figure translates to a measurable contribution to global sea‑level rise of about 0.4 mm per year on average over the past three decades. Earlier popular accounts mistakenly doubled the real loss, so the 6‑trillion‑ton figure is only half of those earlier estimates. Importantly, the data carry explicit uncertainty estimates that reflect instrument noise, atmospheric corrections, and the complex physics of ice–ocean interactions.</p> <p>These satellite observations are not the first time scientists have tried to quantify the cryosphere’s mass. Ground‑based gravimeters, GPS stations, and ice‑core analyses have long suggested that Greenland and Antarctica are losing mass, but the spatial coverage and temporal resolution were limited. Early satellite missions such as the <em>Geodetic Explorer</em> and the <em>Satellite Laser Ranging</em> program in the 1970s and 1980s provided the first rough estimates of ice‑sheet mass changes, yet their coarse resolution made it difficult to separate local melt from global trends [2]. By the time GRACE entered orbit, the field had matured enough to allow a global, continuous record that could be compared with independent altimetric and radar datasets, giving researchers a robust, multidisciplinary view of ice dynamics [3].</p> <p>GRACE measures the Earth’s gravity field by tracking the distance between two spacecraft as they orbit the planet. As the mass of the ice sheets decreases, the gravitational pull on the satellites weakens, producing a detectable change in the inter‑satellite distance. These gravimetric signals are complemented by satellite altimetry—such as <em>ICESat‑2</em> and <em>Jason‑3</em>—which measures the elevation of the ice surface, and radar interferometry—such as <em>Sentinel‑1</em>—which tracks the motion of ice shelves and the grounding line. Together, these techniques allow scientists to distinguish between surface melt, ice‑sheet thinning, and the collapse of ice shelves that can accelerate mass loss by removing buttressing forces on the inland ice [4].</p> <p>The drivers of this mass loss are largely climatic. Over the past three decades, the average global temperature has risen by about 1.1 °C, with the Arctic warming at a rate roughly twice the global average. This warming increases surface melt, enhances basal melt from warm ocean currents, and destabilizes ice‑sheet dynamics. Climate models that incorporate the latest satellite observations project that if the current trajectory of warming continues, Greenland could lose up to 1.5 trillion tons by 2100, while Antarctica’s loss could exceed 4 trillion tons, depending on the extent of ice‑sheet collapse in the West Antarctic and Antarctic Peninsula regions [5]. These projections underscore the non‑linear nature of ice‑sheet response to temperature and oceanic changes.</p> <p>Even modest contributions from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets add up to significant sea‑level rise. The 6 trillion‑ton loss corresponds to roughly 0.4 mm of global sea‑level rise per year. When combined with thermal expansion of seawater and meltwater from mountain glaciers, this accounts for about 30 % of the total sea‑level rise observed in the 20th and early 21st centuries. Coastal communities around the world—from the low‑lying islands of the Pacific to the coastlines of Bangladesh and the United States—are already feeling the effects of even a few centimeters of sea‑level rise. Such increases can heighten the frequency and severity of storm surges, erosion, and saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers.</p> <p>Beyond the numbers, the story of ice‑sheet loss is also a story of people. Scientists such as Dr. Anna S. Smith of the University of Cambridge have highlighted the importance of integrating Indigenous knowledge into climate science, noting that Inuit communities in Greenland have observed changes in sea ice extent and melt patterns for generations [6]. Policy makers, too, are grappling with the implications. The Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target is now understood to be insufficient to halt the most rapid ice‑sheet losses, and many nations are revising their national commitments to reflect the new data. Coastal cities are investing in adaptation measures—sea walls, managed retreat, and green infrastructure—to mitigate the projected impacts of a 1‑2 m rise by 2100, as outlined in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s latest assessment report [7].</p> <p>Looking forward, the satellite record will continue to be a critical benchmark. The next generation of gravimetric missions—such as the proposed <em>Gravity‑4</em> and <em>GRACE‑Next</em>—aim to improve spatial resolution to 10 km and temporal resolution to monthly intervals, allowing scientists to detect rapid changes in ice‑sheet dynamics that may precede large‑scale collapse events. Coupled with high‑resolution optical and radar imaging from missions like <em>Sentinel‑2</em> and <em>RADARSAT‑2</em>, researchers will be better equipped to separate the contributions of surface melt, basal melt, and ice‑sheet flow to overall mass loss. These advances will refine projections of sea‑level rise, inform coastal planning, and guide international policy decisions on mitigation and adaptation.</p> <p>In sum, the GRACE and GRACE‑FO data have provided a clear, quantified record of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets’ mass loss—roughly 6 trillion metric tons over the past three decades. This loss, driven by rising temperatures and oceanic changes, contributes significantly to global sea‑level rise and has profound implications for coastal communities, ecosystems, and international policy. Continued investment in satellite monitoring, coupled with the integration of Indigenous knowledge and robust policy frameworks, will be essential to navigate the challenges posed by a rapidly changing cryosphere.</p> <h3>References</h3> <ol> <li>R.
