Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-20
For the first time since the launch of the Earth‑observation satellites in the 1970s, scientists have quantified the mass of ice lost from the planet’s two largest glaciers: Greenland and Antarctica. Over the past 47 years, satellite measurements show a cumulative loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice—an amount that, if the trend continues, could raise global sea level beyond one metre by the end of the century, displacing millions of people and reshaping coastlines worldwide.[1]

What happened

Satellite gravimetry, pioneered by the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑Follow‑On (GRACE‑FO), has tracked changes in Earth’s gravity field with millimetre‑level precision. By interpreting the subtle shifts in the satellite orbits, researchers converted them into estimates of mass loss from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets. The latest analysis, drawing on data from 1979 to 2026, indicates a net loss of 12.2 trillion tons of ice, a figure that surpasses earlier estimates derived from ground‑based and airborne observations.[1][2]

The rate of loss has accelerated markedly over the past two decades. In the 1980s, the combined ice sheets shed roughly 0.1 trillion tons per year; by the 2010s the rate had climbed to about 0.3 trillion tons annually, and recent years show a further uptick to 0.4 trillion tons per year. This acceleration aligns with the steepening of the global temperature curve and the increasing intensity of atmospheric and oceanic heat transport into the polar regions.[3]

The mass loss translates directly into sea‑level rise. According to the same satellite data, the Greenland ice sheet alone contributed about 0.6 mm of sea‑level rise per year in the 2010s, while Antarctica added roughly 0.5 mm per year. Combined, the two sheets have already raised global sea level by about 4 mm since 1979, a figure that represents roughly one‑third of the total sea‑level rise observed in the satellite altimetry record.[4]

Background and context

The GRACE mission, launched in 2002, was the first satellite pair to measure Earth’s gravity field with the precision needed to detect ice mass changes. Its successor, GRACE‑FO, began operations in 2018 and continues to refine the mass balance estimates with improved instrumentation and longer data coverage. Together, the two missions have provided a continuous, global record of ice mass loss that surpasses the spatial resolution of earlier radar and laser altimetry missions such as CryoSat‑2 and the European Space Agency’s Sentinel‑3.[5]

Methodologically, the gravimetric approach is complementary to surface‑based measurements. While altimetry and ground‑based mass balance studies capture changes in ice surface elevation, gravimetry directly measures the total mass change, including basal melt and sub‑glacial water storage. This dual perspective has helped reconcile discrepancies between different measurement techniques and has highlighted the role of oceanic heat in driving basal melt, particularly in the West Antarctic Ice Sheet.[6]

Uncertainties in the satellite estimates arise from several sources: instrument noise, atmospheric density variations, and the need to separate ice mass changes from other mass fluxes such as groundwater extraction and atmospheric water vapour. The latest analyses incorporate advanced statistical filtering and cross‑validation with independent datasets, reducing the overall uncertainty to ±0.3 trillion tons over the 47‑year period. Nevertheless, natural inter‑annual variability—especially in the Antarctic sector—remains a significant factor that can amplify or dampen the observed trend on decadal timescales.[7]

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions are the dominant driver of the observed ice loss. The rapid decline in albedo as ice melts exposes darker ocean or land, accelerating local warming in a positive feedback loop. Moreover, the influx of warm Atlantic and Pacific waters into the Southern Ocean has increased basal melt rates, further destabilizing the Antarctic ice shelves that buttress the continental ice sheet. Climate models that incorporate these feedbacks project that the current trajectory of ice loss is self‑reinforcing, potentially leading to a tipping point beyond which the ice sheets cannot recover even if emissions are curtailed.[8]

Policy responses have been uneven. The Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target implicitly requires a near‑zero net ice loss, yet current national pledges fall short of the emissions reductions needed to meet that goal. COP26 and subsequent meetings have highlighted the urgency of limiting warming, but fossil‑fuel lobbyists continue to influence policy at both national and international levels, slowing the transition to low‑carbon economies. In contrast, some Arctic nations, such as Canada and Denmark (which administer Greenland), have pledged to reduce emissions from their Arctic operations and to invest in monitoring infrastructure, recognizing the disproportionate impact of ice loss on their indigenous communities.[9]

Indigenous voices emphasize the cultural and subsistence losses that accompany ice retreat. Greenlandic Inuit communities report changes in sea‑ice extent that affect hunting routes and traditional knowledge transmission. Antarctic research stations, while not inhabited permanently, rely on stable ice conditions for safe operations; the rapid thinning of ice shelves threatens the logistical viability of long‑term scientific missions. These communities underscore that the consequences of ice loss extend beyond sea‑level rise to encompass identity, livelihoods, and intergenerational equity.[10]

Economic implications are already materializing. Global insurance markets have raised premiums for coastal properties, and governments are allocating billions for infrastructure hardening and relocation projects. A 1 m sea‑level rise would inundate major urban centers—such as New York, Shanghai, and Mumbai—potentially displacing over 100 million people and costing the global economy trillions of dollars in lost productivity and reconstruction.[11]

What to watch (future implications)

The trajectory of ice loss will hinge on the pace of global emissions reductions. If the world achieves the 1.5 °C pathway, models suggest that ice loss could plateau by the mid‑21st century, limiting sea‑level rise to roughly 0.5 m by 2100. Conversely, a continued 2–3 °C warming scenario could accelerate ice melt, pushing sea‑level rise beyond 1 m and triggering irreversible changes in ocean circulation patterns. Continuous satellite monitoring will be essential to detect any early signs of tipping points, allowing policymakers to adjust adaptation strategies in real time.[12]

Emerging technologies—such as next‑generation gravimetric satellites, high‑resolution radar interferometry, and autonomous ice‑penetrating drones—promise to refine our understanding of ice dynamics and basal melt processes. Coupling these observations with advanced climate models will improve projections of regional sea‑level rise, informing coastal planning and international negotiations over liability and compensation for climate‑induced displacement.[13]

Finally, the scientific community and international bodies must reconcile measurement uncertainties with the need for decisive policy action. The IPCC’s latest assessment underscores that the cost of inaction far outweighs the economic burden of mitigation, and that the window for preventing the most catastrophic outcomes is rapidly closing. As satellite data continue to reveal the stark reality of ice loss, the imperative for coordinated, science‑based policy becomes ever more urgent—both for protecting vulnerable coastlines and for preserving the cultural heritage of indigenous peoples whose lives are intertwined with the polar ice sheets.[14]
