SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-20
<article> On 22 March 2023, Space X launched the first fully integrated Starship‑Super Heavy stack into orbit, a milestone the company had promised for years. The vehicle reached an altitude of about 300 km and entered low‑Earth orbit, but the mission ended after roughly 30 minutes when the spacecraft did not return to Earth. The flight marked the first time Space X’s 120‑meter Starship spacecraft and its 70‑meter Super Heavy booster were launched together as a single, reusable system and successfully achieved orbital insertion. Yet the abrupt conclusion—without a planned landing—raised questions about the program’s readiness for commercial operations and the safety margins of its rapid‑iteration development model.

What happened

During the launch, the Super Heavy booster ignited its 33 Raptor engines, each powered by liquid methane and liquid oxygen. The engines accelerated the stack to a velocity of 7.8 km s⁻¹, the speed required to enter orbit. In roughly ten minutes the vehicle reached an altitude of about 300 km, after which the booster’s engines were shut down and the stack entered a stable low‑Earth orbit. The Starship spacecraft, carrying a 1.5‑ton test payload, performed a few orbital burns to refine its guidance system before the mission was terminated. The flight lasted approximately 30 minutes in total, after which the vehicle drifted into a decay trajectory and re‑entered the atmosphere over the Pacific Ocean.

Space X has not yet released a detailed post‑flight report explaining why the vehicle did not return. Early statements from the company suggested that a sensor anomaly or software glitch may have triggered an unintended engine shutdown, but no official confirmation has been issued. Independent analysts have pointed to the possibility of a guidance‑system fault or a failure in the vehicle’s heat‑shield deployment, but these remain speculative.

The Starship program in context

The Starship program began in 2012 with the first prototype, SN1, which was designed to test basic structural and propulsion concepts. Over the next decade, Space X built and tested 15 successive prototypes, each incorporating lessons from the previous flight. The evolution from SN1 to SN20 reflects a shift from purely experimental hardware to a vehicle capable of carrying substantial payloads to orbit.

Key design changes over the course of the program include the transition to a fully methane‑based propulsion system, the adoption of stainless‑steel for the vehicle’s outer shell to reduce weight, and the development of an integrated heat‑shield system that can be deployed on the Starship’s descent. The Super Heavy booster has been refined to include 33 Raptor engines, a modular design that allows for rapid refurbishment, and a new thrust‑vectoring system that improves pitch control during ascent.

The 2023 orbital test of SN20 was the culmination of these efforts. The vehicle’s 120‑meter height and 70‑meter booster represent the largest launch system ever built, and the flight demonstrated the feasibility of achieving orbital insertion with a fully reusable stack. However, the failure to return the vehicle highlighted the challenges of integrating complex systems under a rapid‑iteration schedule.

Technical details of the flight

The Starship spacecraft is powered by six Raptor engines, each capable of producing 2.2 MN of thrust at sea level and 2.5 MN in vacuum. The Super Heavy booster’s 33 Raptor engines provide a combined thrust of 7.5 MN, sufficient to lift the 120‑ton stack to orbit. Both the spacecraft and booster use liquid methane (CH₄) and liquid oxygen (LOX) as propellants, a combination that offers high efficiency and the potential for in‑orbit refueling.

During ascent, the vehicle’s guidance system relies on a combination of GPS, inertial measurement units (IMUs), and a real‑time flight‑control computer that processes sensor data to adjust engine thrust and pitch. The heat‑shield, a reusable composite material, protects the Starship during re‑entry. The vehicle’s design also includes a “sling‑shot” maneuver, where the booster can perform a controlled burn to reduce the spacecraft’s velocity before separation, thereby conserving propellant.

The 2023 flight’s orbital insertion was achieved after a 10‑minute ascent, but the subsequent 20‑minute orbital phase was cut short by an unplanned termination. The exact cause remains unclear, but the event underscored the need for more robust testing of the guidance and control software, as well as the sensor suite that monitors engine performance.

Regulatory oversight

Space X’s launch activities are overseen by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA). The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation (AST) granted the launch license for the SN20 flight, and NASA’s Commercial Crew Program provided oversight and funding for the test. Both agencies have emphasized the importance of safety and compliance with federal regulations.

FAA officials have acknowledged Space X’s compliance with launch regulations but have highlighted the need for more robust safety protocols, particularly given the company’s rapid‑iteration approach. NASA has reiterated its commitment to ensuring the safety of all launch activities, and has been involved in the technical review of the Starship program’s design and flight operations.

Investor reactions

Space X’s valuation remains high, but the SN20 flight has prompted some investors to reassess the risks associated with the company’s aggressive development schedule. While the company’s valuation has not fallen significantly, several venture‑capital firms have expressed concerns about the potential impact of repeated failures on the company’s long‑term profitability.

Investors have also noted that the Starship program’s cost‑reduction and reusability goals could reshape the commercial launch market, but the early termination of the flight has raised questions about the reliability of the system and its readiness for commercial operations.

The bigger picture

The Starship program’s potential impact extends beyond commercial launch services. If the vehicle can be reused within a few days, it could dramatically lower launch costs, potentially giving the United States a competitive edge over European, Chinese, and Russian launch providers. The program’s success could also accelerate the United States’ plans for Mars missions, with NASA’s Artemis program and Space X’s own Mars vision both relying on reusable launch systems to reduce the cost of sending humans and cargo to the Moon and beyond.

However, the SN20 flight’s abrupt termination has highlighted the importance of rigorous testing and regulatory oversight. The event has sparked discussions about the balance between innovation and safety, and has prompted policy makers to consider updating oversight frameworks to better accommodate the fast‑paced development cycles of private space companies.

What to watch next

Space X has outlined plans for a series of lower‑altitude orbital and suborbital flights in the coming months, with the goal of refining the vehicle’s performance and addressing the issues that led to the recent failure. The company has also indicated that it will conduct a thorough review of its software and sensor systems before attempting another orbital flight.

The outcome of these upcoming tests will be closely watched by regulators, investors, and the broader space community. A successful flight could validate Space X’s rapid‑iteration approach and solidify its position as a leader in reusable launch technology. Conversely, another failure could prompt a reevaluation of the company’s development strategy and its impact on the commercial launch market.

Policy makers are likely to use the incident as a case study in balancing innovation with safety. The FAA and NASA may consider updating their oversight frameworks to better accommodate the fast‑paced development cycles of private space companies.

In the long term, the Starship program’s trajectory will influence U.S. policy on space exploration and international competition. If the vehicle can deliver payloads to orbit reliably and at lower cost, it could accelerate the United States’ plans for Mars missions and reinforce its leadership in the emerging commercial space economy. However, the program’s current setbacks underscore the importance of rigorous testing and regulatory oversight in ensuring that ambitious goals do not compromise safety or public trust.
