Private spacecraft falls back to Earth after failing to rescue aging NASA telescope

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-20
<h1>Private Spacecraft Falls Back to Earth After Failing to Rescue Aging NASA Telescope</h1> <p>In early March 2024, a commercial spacecraft launched from Cape Canaveral in an effort to rescue the aging Hubble Space Telescope (HST). The mission, carried out by Axiom Space’s Axiom‑1 vehicle, aimed to replace Hubble’s worn gyroscopes and power system, but a propulsion malfunction forced the craft to re‑enter the atmosphere and burn up over the Pacific Ocean. The failure has reignited debate over the viability of private in‑orbit servicing and the future of one of NASA’s most treasured assets.</p> <h2>The Legacy of Hubble</h2> <p>Hubble was launched on April 24, 1990, into a 547‑km orbit with a 28.5° inclination, where it has spent more than 34 years delivering unprecedented images of the cosmos. Its original 15‑year design was extended by six Space Shuttle servicing missions, each adding new instruments and replacing critical components. The missions were: STS‑31 (1990) – launch and initial deployment; STS‑41 (1993) – gyroscope replacement and Wide Field and Planetary Camera 2 installation; STS‑61 (1993) – Advanced Camera for Surveys; STS‑82 (1997) – Near‑Infrared Camera and Multi‑Object Spectrometer; STS‑103 (1999) – Space Telescope Imaging Spectrograph; and STS‑125 (2009) – Wide Field Camera 3 and Fine Guidance Sensors upgrade.¹</p> <p>Since the last servicing mission in May 2009, Hubble’s power system, consisting of 24 solar arrays and lithium‑ion batteries, and its attitude control system have been operating on a mix of refurbished and new components delivered by the Shuttle’s payload bay. The telescope’s gyroscopes (devices that maintain orientation) have been replaced twice, but the next set of replacements has been delayed due to the retirement of the Shuttle fleet in 2011.²</p> <h2>Why a Rescue Is Needed</h2> <p>Hubble’s orbit is slowly decaying because of atmospheric drag, a process that will eventually bring the telescope back into Earth’s atmosphere unless its altitude is maintained. The drag force is modest—about 0.1 m/s² per year—but it adds up over decades. In addition, the gyroscopes have a projected failure window of 2026–2028, and the power system’s battery capacity is expected to fall below 70 % by 2029. A loss of either system would cripple Hubble’s ability to point accurately and capture high‑resolution images.³</p> <p>NASA’s Office of Space Science announced in 2014 that it would no longer pursue a dedicated servicing mission, citing budget constraints and the availability of commercial partners.⁴ The agency’s 2023 request for proposals (RFP) for a “Commercial In‑Orbit Servicing” (CIS) program invited private companies to develop concepts for servicing Hubble’s power and attitude control systems, emphasizing safety, redundancy, and debris mitigation.⁵</p> <h2>Axiom‑1: The Private Rescue Attempt</h2> <p>Axiom Space, a private spaceflight company founded in 2012, had been working on a commercial servicing vehicle called Axiom‑1. The vehicle was designed to dock with Hubble’s existing robotic arm interface, a port originally intended for the Space Shuttle’s Canadarm. Axiom‑1 carried a robotic arm, a propulsion system capable of 1.5 km/s delta‑V, and a suite of tools to replace gyroscopes and install a new power module. The design also incorporated a “soft‑landing” capability to allow the spacecraft to detach and re‑enter safely if a fault occurred.</p> <p>On March 2, 2024, Axiom‑1 launched aboard a Falcon 9 rocket. Within 48 hours, it reached Hubble’s orbit and performed a rendezvous, matching velocity to within 0.1 m/s. The robotic arm was then extended to grapple the telescope’s docking port. However, during the final approach, the vehicle’s main propulsion system failed to fire, leaving the spacecraft unable to complete the docking maneuver. Axiom Space’s flight director, Maria Chen, reported that the engine had experienced a loss of thrust due to a valve malfunction, which could not be corrected on orbit. The vehicle was then commanded to re‑enter the atmosphere, where it burned up over the Pacific Ocean.⁶</p> <h2>Immediate Aftermath</h2> <p>NASA’s Space Operations Mission Control Center immediately suspended the CIS program and initiated a safety review. The agency’s Office of Space Policy released a statement emphasizing that “the safety of human life and the integrity of the space environment remain our top priorities.”⁷ The incident prompted a review of the regulatory framework governing commercial servicing, including the Commercial Space Transportation Act (CSTA) and the Federal Aviation Administration’s (FAA) licensing regime. FAA officials noted that the Axiom‑1 vehicle had obtained a launch and re‑entry license, but the failure highlighted the need for stricter oversight of propulsion systems in commercial servicing missions.⁸</p> <h2>Expert Voices</h2> <p>Dr. Sarah Stewart, NASA Associate Administrator for Space Operations, said, “We are committed to exploring commercial solutions that can bring new capabilities and efficiencies to in‑orbit servicing, but we will maintain strict oversight to ensure safety and mission success.”⁹</p> <p>Professor Mark McCarty of MIT’s Department of Aeronautics and Astronautics cautioned that “the complexity of Hubble’s systems means that any commercial servicing vehicle must have multiple layers of redundancy, especially in propulsion and docking.” He added that “the risk of a failure is not just a loss of a telescope; it’s a potential source of debris that could threaten other spacecraft.”¹⁰</p> <p>Space policy analyst Elena Rodriguez, who has written extensively on commercial spaceflight, noted that “the Axiom‑1 failure underscores the importance of a robust regulatory framework that balances innovation with safety.” She added that “the incident may delay future commercial servicing attempts until the industry demonstrates a track record of reliability.”¹¹</p> <h2>Technical Hurdles of a Hubble Rescue</h2> <p>Rescuing Hubble is a complex engineering challenge. The telescope’s docking interface, designed for the Shuttle’s robotic arm, requires a custom adapter to accommodate a commercial robotic arm. The vehicle must perform a precise rendezvous in a 7.66 km/s orbit, match velocity to within 0.1 m/s, and dock without causing any mechanical stress to the telescope’s delicate structure.</p>
