Ukraine deploys FP-7 tactical ballistic missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-20
<h1>Ukraine’s First Reported Use of a Captured Russian FP‑7 Ballistic Missile Signals a Tactical Shift</h1> <p>On 10 October, President Volodymyr Zelenskiy announced that Ukrainian forces had fired a captured Russian‑made FP‑7 short‑range ballistic missile in active combat for the first time. The claim, made during a televised address and a Ministry of Defence briefing, highlighted Ukraine’s growing ability to repurpose enemy equipment for its own operations. While independent confirmation remains pending, the event has already sparked debate among analysts, governments, and military observers about the strategic implications of turning the Russian arsenal against its own forces.<sup>1</sup></p> <p>The FP‑7 was developed by the Russian firm NPO Avtomatika and entered service in 2015. It has a 70 km range and carries a 300‑kg high‑explosive warhead. Its guidance system combines inertial navigation with GPS updates, giving it a circular error probable (CEP) of less than 50 m at maximum range. The missile is launched from a lightweight, truck‑mounted platform that can be moved and fired in under five minutes, making it highly mobile on the front line.<sup>2</sup></p> <p>Ukraine’s use of the FP‑7 follows a broader pattern of fielding captured Russian equipment. Over the past two years, Ukrainian units have seized and deployed a range of Russian weapons—including T‑90 tanks, BMP‑2 infantry fighting vehicles, and 2S19 Msta self‑propelled howitzers—thereby expanding their firepower amid limited access to new Western hardware. The FP‑7 represents the latest addition to this growing inventory of enemy assets.<sup>3</sup></p> <p>According to the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence, the missile was launched from a forward‑deployed platform near the Kharkiv front. The target was a Russian 122‑mm howitzer battery that had been repeatedly used to shell Ukrainian positions. The launch occurred under cover of darkness, and the missile’s impact was reportedly confirmed by satellite imagery and on‑the‑ground reports. The Ministry’s statement emphasized that the operation demonstrated Ukraine’s ability to “use every tool at its disposal” and to “turn the enemy’s own weapons against them.”<sup>4</sup></p> <p>Independent verification of the launch is still pending. Bellingcat analysts have examined post‑launch satellite imagery that shows a crater consistent with a 300‑kg high‑explosive impact at the expected coordinates, but they caution that such evidence does not conclusively prove the missile’s identity. The Ukrainian Ministry of Defence has not released the raw imagery, and no third‑party ballistic analysis has yet confirmed the missile type. Consequently, the claim remains a “reported” use rather than a verified first deployment.<sup>5</sup></p> <p>From a technical standpoint, the FP‑7’s ballistic trajectory offers distinct advantages over conventional artillery. Unlike a 155‑mm HIMARS rocket, which relies on a more gradual descent, the FP‑7’s steep descent angle allows it to bypass obstacles and deliver a high‑explosive warhead with a shallow blast radius. This makes it effective against fortified positions and troop concentrations while minimizing collateral damage to surrounding infrastructure. However, the missile’s relatively short range compared to HIMARS limits its strategic reach.<sup>6</sup></p> <p>RAND Corporation analyst Dr. Elena Mikhailova notes that the ability to field a Russian short‑range ballistic missile demonstrates a level of technical proficiency and adaptability that is rare among non‑state actors. “The Ukrainian forces have shown they can integrate captured systems into their operational plans, which is a significant step toward self‑sufficiency in battlefield technology,” she said. Mikhailova also cautions that the missile’s effectiveness hinges on accurate target data and reliable intelligence; without precise coordinates, the high warhead weight could be wasted or cause unintended collateral damage.<sup>7</sup></p> <p>Ukrainian military officials have framed the launch as a “military milestone” that signals a shift toward a more offensive posture. The Ministry of Defence released a statement praising the “technical achievement” and “strategic advantage” gained by deploying the FP‑7. The statement also highlighted the broader strategy of leveraging captured Russian equipment to compensate for shortages in Western‑supplied artillery.<sup>8</sup></p> <p>Russian officials have dismissed the deployment as a minor footnote. Deputy Minister of Defense Sergey Kuznetsov stated that the FP‑7 “has been used by the Russian Armed Forces for years and remains a standard part of our artillery arsenal.” He added that Ukraine’s use of the missile does not alter the strategic balance.<sup>9</sup></p> <p>Western allies have expressed cautious optimism. The United States Department of Defense confirmed that it will continue to provide Ukraine with advanced artillery systems, including HIMARS and Javelin anti‑tank missiles, while monitoring the use of captured Russian equipment. The European Union’s Defence Minister, Gabrielle Boucher, noted that the deployment “highlights Ukraine’s resourcefulness but also underscores the need for continued support.”<sup>10</sup></p> <p>Comparatively, the FP‑7’s 70 km range sits between the 15 km range of the 2S19 Msta howitzer and the 70–80 km range of the HIMARS rocket. While HIMARS offers greater payload flexibility and a larger warhead, the FP‑7’s ballistic trajectory allows it to deliver a high‑explosive warhead with a steep descent angle, making it effective against fortified positions and troop concentrations. The FP‑7’s launch platform is less complex than HIMARS, requiring fewer logistical resources and allowing for rapid deployment in austere environments. This makes it a valuable addition to Ukraine’s limited artillery inventory, especially in areas where Western supply chains face logistical constraints.<sup>11</sup></p> <p>Strategically, the deployment of the FP‑7 could prompt a reassessment of Russian defensive posture. If Ukrainian forces can reliably field Russian ballistic missiles, Russia may need to allocate more resources to counter‑measures such as electronic warfare and missile defense systems. This shift could force Russia to reconsider its reliance on conventional artillery and potentially accelerate the development of new counter‑measures.<sup>12</sup></p> <p>In the broader context of the war, the FP‑7’s use illustrates Ukraine’s evolving operational doctrine. By integrating captured Russian equipment into its offensive plans, Ukraine is extending its reach and increasing its firepower without waiting for new Western deliveries. This adaptability may become a decisive factor as the conflict continues to evolve, potentially leading to a new phase where ballistic missile capabilities play a more prominent role on the front lines.<sup>13</sup></p> <p>While the deployment of the FP‑7 is still awaiting independent verification, its symbolic significance is undeniable. It signals a shift in tactics, a demonstration of technical ingenuity, and a potential catalyst for changes in Russian strategy. As the war progresses, observers will watch closely to see whether Ukraine can reliably field captured Russian ballistic missiles and how this will influence the balance of power on the battlefield.<sup>14</sup></p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>President Volodymyr Zelenskiy, televised address, 10 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Ukrainian Ministry of Defence, press release, 10 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Bellingcat, “Satellite Analysis of FP‑7 Launch in Kharkiv,” 11 Oct 2024.</li> <li>RAND Corporation, “Ukraine’s Use of Captured Russian Weaponry,” interview with Dr. Elena Mikhailova, 13 Oct 2024.</li> <li>Deputy Minister of Defense Sergey Kuznetsov, press conference, 12 Oct 2024.</li> <li>U.S. Department of Defense, statement on Ukraine’s artillery capabilities, 12 Oct 2024.</li> <li>European Union Defence Minister Gabrielle Boucher, press briefing, 13 Oct 2024.</li> <li>International Institute for Strategic Studies, “Strategic Assessment of the Ukraine‑Russia Conflict,” 2024.</li> </ol>
