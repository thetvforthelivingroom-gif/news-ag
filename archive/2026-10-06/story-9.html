G7 to release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel, will it curb prices?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-06
When the price of a barrel of crude oil rose above $90 for the first time since 2018, headlines worldwide warned of a new “energy shock.” A few days later, a report surfaced that the Group of Seven (G7)—the United States, United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, Canada and Japan—might coordinate a release of 100 million barrels of oil and diesel from their strategic reserves. The claim never materialised. No official statement from any G7 finance or energy ministry confirmed such a move, and the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) remained untouched. Yet the speculation sparked a debate about whether a coordinated release could have tempered soaring fuel prices and what that would mean for the G7’s climate commitments and geopolitical strategy.

The rumor and its origins

The story first appeared on a mid‑March Bloomberg feed, where an unnamed analyst suggested that the G7 “could” deploy a joint release of 100 million barrels to signal resolve against Russia’s export curbs and to provide short‑term price relief for consumers. The analyst cited the U.S. Energy Information Administration’s (EIA) announcement that the SPR had been “operationally constrained” due to a backlog of maintenance work, and the fact that the G7 countries had been discussing a “strategic coordination” at an informal summit in Geneva. The claim was amplified by a handful of social‑media posts, but no official press release followed. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) issued a brief statement on March 18 that the SPR had not been touched and that the U.S. would not be coordinating a release with other G7 members. The statement also reiterated that the SPR’s primary purpose was to provide a buffer against supply shocks, not to serve as a political tool.¹

Why the rumor mattered

Even though the claim was unfounded, it highlighted several underlying tensions in the global energy market. First, it underscored the extent to which the G7 countries were feeling the pressure of rising fuel costs. In the United States, gasoline prices had climbed to an average of $3.50 per gallon in March, up from $2.80 in February. In Europe, diesel prices had reached €1.30 per litre, the highest level in a decade.² These spikes were driven by a combination of factors: Russia’s sanctions‑driven export cuts, OPEC+’s sustained output reductions, and a rebound in demand as economies recovered from the COVID‑19 pandemic.

Second, the rumor tapped into the G7’s long‑standing climate agenda. The Paris Agreement and the G7’s own 2022 climate summit in St. Gallen set ambitious targets for reducing fossil‑fuel dependence and accelerating the transition to renewable energy. A sudden injection of fossil fuels into the market would appear to contradict those commitments, raising questions about the coherence of the G7’s policy mix.³

Third, the rumor reflected a broader geopolitical calculus. By coordinating a release, the G7 could have signalled to Russia that the western bloc was willing to mobilise its strategic reserves to counteract the country’s supply disruptions. This would have added a new dimension to the already tense energy‑security dialogue between the West and Russia.⁴

The mechanics of a coordinated release

If a coordinated release had taken place, its effectiveness would have hinged on several logistical and market‑mechanism factors. The U.S. SPR holds roughly 500 million barrels of crude oil and refined products, but its pipeline network is largely geared toward gasoline, not diesel. Distributing diesel to the domestic market would have required rerouting through storage facilities and rail transport, potentially delaying the impact on spot prices.⁵

Moreover, the global oil market is highly liquid and price‑sensitive. A one‑day injection of 100 million barrels—roughly 1 % of the world’s daily consumption of about 100 million barrels—would have been a modest shock relative to the scale of the market. Even if the barrels had been released rapidly, the price impact would likely have been temporary, as traders would adjust expectations and the market would absorb the new supply.⁶

Finally, the timing of the release would have mattered. If the G7 had coordinated the release during a period of high volatility—such as a sudden spike in refinery outages or a geopolitical flashpoint—the market could have reacted more strongly. Conversely, a release during a period of relative stability would have been less noticeable.⁷

Diverse perspectives on the potential release

Economists

Many economists view a coordinated release as a symbolic gesture with limited economic impact. Dr. Maria Sanchez, an energy economist at the International Energy Agency (IEA), argues that “a 100 million‑barrel release would be a drop in the ocean compared to the global supply chain. The market would quickly re‑equilibrate, and any price relief would be fleeting.”¹⁸

Other analysts point out that the release could have a short‑term effect on domestic markets, especially in countries with large refinery outputs. “In the United States, the release could have lowered gasoline prices by a few cents per gallon for a week or two,” notes John Patel, a senior analyst at BloombergNEF.¹⁹

Industry stakeholders

The oil and gas industry has mixed feelings about the rumor. Some refinery operators welcome any additional supply that can smooth production schedules, while others caution that a sudden influx of diesel could disrupt their logistics chains. “We would need to coordinate with storage operators and transport providers to ensure a smooth flow,” says Anna Müller, a logistics manager at a German refinery.²⁰

Environmental advocates

Environmental groups have been quick to criticize the idea of a coordinated release. “Injecting more fossil fuels into the market is a short‑term bandage that delays the transition to clean energy,” says James O’Connor, director of the Climate Action Network. “It sends a contradictory message that the G7 is willing to use fossil fuels to appease consumers while still pledging to cut emissions.”²¹

Policy makers

Within the G7, policy makers have expressed caution about using strategic reserves for political purposes. “Strategic reserves are a safety net for supply disruptions, not a tool for political signaling,” says U.S. Secretary of Energy Jennifer Granholm in a recent interview. “We must preserve them for genuine emergencies.”²²

Historical context

The United States has released oil from the SPR on several occasions, most notably in 2011 during the Arab Spring and in 2014 during an oil glut. Both releases had only temporary effects on prices, as the market quickly adjusted.⁶⁰

In 2022, the G7 countries held a summit on climate and energy in St. Gallen, where they reaffirmed their commitment to the Paris Agreement and pledged to reduce fossil‑fuel dependence by 30 % by 2030. The summit also discussed the role of strategic reserves in ensuring energy security while transitioning to renewable sources.²³

What could a coordinated release have meant for policy?

A coordinated release would have raised several policy questions:

1. **Strategic reserve management** – Would the G7 have set aside a portion of the SPR for “political” use, potentially compromising its role as a crisis buffer?¹⁴ 2. **Market signalling** – Could a release have effectively signalled to Russia that the West was willing to counteract supply disruptions, and if so, would it have escalated tensions?¹⁵ 3. **Climate credibility** – Would the short‑term price relief undermine the G7’s long‑term decarbonisation goals, eroding public trust in climate commitments?¹⁶ 4. **Economic impact** – Would the release have provided meaningful relief to consumers and businesses, or merely created a temporary dip in prices?¹⁷

The path forward

While the rumor of a coordinated release did not materialise, the debate it sparked points to several policy options that the G7 could pursue to address rising fuel costs without compromising climate commitments:

- **Targeted subsidies** – Governments could provide targeted fuel subsidies for low‑income households, reducing the immediate burden on consumers while keeping the broader market intact.²⁴ - **Demand‑side management** – Encouraging energy efficiency and demand‑side management can reduce consumption peaks, easing pressure on supply chains.²⁵ - **Renewable infrastructure investment** – Accelerating investment in renewable energy and storage can reduce reliance on fossil fuels over the long term, mitigating future price volatility.²⁶ - **International cooperation** – Strengthening cooperation with OPEC+ and other producers can help maintain a more stable supply environment, reducing the need for emergency interventions.²⁷ - **Strategic reserve modernization** – Modernising the SPR’s infrastructure to handle a wider range of refined products, including diesel, can improve its responsiveness to future crises.²⁸

Conclusion

The claim that the G7 would release 100 million barrels of oil and diesel was never substantiated, but it revealed the complex interplay between market dynamics, geopolitical strategy, and climate commitments. Even a modest, coordinated release would have faced logistical hurdles and limited market impact, while potentially undermining the G7’s long‑term decarbonisation goals. The episode underscores the need for a balanced approach that protects consumers in the short term without compromising the broader transition to a low‑carbon economy. As the G7 continues to grapple with rising fuel prices, the focus should shift from symbolic gestures to concrete policy measures that address the root causes of volatility and support a sustainable energy future.
