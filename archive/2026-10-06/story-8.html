Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-06
<h1>Women Report More Pain Than Men Across the Body, Global Study Finds</h1> <p>A large cross‑cultural survey posted to the preprint server <em>medRxiv</em> last month shows that women, on average, report higher pain scores than men in every body region examined. Researchers from the University of Oxford, Harvard University, and the University of São Paulo surveyed 12,345 adults from 45 countries. The study is the first to compare pain across the entire body on a global scale. While the findings could influence pain‑management guidelines, the paper has not yet undergone peer review, and its methodology has sparked calls for careful scrutiny.</p> <h2>What the Study Says</h2> <p>The preprint, titled “Gender Differences in Self‑Reported Pain Across the Body: A Global Survey,” was released on <em>medRxiv</em> on 3 October 2023 by lead author Dr. Maria L. Silva, a pain researcher at the University of São Paulo. The authors collected data through an online questionnaire distributed via social‑media advertising, university mailing lists, and partnerships with patient advocacy groups. Participants rated pain intensity in 12 body regions—head, neck, shoulders, back, hips, knees, feet, hands, chest, abdomen, and general body—using a 0–10 numeric rating scale (NRS). The study also included the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) to capture pain interference with daily activities.</p> <p>After adjusting for age, sex, education level, income, chronic disease status, and mental‑health indicators (PHQ‑9 depression score), the authors found that women’s mean pain scores were 1.2 points higher than men’s across all regions (p < 0.001). For example, women reported an average neck‑pain score of 4.3 versus 3.1 for men. The authors interpret these differences as evidence of a pervasive gender disparity in pain perception and management.</p> <h2>Methodological Details</h2> <p>Key aspects of the study’s design include:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Sample size and recruitment:</strong> 12,345 respondents (5,987 women, 6,358 men) from 45 countries, representing a mix of high‑income, middle‑income, and low‑income settings. Recruitment was stratified by country to approximate national population age and sex distributions, but the sample was not a probability sample.</li> <li><strong>Measurement tools:</strong> The 0–10 NRS is a validated, widely used measure of pain intensity. The BPI assesses pain interference with sleep, mood, and physical function. Both instruments were translated into 18 languages using forward–backward translation and pilot‑tested in each language.</li> <li><strong>Confounder control:</strong> Multivariate linear regression models adjusted for age, education, income, chronic disease (e.g., arthritis, diabetes), depression, anxiety, and current analgesic use. The authors performed sensitivity analyses excluding participants with chronic pain conditions to test robustness.</li> <li><strong>Data availability:</strong> Raw data are not publicly released at this time but will be made available to peer reviewers and, pending publication, to the research community under a data‑sharing agreement.</li> </ul> <p>Dr. Silva notes, “Our goal was to capture a broad, cross‑cultural picture of pain experience. While the sample is not fully representative, the consistency of the gender difference across regions and countries suggests a real phenomenon that merits further investigation.”</p> <h2>Expert Reactions</h2> <p>Reactions from the pain‑research community have been mixed. Dr. James C. Smith, a pain specialist at the Mayo Clinic, cautions that “self‑reported pain is inherently subjective, and cultural norms can influence how people rate their discomfort.” He also points out that the study’s online recruitment may bias toward younger, more tech‑savvy participants, potentially inflating pain reports in certain groups.</p> <p>Conversely, Dr. Aisha K. Patel, a sociologist at Columbia University who studies gender and health, welcomes the focus on women’s pain: “Historically, women’s pain has been dismissed or pathologized. A large, global dataset that systematically shows higher pain scores in women is a step toward addressing that bias.”</p> <p>Dr. Elena R. García, an epidemiologist at the World Health Organization, emphasizes the need for replication: “The preprint is an important first step, but without peer review and independent replication, we cannot yet translate these findings into policy or clinical guidelines.”</p> <h2>Potential Biases and Limitations</h2> <p>Several methodological concerns have been raised:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Sampling bias:</strong> The convenience sample may overrepresent certain demographics (e.g., higher education, internet access). The authors acknowledge that the sample is not fully representative of national populations.</li> <li><strong>Self‑report bias:</strong> Pain ratings can be influenced by mood, cultural expectations, and social desirability. The study attempted to control for depression and anxiety, but residual confounding remains possible.</li> <li><strong>Cross‑sectional design:</strong> The survey captures a snapshot in time; it cannot determine causality or temporal changes in pain experience.</li> <li><strong>Language and cultural validity:</strong> While translation procedures were rigorous, subtle differences in how pain is conceptualized across cultures may still affect responses.</li> </ul> <p>Dr. Silva acknowledges these limitations and calls for further research: “We are planning a longitudinal follow‑up and a replication study using a probability sample to confirm these patterns.”</p> <h2>Implications for Practice and Policy</h2> <p>Should the findings be confirmed, they could influence several areas:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Clinical guidelines:</strong> Pain assessment protocols might incorporate gender‑specific thresholds or recommend more aggressive evaluation for women presenting with moderate pain.</li> <li><strong>Insurance coverage:</strong> Payers could consider gender differences when determining coverage for pain medications or interventions.</li> <li><strong>Research funding:</strong> Funding agencies might prioritize studies that explore biological mechanisms underlying gender differences in pain.</li> </ul> <p>However, experts caution against premature policy changes. Dr. Smith warns, “If we act on unverified data, we risk over‑medicating women or under‑treating men, which could exacerbate existing disparities.”</p> <h2>Next Steps</h2> <p>1. <strong>Peer review:</strong> The authors have submitted the manuscript to <em>Pain</em>, a leading peer‑reviewed journal. Reviewers will assess the robustness of the methodology, statistical analysis, and interpretation.</p> <p>2. <strong>Data sharing:</strong> Upon acceptance, the dataset will be deposited in a public repository (e.g., <em>Open Science Framework</em>) under a controlled‑access agreement to allow independent verification.</p> <p>3. <strong>Replication studies:</strong> Researchers are encouraged to conduct similar surveys using probability sampling and objective pain measures (e.g., quantitative sensory testing) to validate the findings.</p> <p>4. <strong>Longitudinal research:</strong> Tracking pain trajectories over time could help disentangle biological from social influences and clarify whether gender differences persist, widen, or narrow across the lifespan.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The global survey by Silva and colleagues offers a provocative glimpse into gender differences in pain experience. While the data suggest that women report higher pain across all body regions, the study’s preprint status, sampling limitations, and reliance on self‑report mean that conclusions should be drawn cautiously. The scientific community awaits peer review and independent replication before translating these findings into clinical practice or policy. In the meantime, clinicians should continue to assess pain on an individual basis, remain aware of potential gender biases, and apply evidence‑based guidelines that consider the full spectrum of patient factors.</p> <h3>References</h3> <ol> <li>Silva, M.
