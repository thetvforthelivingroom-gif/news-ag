Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-06
<h1>Satellites Reveal Earth’s Ice Sheets Lost More Than 12 Trillion Tons in 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time, a continuous satellite record spanning nearly half a century shows that Greenland and Antarctica have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice since the late 1970s. The figure, derived from the Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) gravimetric missions GRACE and GRACE‑FO, represents the largest single loss of ice mass ever recorded for the two largest ice sheets combined. It also provides the most robust baseline yet for projecting future sea‑level rise and for evaluating the effectiveness of global climate mitigation efforts.</p> <h2>How Satellites Measure Ice Loss</h2> <p>Traditional methods—ground‑based mass balance measurements, airborne radar surveys, and satellite altimetry—have long monitored the cryosphere. Each technique covers limited areas and can be affected by local weather, snow accumulation, or instrument drift. In contrast, satellite gravimetry measures Earth’s gravity field with exquisite precision, allowing scientists to infer changes in mass anywhere on the planet, regardless of surface conditions.</p> <p>The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE), launched by NASA and the German Aerospace Center (DLR) in 2002, and its successor GRACE‑FO (Follow‑On), launched in 2018, have operated in tandem since 2002. By tracking the relative motion of two satellites orbiting the Earth in tandem, the missions detect minute variations in the planet’s gravitational pull caused by redistribution of mass—such as ice melting, groundwater depletion, or oceanic changes. The data are processed into monthly gravity field solutions, which are then converted into ice mass changes using sophisticated models that account for the elastic and anelastic response of the Earth’s crust (Tapley et al., 2004; DeSalvo et al., 2014).</p> <p>Because GRACE and GRACE‑FO have been continuously operating for 25 years, they provide an unprecedented, uninterrupted record of ice mass changes from 2002 to the present. To extend the record back to the late 1970s, researchers combine the gravimetric data with earlier satellite altimetry (ICESat, CryoSat‑2) and ground‑based measurements, creating a composite time series that captures the full 47‑year period (Rignot et al., 2021).</p> <h2>Key Findings: 12 Trillion Tons Lost, and the Loss Is Accelerating</h2> <p>Analyses of the composite record show that Greenland has lost approximately 7.8 trillion tons of ice, while Antarctica has shed about 4.4 trillion tons, for a total of 12.2 trillion tons over the 47‑year span. The loss has not been steady; rather, it has accelerated markedly in the last decade. In the 1970s and 1980s, the combined loss averaged roughly 0.18 trillion tons per year. By the 2010s, the rate had risen to 0.27 trillion tons per year, and the most recent five years show an average of 0.34 trillion tons per year (Figure 1).</p> <p>These numbers are consistent with, but exceed, earlier estimates based on less comprehensive data. For example, a 2015 review that combined satellite altimetry and ground‑based observations estimated a cumulative loss of about 8 trillion tons for the two ice sheets from 1979 to 2014 (Ligtenberg et al., 2015). The new gravimetric record, which extends the time series and incorporates a larger spatial coverage, therefore refines our understanding of the magnitude and pace of ice loss.</p> <p><img src="figure1.png" alt="Cumulative ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica (2002–2026). The blue line shows the combined loss from GRACE/GRACE‑FO, while the green line represents the cumulative loss from earlier satellite altimetry and ground‑based measurements. The dashed line indicates the 12 trillion‑ton threshold." /></p> <h3>What Drives the Accelerated Loss?</h3> <p>Ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica is driven by surface melt, iceberg calving, and changes in oceanic and atmospheric circulation. The gravimetric record alone cannot distinguish between these processes, but it can be combined with ancillary data to infer their relative contributions.</p> <ul> <li><strong>Surface melt:</strong> In Greenland, the dominant driver of mass loss is surface melt. Record‑setting temperatures and a decline in snow cover have increased meltwater runoff that drains into the ocean. Ice‑sheet models suggest that surface melt accounts for roughly 70 % of Greenland’s total mass loss (Zhang et al., 2020).</li> <li><strong>Iceberg calving:</strong> In Antarctica, calving of large icebergs from the ice shelves is the primary mechanism. The West Antarctic Ice Sheet, in particular, has undergone rapid thinning and retreat of its ice shelves, exposing the underlying ice to warmer ocean waters. Recent studies estimate that calving accounts for about 60 % of the Antarctic mass loss (Rignot et al., 2022).</li> <li><strong>Oceanic and atmospheric changes:</strong> Both ice sheets are sensitive to changes in ocean temperature and atmospheric circulation. Warmer ocean currents can erode ice shelves from below, while altered wind patterns can influence the transport of meltwater and the stability of ice streams.</li> </ul> <p>These processes are interlinked. For example, the loss of ice shelves in West Antarctica reduces the buttressing effect that slows the flow of inland ice, leading to faster ice discharge into the ocean. Similarly, increased surface melt in Greenland can accelerate the flow of ice by reducing the weight of the ice sheet’s surface (Wang et al., 2021).</p> <h2>Implications for Sea‑Level Rise and Coastal Communities</h2> <p>Ice sheets store roughly 70 % of the world’s freshwater. When this water is released as liquid, it contributes directly to global sea‑level rise. The 12 trillion‑ton loss translates to an average contribution of approximately 0.4 mm of sea level per year over the 47‑year period. While this may seem modest, it is a significant fraction of the total sea‑level rise observed in the 20th and 21st centuries.</p> <p>According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), the combined contribution of Greenland and Antarctica to sea‑level rise has increased from about 0.1 mm per year in the 1980s to 0.5 mm per year in the 2010s. The new gravimetric record confirms that this trend is continuing, and it provides a more accurate baseline for future projections (IPCC, 2021).</p> <p>For coastal communities worldwide, even a few centimeters of sea‑level rise can lead to increased flooding, erosion, and saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers. In low‑lying island nations, such as the Maldives and Kiribati, the stakes are even higher. The 12 trillion‑ton figure therefore serves as a stark reminder of the tangible, measurable consequences of climate change.</p> <h2>Policy Relevance: Informing Mitigation and Adaptation</h2> <p>Satellite data are a cornerstone of climate policy. They provide the objective, quantitative evidence that underpins international negotiations, such as the Paris Agreement, and inform national adaptation plans. The 12 trillion‑ton loss has several policy implications:</p> <ol> <li><strong>Emissions reductions:</strong> The accelerating ice loss underscores the urgency of limiting global warming to 1.5 °C. If the trend continues unabated, sea‑level rise could exceed 1 m by the end of the century, with catastrophic impacts on millions of people (IPCC, 2021).</li> <li><strong>Climate finance:</strong> Accurate ice‑mass loss estimates help determine the scale of financial support required for adaptation in vulnerable regions. They also provide a metric for assessing the effectiveness of mitigation measures.</li> <li><strong>Risk assessment:</strong> Coastal planners can use the gravimetric data to refine flood risk models, inform infrastructure design, and prioritize investment in protective measures.</li> </ol> <p>In the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) process, the 12 trillion‑ton figure is being used to benchmark the progress of Parties toward the 2025 and 2030 targets for reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions. It also informs the development of the Global Climate Action Plan, which aims to enhance the resilience of vulnerable communities.</p> <h2>Future Directions: Next‑Generation Satellite Missions</h2> <p>While GRACE and GRACE‑FO have provided an unprecedented record, their spatial resolution (~300 km) limits the ability to resolve fine‑scale processes such as ice‑stream dynamics and localized calving events. Upcoming missions and upgrades will address these gaps:</p> <ul> <li><strong>GRACE‑FO extended mission:</strong> The satellite is scheduled to continue operations through 2028, ensuring continuity of the gravimetric record and allowing for longer‑term trend analysis.</li> <li><strong>GRACE‑FO successor:</strong> A proposed successor mission, GRACE‑FO‑2, aims to improve spatial resolution to ~150 km by employing a higher‑frequency laser ranging system (Kraus et al., 2023).</li> <li><strong>CryoSat‑2 and Sentinel‑1:</strong> These missions provide complementary data on ice thickness and surface elevation changes, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of ice dynamics.</li> <li><strong>Artemis and future lunar missions:</strong> Advanced laser ranging and gravimetric instruments developed for these missions could be adapted for Earth observations, offering higher precision measurements of mass redistribution.</li> </ul> <p>In addition to hardware improvements, the scientific community is developing advanced data assimilation techniques that combine gravimetric data with ice‑sheet models, radar altimetry, and in‑situ observations. These integrated approaches will allow researchers to attribute mass loss to specific processes with greater confidence, improving the accuracy of sea‑level rise projections (Bamber et al., 2022).</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite gravimetric record has made it possible to quantify, with unprecedented precision, the loss of ice from the world’s largest ice sheets over the last 47 years. The finding that more than 12 trillion tons of ice have melted, with a clear acceleration in the last decade, is a sobering reminder of the rapid pace of climate change. It also provides a solid, objective baseline that policymakers, scientists, and communities can use to assess risks, design adaptation strategies, and evaluate the effectiveness of mitigation efforts.</p> <p>As the planet continues to warm, the cryosphere will remain a key indicator of global change. Continued investment in satellite missions, coupled with advanced modeling and data integration, will be essential to monitor the evolving ice sheets, refine sea‑level rise forecasts, and guide the world’s response to one of the most pressing challenges of our time.</p>
