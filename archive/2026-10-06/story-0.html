Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-06
<article> <h1>Satellites Reveal Over 12 Trillion Tons of Ice Lost From Greenland and Antarctica Since 2002</h1> <p>For the first time, a continuous satellite record shows that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice between 2002 and 2020. If all of that ice melted, global sea level would rise by roughly 0.41 m (Smith 2021; IPCC 2021). The loss has accelerated in recent years, and scientists warn that a forthcoming “super El Niño” could amplify the trend, threatening coastal cities, fragile ecosystems, and the global climate system.</p> <h2>Satellite evidence: a clear, long‑term record</h2> <p>The data come from the <em>Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment</em> (GRACE) and its successor, GRACE‑Follow‑On (GRACE‑FO). GRACE launched in 2002 and GRACE‑FO in 2018, and both missions measure tiny changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution. By tracking these changes, researchers can calculate the total mass change of the ice sheets (Smith 2021). Complementary radar altimeters on <em>CryoSat</em> and <em>ICESat‑2</em> provide surface elevation changes; when combined with density estimates, they refine the mass calculations (O’Neill 2022).</p> <p>“The combination of gravimetry and altimetry gives us an unprecedented, continuous record of ice loss,” says Dr. Sarah L. Smith, a glaciologist at the University of Cambridge. “We can now see not just the yearly fluctuations but the long‑term trend that is driven by climate change.”</p> <p>Earlier satellite records, such as the <em>ICESat</em> mission (2003‑2010) and the GRACE data from 2002 to 2010, already hinted at a rapid decline (O’Neill 2022). However, the full 18‑year window of 2002‑2020 provides the most robust estimate of the total mass loss to date. The GRACE‑FO mission, launched in 2018, has continued to extend this record, allowing scientists to track changes in real time and to assess the impact of episodic climate events.</p> <h2>What the numbers mean</h2> <p>Over the 18‑year period, the combined ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica is estimated at 12.1 trillion tons (12.1 × 10¹² kg). This figure is equivalent to a volume of water that would raise global mean sea level by 0.41 m (41 cm) if it were to melt completely (IPCC 2021). The IPCC’s 2021 Assessment Report cites a similar figure of 0.41 m for the same period, confirming the satellite‑derived estimate.</p> <p>While the 0.41 m figure may sound modest compared with the 1 m figure that appeared in earlier, less precise studies, it is still a significant increase—enough to submerge low‑lying islands, threaten coastal infrastructure, and displace millions of people. The rate of loss has accelerated from an average of 0.2 m per decade in the early 2000s to 0.4 m per decade in the 2010s (Smith 2021).</p> <h2>Drivers of the accelerated melt</h2> <p>Anthropogenic warming is the primary driver. Rising atmospheric temperatures increase surface melt, while warmer ocean waters erode ice from below. In Greenland, the oceanic front has moved northward, exposing more ice to warm waters (O’Neill 2022). In Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet is particularly vulnerable because its bedrock lies below sea level, making it prone to rapid collapse (O’Neill 2022).</p> <p>Precipitation changes also play a role. While some regions have seen increased snowfall, the overall net balance of accumulation versus ablation has shifted toward loss (Smith 2021). Basal lubrication—water seeping through the ice to the bedrock—accelerates ice flow, further enhancing mass loss (O’Neill 2022). Ocean‑current feedbacks, such as the weakening of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, alter heat transport to polar regions and amplify the warming (Smith 2021).</p> <h2>Historical context and IPCC estimates</h2> <p>Satellite observations are not the first evidence of polar ice loss. Ground‑based measurements from the 1970s and 1980s already documented thinning of the Greenland ice sheet (Rignot 2004). The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) first quantified global sea‑level rise in its 1992 report, attributing roughly 30 % of the rise to ice melt (IPCC 1992). Subsequent assessments—AR4 (2007) and AR5 (2013)—estimated that Greenland and Antarctica contributed 0.6 cm and 0.3 cm of sea‑level rise per year, respectively, up to 2010 (IPCC 2013). The 2021 report refined these estimates, noting that the ice sheets have been losing mass at a rate that is accelerating faster than previously thought (IPCC 2021).</p> <h2>Policy context: the Paris Agreement and the gap in action</h2> <p>The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, set a global goal of limiting warming to well below 2 °C above pre‑industrial levels, with a target of 1.5 °C. Yet global CO₂ emissions rose to 36.4 GtCO₂ in 2023 (IEA 2024), and the temperature trend remains on track for 2.7 °C by the end of the century if current policies stay unchanged (IEA 2024). Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) submitted under the UNFCCC fall short of the emissions reductions required to meet the 1.5 °C target (UNFCCC 2023).</p> <p>“The ice loss we’re seeing is a clear indicator that the world is not on track to meet the Paris targets,” says Dr. Maria Torres, a climate policy analyst at the Stockholm Environment Institute. “We need to translate the science into concrete mitigation pathways—reducing emissions, protecting vulnerable ecosystems, and investing in adaptation.”</p> <h2>Super El Niño: a looming amplifier</h2> <p>Climate models now project a high‑probability “super El Niño” event in 2025‑2026, with sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern Pacific exceeding the 10‑year average by more than 2 °C (CPC 2023). A super El Niño can alter global weather patterns, intensify heatwaves, and increase precipitation in some regions while causing droughts in others (CPC 2023).</p> <p>“A strong El Niño can boost surface melt on the ice sheets by up to 10 % in a single year,” notes Dr. James H. O’Neill, a glaciologist at the University of Washington. “The oceanic warming associated with the event also accelerates basal melt, especially in the West Antarctic Ice Sheet.” (O’Neill 2022)</p> <p>Climate scientist Zeke Hausfather has called for governments to prepare for the super El Niño’s impacts. “We need to plan for a worst‑case scenario that could accelerate ice loss and strain marine ecosystems,” he says (Hausfather 2023).</p> <h2>Impacts on ecosystems and communities</h2> <p>The Great Barrier Reef, for instance, is already experiencing a recovery from the 2019 bleaching
