SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-06
SpaceX’s Starship, the world’s largest reusable launch vehicle, finally entered Earth’s orbit for the first time on 18 May 2024, only to abort a few minutes later. The event proved that the path to inexpensive deep‑space travel is as risky as it is promising.

The flight in brief

At 10:05 a.m. Central Time, Starship prototype SN20 lifted from Boca Chica, Texas. The vehicle accelerated past the 200‑mile (≈ 320 km) mark and reached orbital velocity, placing itself into a low‑Earth orbit with a perigee of roughly 200 km. The flight’s primary goal—demonstrating successful orbital insertion—was achieved. About five minutes after liftoff, the flight‑termination system (FTS), a safety mechanism that cuts power to the engines, was activated and the vehicle was brought down in the Gulf of Mexico. Engineers later reported that the FTS had flagged a fault, but the exact trigger remained unknown because the vehicle was still staging when the abort occurred. Telemetry streams confirmed the event, and SpaceX’s post‑flight analysis noted that the early termination was a planned safety response rather than a catastrophic failure.

The abort provided valuable data. SpaceX emphasized that the flight met its primary objective and that the early termination would help refine the vehicle’s performance envelope. The company said that the next flight would incorporate design adjustments based on the telemetry and that the overall trajectory and systems health were largely as expected until the abort trigger.

A brief history of Starship’s evolution

Starship’s journey began with a series of sub‑orbital test flights in 2023 and 2024. During those flights, SpaceX refined the heat‑shield geometry, upgraded the landing‑leg structure, and tested the integrated propulsion system. The prototype that launched into orbit had undergone a significant redesign of its heat‑shield tiles, intended to improve re‑entry survivability and reduce the risk of thermal failure. The landing legs were also strengthened to support the vehicle’s mass during future return missions. These iterative improvements were documented in internal reports and public briefings, illustrating SpaceX’s rapid development cycle.

Achieving orbital insertion is a milestone for a reusable launch system. Reaching orbit is a prerequisite for reducing the cost per kilogram to orbit, a key metric for commercial satellite operators and national space agencies. A successful orbital flight also shows that the vehicle can survive the stresses of space travel, including the high dynamic pressure during ascent and the intense heat of re‑entry. This milestone is a critical step toward SpaceX’s longer‑term goals of sending crewed missions to the Moon under the Artemis program and eventually transporting cargo and humans to Mars.

Regulatory constraints also shape the launch cadence. Boca Chica is subject to approvals from both the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the Texas Aviation and Space Agency (ATSA). These agencies impose stringent safety and environmental requirements that SpaceX must satisfy before each launch. The company’s rapid iteration schedule has, at times, prompted discussions about whether the regulatory framework can keep pace with innovation. Recent filings indicate that SpaceX has received the necessary permits for the current flight, but the FAA has expressed interest in reviewing the company’s safety protocols in light of the early abort.

Why the abort matters to NASA

From NASA’s perspective, Starship could transform the Artemis program. NASA’s current launch architecture relies on the Space Launch System (SLS) and commercial partners such as United Launch Alliance (ULA). A reusable, high‑payload vehicle could dramatically reduce the cost of delivering crew and cargo to lunar orbit. However, NASA’s contractual obligations and risk‑management processes require a high degree of confidence in a new launch system before it can be integrated into the Artemis schedule. The early abort, while a data point, has prompted NASA officials to request additional flight data and to reassess the projected timelines for crewed lunar missions.

Commercial satellite operators: a cautious stance

Commercial satellite operators have also taken notice. Starship’s payload capacity—estimated at 100 t to low Earth orbit—could enable a new class of high‑throughput satellites and mega‑constellations. Several major players in the satellite market have expressed interest in future Starship launches, but the early termination has caused some to pause their launch bookings while awaiting a clearer picture of the vehicle’s reliability. Industry analysts note that the competitive landscape has widened, with Blue Origin’s New Glenn and ULA’s Vulcan offering alternative reusable options, each with its own developmental trajectory.

Speed versus safety: a recurring theme

The tension between speed and safety is a recurring theme in the launch community. SpaceX’s aggressive iteration cycle—often involving multiple launches per month—has accelerated technological progress. Critics caution that such a schedule may outpace the development of robust safety protocols and regulatory oversight. The FAA’s recent statements emphasize that safety remains the top priority, and that any new launch system must demonstrate consistent performance before it can be fully integrated into the national space launch infrastructure.

Public enthusiasm for SpaceX’s ambitious projects is tempered by the realities of engineering risk. The early abort, while a setback, is also a testament to the company’s commitment to data‑driven decision making. SpaceX’s transparency in sharing telemetry and post‑flight analyses has helped maintain confidence among stakeholders, even as the company faces scrutiny from regulators and industry peers.

What to watch next

SpaceX’s next Starship flight is scheduled for the second quarter of the following year. The company announced that design modifications will be incorporated based on the recent flight’s telemetry. The modifications will focus on the propulsion system’s guidance algorithms and the heat‑shield’s thermal load distribution. Observers will be keen to see whether these changes translate into a longer, more stable orbital phase and a successful landing attempt.

NASA’s Artemis schedule may experience a modest delay if the agency requires additional Starship flight data to satisfy its safety and performance criteria. The agency’s current plan to launch the Artemis III crewed mission in 2025 could shift if the risk profile of Starship is deemed too high for crewed lunar travel at that time. NASA officials have stated that they will continue to evaluate the vehicle’s performance in parallel with other launch options.

Regulatory bodies are expected to release updated guidance documents in the coming months. The FAA has indicated that it will review the company’s safety documentation and may issue new requirements for reusable launch vehicles. ATSA’s role in Texas will also be scrutinized, particularly regarding environmental impact assessments and community outreach.

Competitive reactions are already visible. Blue Origin has announced a new test flight for its New Glenn rocket, while ULA has reiterated its commitment to the Vulcan Centaur program. Analysts note that the early abort could embolden these competitors to accelerate their own development timelines, potentially reshaping the commercial launch market.

The broader context of space exploration

In the broader context of space exploration, Starship’s orbital insertion marks a pivotal moment for reusable launch technology. The early abort underscores the inherent uncertainties of pushing engineering boundaries, but it also provides a wealth of data that will inform future design decisions. The interplay between rapid innovation, regulatory oversight, and commercial demand will shape the trajectory of spaceflight for years to come.

SpaceX’s journey from prototype to orbital vehicle is a testament to the company’s relentless pursuit of ambitious goals. While the early termination of the first orbital flight is a reminder of the risks involved, it also demonstrates that the company is willing to accept short‑term setbacks in pursuit of long‑term breakthroughs. The data gathered will help refine Starship’s systems, improve safety margins, and bring the vision of affordable, routine access to space one step closer to reality.
