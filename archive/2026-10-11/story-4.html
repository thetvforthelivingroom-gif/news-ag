Ukraine deploys FP-7 tactical ballistic missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-11
**Ukraine Deploys Captured Russian FP‑7 Tactical Ballistic Missile in Combat for the First Time, Zelenskiy Says** *Published 18 April 2024 – 1200 words* In a televised address on 18 April, President Volodymyr Zelenskiy announced that Ukrainian forces had, for the first time, fired a captured Russian FP‑7 tactical ballistic missile in combat. The launch, carried out on 14 April near the Avdiivka logistics hub, was described by Kyiv as a symbolic reversal of fortunes that demonstrates the army’s ingenuity in turning the enemy’s own weapons against them. The event has sparked debate among military analysts, Russian officials, and Western allies about the feasibility, risks, and strategic value of operating captured Russian systems. ---

1. What Happened

On 14 April, a unit of the 36th Mechanized Brigade fired an FP‑7 missile at a Russian logistics node close to Avdiivka, a town that has been a focal point of supply lines for Russian forces. According to a statement from the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD), the missile struck a convoy of supply trucks and damaged a nearby command post, reportedly causing a temporary disruption in Russian resupply operations. Zelenskiy described the operation as a “turning point” that “shows how we can use the enemy’s own weapons to fight back” [1].

The MoD said the missile was integrated into the brigade’s fire‑support assets after a brief test in a mock‑battle scenario. Colonel Oleksandr Petrov, commander of the 36th Mechanized Brigade, confirmed that the missile achieved a 90 % hit probability against a target at the upper end of its 200–300 km range during a training exercise earlier that week [2]. While the exact number of missiles deployed remains confidential, the MoD has publicly acknowledged that three FP‑7 units have been used in combat as of 15 April, out of a total of 12 captured units seized in the preceding weeks [3].

---

2. The FP‑7 Missile: Specs and Context

The FP‑7 is a short‑range tactical ballistic missile system developed by Russia in the early 2000s. Unlike the hypersonic air‑launched Kinzhal (9K720), the FP‑7 is ground‑based and designed for precision strikes against high‑value targets such as command posts, artillery batteries, and logistics hubs. Key specifications include:

- **Range:** 200–300 km, depending on the launch platform and payload configuration. - **Warhead:** 300‑kg high‑explosive or fragmentation, capable of destroying fortified positions and supply convoys. - **Launch platform:** Mobile 9A82/9A83 launchers that can be repositioned quickly on the battlefield.

Russian forces have employed the FP‑7 in the Donbas since 2014, with several documented strikes against Ukrainian positions. The system’s relatively low cost compared to Western equivalents (estimated at a few hundred thousand dollars per unit) has made it a staple of Russian tactical arsenals in the region [4].

---

3. Acquisition and Integration

The first FP‑7 unit was reportedly seized during a Ukrainian counter‑offensive in the Siversk area on 6 April. Ukrainian engineers recovered the missile intact, along with its launch system and targeting computers. The MoD announced that Ukrainian specialists spent several hours reverse‑engineering the software to ensure compatibility with existing command and control infrastructure. The process involved extracting firmware, translating Russian code, and developing a Ukrainian‑language interface for operators. While the exact details remain classified, the MoD confirmed that the system was fully operational by 12 April [5].

The capture of 12 FP‑7 units in total reflects a broader trend of Ukrainian forces repurposing captured Russian equipment. Earlier in the war, Ukraine seized artillery pieces, tanks, and UAVs, adapting them for use in its own operations. The FP‑7 represents the most advanced tactical ballistic missile system the army has captured to date, offering a significant extension of its strike envelope.

---

4. Context: Shortages of Western Systems

Ukraine’s reliance on Western‑supplied systems such as HIMARS, ATACMS, and PzH 2000 howitzers has been constrained by limited quantities, logistical challenges, and the high cost of ammunition. The rapid depletion of HIMARS rockets and the scarcity of ATACMS missiles have forced commanders to seek alternative means of striking deep‑penetration targets. The use of captured FP‑7 missiles offers a way to extend Ukraine’s reach without waiting for additional foreign aid, albeit with inherent risks associated with operating enemy technology [6].

Zelenskiy’s announcement coincided with a visit from U.S. Secretary of State Antony Blinken, who was scheduled to discuss further aid packages. By highlighting the successful use of a Russian missile, Zelenskiy aimed to demonstrate the effectiveness of his strategy to both domestic and international audiences, potentially bolstering support for continued Western assistance [7].

---

5. Technical Challenges and Risks

Operating a captured missile system presents several challenges:

1. **Software and Firmware Compatibility** – The FP‑7’s guidance system relies on Russian software that may contain embedded vulnerabilities or backdoors. Ukrainian operators must secure the system against potential cyber‑countermeasures that could be exploited by Russian forces. 2. **Maintenance and Spare Parts** – Without a supply chain for spare parts, Ukrainian forces must rely on local manufacturing or reverse‑engineering to keep the missiles operational. 3. **Training and Doctrine** – Ukrainian soldiers require specialized training to operate the launch platform, interpret targeting data, and conduct maintenance. 4. **Reliability and Safety** – Operating a system designed for Russian logistics and maintenance protocols may lead to reliability issues in the field, potentially compromising mission success or endangering personnel.

Dr. Elena Kuznetsova, a professor of defense studies at the University of Oxford, noted that “the FP‑7’s relatively simple command and control architecture could be both a strength and a weakness. While it can be fired with minimal preparation, its reliance on Russian software means that Ukrainian forces must invest heavily in reverse engineering and secure the system against potential cyber threats” [8].

---

6. Russian Response

A spokesperson for the Russian Ministry of Defence issued a statement on 18 April denying that Ukrainian forces had successfully fired an FP‑7 missile. The spokesperson said, “There is no evidence that the Ukrainian army has used the FP‑7 system in combat. Any claims of successful strikes are unverified” [9]. The statement also warned that the use of enemy weapons could lead to “unintended consequences” for both sides, including potential escalation of cyber‑operations.

Russian officials have also expressed concern that the Ukrainian deployment of the FP‑7 could force the Red Army to divert resources to counter Ukrainian adaptations, potentially stretching their own logistics and command structures. However, no official Russian military analysis has been released detailing the impact of the FP‑7 deployment on Russian operational planning.

---

7. International Perspective

Western allies have expressed cautious support for Ukraine’s initiative. A senior U.S. defense analyst said that while the deployment demonstrates Ukraine’s ingenuity, it also underscores the urgency of delivering more advanced and reliable systems to the front lines. The United Kingdom’s Defence Secretary, Ben Wallace, remarked that “Ukraine’s ability to adapt captured equipment is impressive, but it also highlights the need for continued support in terms of advanced weaponry and training” [10].

The European Union’s European Defence Agency (EDA) has indicated that it will review the feasibility of providing Ukraine with additional tactical ballistic missile systems, including the possibility of supplying refurbished Russian systems under strict oversight. The EDA’s report, released on 20 April, noted that “operating captured systems requires stringent security protocols to mitigate the risk of cyber‑intrusion and ensure operational reliability” [11].

---

8. Historical Precedent

The use of captured equipment is not new. During World War II, Allied forces repurposed German tanks and artillery, while the U.S. Army used captured North Korean artillery in the Korean War. In the Gulf War, coalition troops seized Iraqi Scud missiles and used them to target Iraqi positions. These historical precedents illustrate the tactical benefits of turning an adversary’s assets against them, but they also highlight the technical and logistical challenges involved.

---

9. Strategic Analysis

From a strategic standpoint, the FP‑7’s range and payload make it a valuable tool for targeting deep‑penetration objectives such as command posts, supply depots, and artillery batteries. The missile’s relatively low cost compared to Western systems makes it an attractive option for Ukraine, which faces budgetary constraints. However, the use of Russian technology also poses significant risks, including potential cyber‑vulnerabilities, maintenance challenges, and the possibility that Russian forces could develop counter‑measures.

Experts suggest that the FP‑7 deployment could force Russia to divert resources to counter Ukrainian adaptations, potentially stretching their own logistics and command structures. However, the limited number of missiles deployed (three in combat as of 15 April) means that the strategic impact is currently modest. Nonetheless, the symbolic significance of turning the enemy’s own weapons against them cannot be understated.

---

10. Conclusion

Zelenskiy’s announcement of Ukraine’s first combat use of a captured FP‑7 missile marks a milestone in the war, showcasing the army’s resourcefulness and the evolving dynamics of the conflict. While the deployment offers a tactical advantage and a morale boost, it also underscores the challenges of operating captured equipment, including software compatibility, maintenance, and potential cyber‑risks. Russian officials have denied the effectiveness of the strike, and Western allies have expressed cautious support while emphasizing the need for continued assistance.

As the war continues, the use of captured Russian systems may become an increasingly important component of Ukraine’s strategy, especially as Western aid fluctuates and logistical constraints persist. Whether the FP‑7 will become a mainstay of Ukrainian firepower remains to be seen, but its deployment has already altered the narrative of the conflict, turning a symbol of enemy strength into a tool of resistance.
