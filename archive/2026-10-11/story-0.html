Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-11
<h2>Satellite‑Derived Ice Loss: 4.5 Trillion Tonnes Gone from Greenland and Antarctica (1975‑2022)</h2> <p>A new, long‑term satellite analysis shows that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed more than 4.5 trillion tonnes of ice between 1975 and 2022. Greenland alone lost roughly 2.5 trillion tonnes, while Antarctica shed about 2.0 trillion tonnes. These figures double the mass‑loss estimates that earlier, shorter‑term studies reported, underscoring that the cryosphere is responding to warming at a pace faster than previously believed. The loss has already translated into a measurable rise in global sea level and signals that future sea‑level projections may need to be revised upward. [1]</p> <h2>How the Numbers Were Derived</h2> <p>The 4.5‑trillion‑tonne figure comes from a synthesis of two complementary satellite techniques. Gravimetric data from the GRACE and GRACE‑Follow‑On missions measure changes in Earth’s gravity field, which can be converted into mass changes of the ice sheets. Altimetric data from CryoSat, ICESat‑2, and Landsat track surface elevation changes. By combining gravimetry and altimetry, scientists can separate true ice‑mass loss from surface deformation, snow accumulation, and ice‑shelf thinning, yielding a more precise estimate of melt. This dual‑method approach has reduced the uncertainty in ice‑mass estimates from several percent to less than one percent. [2]</p> <h2>Accelerating Loss in Greenland and Antarctica</h2> <p>Greenland’s ice loss averages about 200 million tonnes per year, while Antarctica’s loss is more variable, reflecting the complex dynamics of its ice shelves and grounded ice streams. The rate of loss has accelerated dramatically: in the 1990s the combined loss averaged roughly 0.1 trillion tonnes per year, rising to about 0.3 trillion tonnes per year in the 2010s. This acceleration is evident in both mass loss and surface elevation decline, indicating that the ice sheets are not merely melting at a steady rate but are entering a regime of rapid change. [3]</p> <h2>Sea‑Level Rise in Lockstep with Ice‑Mass Loss</h2> <p>Global sea level has risen in tandem with the ice‑mass loss. From the 1990s to the 2010s, the average sea‑level rise increased from about 1.5 mm per year to nearly 3.3 mm per year. Ice‑mass loss accounts for roughly one‑third of this rise, with the remainder driven by thermal expansion of seawater and terrestrial water storage changes. The accelerated melt therefore tightens the lower bounds of IPCC sea‑level projections, suggesting that even modest temperature increases could lead to more pronounced sea‑level rise than previously anticipated. [5]</p> <h2>Impacts on Indigenous Communities and Local Economies</h2> <p>Indigenous peoples in Greenland, the Inuit of Canada, and the Māori of New Zealand depend on stable ice and coastal ecosystems for subsistence, culture, and identity. Rapid ice loss threatens traditional hunting grounds, disrupts freshwater supplies, and erodes cultural heritage. Local economies that rely on tourism and fisheries also feel the impact as sea levels rise and coastal erosion accelerates. The new satellite data provide a concrete, quantifiable metric that underscores the urgency of protecting these communities. [6]</p> <h2>Revising National Adaptation Plans</h2> <p>Low‑lying island nations such as the Marshall Islands and Tuvalu are accelerating infrastructure projects to protect critical assets, while megacities like Jakarta and Shanghai are investing in flood‑control systems. The refined ice‑loss data give policymakers a solid scientific basis to justify these costly measures and to incorporate higher sea‑level rise scenarios into national adaptation plans. The United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction highlights the importance of such data in its 2022 Outlook. [7]</p> <h2>Policy Implications for the Fossil‑Fuel Industry and the Paris Agreement</h2> <p>The satellite data underscore the consequences of continued emissions, strengthening the case for a rapid transition to low‑carbon economies. The Paris Agreement’s nationally determined contributions now have a clearer, science‑based target for limiting sea‑level rise, thanks in part to the refined ice‑mass estimates. While the fossil‑fuel industry remains a major source of employment and revenue in many regions, the evidence points to the need for a decisive shift toward sustainable energy pathways. [8]</p> <h2>Uncertainties and the Path Forward</h2> <p>Despite the progress, uncertainties remain regarding the relative contributions of ice‑shelf collapse versus grounded ice melt. Some models suggest that once critical thresholds are crossed, ice‑shelf loss could accelerate abruptly, leading to rapid sea‑level rise. Others emphasize that grounded ice loss, driven by surface melt and basal melt, will dominate over the next century. Continued observation and modeling are essential to capture the full spectrum of possible outcomes and to refine future projections. [9]</p> <h2>Conclusion: A Call for Urgent Action</h2> <p>The satellite‑derived loss of 4.5 trillion tonnes of ice between 1975 and 2022 is a stark reminder that the cryosphere is not a passive backdrop to climate change but an active, rapidly evolving system. These data sharpen our understanding of past trends, improve future projections, and provide a compelling basis for urgent policy action. As the planet warms, the stakes grow higher for communities, economies, and the global climate system alike. The time for decisive action—both in reducing emissions and in adapting to inevitable changes—has never been more critical.
