More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-11
<article> When more than 400 schools across France shuttered on Monday, the country was forced to confront the cost of its protest cycle. The sudden closures left students, parents and teachers scrambling for alternative arrangements, while the nation’s political balance teetered on the brink of a new crisis. The move was part of a broader wave of demonstrations that have rattled France’s institutions for months, and it underscored how deeply public dissent can disrupt everyday life.

What Happened

On Monday, 12 May, a coordinated decision led to the closure of 412 schools nationwide, from the bustling districts of Paris to the remote communes of Corsica and the overseas departments of Réunion and Martinique. The shutdown was announced by local authorities in real time, with municipal councils and school boards posting notices on their websites and social‑media pages at 08:15 h, just minutes before the first bell was supposed to ring. The decision was not made unilaterally by the Ministry of Education; instead, local officials consulted with student unions, teachers’ federations, and municipal representatives of the protest organizers to maximise the impact of the action [T2].

The closures were part of a series of actions that have been unfolding over the past weeks. Protesters have been calling for a halt to the government’s proposed reforms, and the school shutdowns were intended to amplify their message. In the hours before the school day began, students and teachers gathered outside school gates, holding signs and chanting slogans. The scenes were captured on social media, where hashtags such as #ClôtureDesÉcoles and #StopReformes trended across France, spreading the message beyond the physical boundaries of the closed schools [T3].

The decision to close schools was announced on Monday, but the planning had begun several days earlier. According to a statement from the Fédération Syndicale Unitaire (FSU), the student union that coordinated the action, the idea of a nationwide school shutdown was first discussed on 9 May, when the union’s national council met in Lyon. The council agreed that a coordinated closure would send a strong signal to the government and would force the public to confront the consequences of the reforms.

Background and Context

France has a long history of using school closures as a protest tactic. While the current wave of demonstrations is centred on the government’s recent policy proposals, the strategy of shutting down educational institutions has been employed in past protest cycles. In 2013, students nationwide closed schools to protest the “Pacte de modernisation de l’éducation” (PME). In 2018, the “Gilets Jaunes” movement saw school closures in several cities, including Lyon and Marseille, to draw attention to the perceived injustice of the new tax reforms. The tradition of using schools as a focal point for dissent reflects the central role that education plays in French civic life and the perceived moral authority of students and teachers as agents of change [T2].

The current protest cycle began with a series of strikes and demonstrations that targeted the government’s proposed reforms. The reforms, announced in the summer, aimed to reshape several aspects of public policy, including pension structures, labour regulations, and the cost of living. The reforms were met with widespread opposition from unions, student groups, and local authorities, who argued that they would undermine workers’ rights and increase the burden on citizens. The protests have escalated over the past weeks, with thousands of demonstrators taking to the streets in Paris, Lyon, Marseille, and other major cities. The school closures are the latest manifestation of the protest strategy, and they signal the protesters’ willingness to disrupt daily life in order to force the government to address their concerns [T3].

The Ministry of Education, headed by Minister Jean‑Michel Blanquer, has repeatedly stated that it does not endorse the closures. In a press release issued at 10:30 h, Blanquer said, “The education system is a public service that must remain accessible to all children. We are working with local authorities to minimise the impact of these disruptions.” However, the ministry also warned that any unilateral closure of schools without the Ministry’s approval could be considered a breach of the law, potentially leading to administrative sanctions for the local officials involved [T4].

The Impact on Students, Teachers and Parents

For students, the closures were a disruption to their daily routine. Many were left without a clear plan for the day, as schools were closed without advance notice. Some students were forced to stay home, while others were directed to alternative learning centres or community hubs. Teachers, who had prepared lessons for the day, were left in a difficult position, as they had to adjust their plans on the fly. The closures also raised concerns about the quality of education, as the loss of a day of instruction could have a measurable impact on learning outcomes, especially for students in the final year of high school who are preparing for the baccalauréat [T5].

Parents were also caught in the crossfire. Many had to find last‑minute childcare solutions, often relying on informal networks of neighbours or friends. Some parents, particularly those in the public sector, found themselves unable to take time off work to supervise their children, leading to a surge in absenteeism at local businesses. According to a survey conducted by the French Institute of Public Opinion (IFOP) on 13 May, 27 % of parents reported that the closures had caused them to miss work, while 42 % said they had to arrange alternative childcare on short notice.

Teachers’ unions, including the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) and the Force Ouvrière (FO), expressed mixed feelings. While some teachers welcomed the solidarity with students, others worried about the long‑term effects on the curriculum. “We cannot afford to lose a day of instruction,” said Marie‑Claire Dumas, a senior teacher in Lyon. “But we also understand the need to stand in solidarity with our students.” The unions have called for a national dialogue on how to balance the right to protest with the obligation to provide uninterrupted education.

Legal and Political Implications

The legality of the school closures is a contentious issue. Under French law, the Ministry of Education holds the authority to open or close schools. Local authorities can only do so in exceptional circumstances, such as emergencies or public safety concerns. The protests, however, were organised by civil society groups and not by the Ministry. As a result, the closures could be viewed as a violation of the law, potentially leading to administrative sanctions for the local officials involved.

In a statement released on 14 May, the Ministry of Interior, led by Minister Gérald Darmanin, warned that any local authority that closed schools without the Ministry’s approval could face disciplinary action. “The safety and continuity of education are paramount,” Darmanin said. “We will investigate any irregularities and take appropriate measures.”

Politically, the school closures have amplified the pressure on President Emmanuel Macron’s administration. The reforms, particularly the pension reform, have been a major point of contention. The protests have already led to a series of strikes by public sector workers, including teachers, police, and civil servants. The school closures add another layer of complexity, as they risk alienating a significant portion of the electorate that values education and stability.

The opposition parties, particularly the Socialist Party and the Communist Party, have seized the opportunity to rally support. “The government’s reforms are a threat to the social contract,” said Jean‑Pierre Lefebvre, a Socialist deputy. “The school closures are a clear sign that the people are not willing to accept these changes without a meaningful dialogue.”

Historical Context

The use of school closures as a protest tactic is not new in France. The most famous example is the 1968 student protests, which saw thousands of schools shut down across the country. The 2013 “Pacte de modernisation de l’éducation” also saw a wave of school closures, as students protested the perceived erosion of public education. In 2018, the “Gilets Jaunes” movement saw school closures in several cities, including Lyon and Marseille, to draw attention to the perceived injustice of the new tax reforms.

These historical precedents illustrate the power of school closures to disrupt daily life and to bring attention to political grievances. They also highlight the risks of using education as a bargaining chip, as the loss of a day of instruction can have long‑term consequences for students and teachers alike.

The Bigger Picture

The school closures are part of a broader protest strategy that includes strikes, demonstrations, and social‑media campaigns. The protesters are using a multi‑layered approach to pressure the government. On one hand, they are targeting the government’s reforms directly, with marches and rallies. On the other hand, they are targeting the public’s daily life, with school closures and strikes by public sector workers.

The use of social media has amplified the impact of the protests. Hashtags such as #ClôtureDesÉcoles and #StopReformes trended on Twitter, Instagram, and TikTok, spreading the message beyond the physical boundaries of the closed schools. The protests have also attracted international attention, with students and teachers in other countries expressing solidarity and calling for similar actions.

The protest network has also demonstrated a high level of coordination and organisation. Local authorities, student unions, teachers’ federations, and municipal councils have worked together to plan and execute the closures. The coordination involved the use of social media to spread information about the closures and to encourage participation in the protests. The coordination was a testament to the strength of the protest network and the ability of local authorities to mobilise quickly in response to national events [T2].

Conclusion

When more than 400 schools in France closed on Monday, the country was forced to confront the cost of its protest cycle. The sudden closures left students, parents and teachers scrambling for alternative arrangements, while the nation’s political balance teetered on the brink of a new crisis. The move was part of a broader wave of demonstrations that have rattled France’s institutions for months, and it underscored how deeply public dissent can disrupt everyday life.

The school closures are a stark reminder that protest tactics can have far‑reaching consequences, not only for the political system but also for the everyday lives of ordinary citizens. As the protests continue and the government faces mounting pressure, the question remains: can France find a way to address the grievances of its citizens while preserving the continuity of its public services? Only time will tell, but the events of Monday will undoubtedly be remembered as a pivotal moment in France’s ongoing struggle for social justice and democratic participation.
