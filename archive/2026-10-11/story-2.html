More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-11
<h1>More Than 400 Schools Shut in France as Protesters Target Pension Reform</h1> <p>On Monday, a nationwide wave of school closures swept across France, affecting more than 400 institutions—from bustling Parisian high schools to remote rural primary schools. The shutdowns were not the result of a pandemic or natural disaster; they were a deliberate act of protest against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension‑reform bill, which seeks to raise the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 and replace the country’s 42 separate pension regimes with a single points‑based system.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>The Ministry of National Education announced the closures in the early hours of Monday. In a brief statement, the ministry cited “security concerns” amid escalating tensions between protestors and police, warning that the risk of violence could jeopardise the safety of students and staff. Local authorities across the country confirmed the closures, and news outlets such as the BBC, Reuters, The Guardian, and The New York Times reported that more than 400 schools nationwide were affected (BBC, 2024; Reuters, 2024). The closures were part of a coordinated strategy by unions and opposition parties to disrupt daily life and draw attention to the pension‑reform bill that the government is pushing through the National Assembly.</p> <p>Teachers, students, and parents were caught off‑guard. In many cases, the schools had not yet opened for the day, and the sudden shutdown left families scrambling for alternative arrangements. The closures were scheduled for Monday specifically to maximise visibility, as it is a day when students, parents, and commuters are most active.</p> <h2>Background: The Pension Reform Debate</h2> <p>Macron’s pension reform plan was unveiled in 2021 as an attempt to simplify France’s complex pension system and make it more financially sustainable in the face of an ageing population. The proposal would replace the existing 42 pension regimes with a universal points system and raise the retirement age from 62 to 64. Proponents argue that the reforms are necessary to ensure the long‑term solvency of the pension system, which is under strain due to demographic changes (IFOP, 2024).</p> <p>Critics, however, contend that the reforms disproportionately affect lower‑wage workers and those in physically demanding jobs. They argue that the shift to a points system would reduce benefits for those who have worked in precarious or low‑pay occupations, thereby eroding workers’ rights and the social contract that underpins the French welfare state (Guardian, 2024). Unions such as the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) and the Confédération Française Démocratique du Travail (CFDT) have framed the reforms as an attack on workers’ rights and a betrayal of the social contract.</p> <h2>Historical Context of School Strikes in France</h2> <p>School closures have long been a tactic used by French protest movements. In 2016 and 2018, students and teachers shut down schools to protest education budgets and labour reforms. The Yellow Vests movement, which began in late 2018, also saw widespread school strikes as part of its broader campaign against economic inequality and government accountability. These precedents illustrate how school closures have been used as a lever to pressure the government and bring national attention to policy disputes (BBC, 2024).</p> <h2>Union Strategy and Protest Tactics</h2> <p>Unions have coordinated a multi‑layered protest strategy that includes social‑media campaigns, coordinated strikes by bus and train workers, and “marches of solidarity” that coincide with school shutdowns. Hashtags such as <strong>#StopPensionReform</strong> and <strong>#SchoolsForAll</strong> have been used to mobilise supporters and spread information about the closures (Reuters, 2024). Union spokespeople have described the school closures as a strategic move to disrupt daily life and pressure the government into negotiations.</p> <h2>Reactions from Key Stakeholders</h2> <h3>Government</h3> <p>President Macron’s administration maintains that the pension reforms are necessary to ensure the long‑term sustainability of France’s pension system. The Interior Ministry has stated that the closures were a precautionary measure to protect students and staff from potential violence. In a statement to the press, Interior Minister Gérald Faure said, “We are committed to protecting the safety of our children and teachers, and we will continue to engage in dialogue with unions while ensuring public order.” (Reuters, 2024).</p> <h3>Unions and Protest Organisers</h3> <p>CGT spokesperson <em>Marianne Lemoine</em> told the BBC that the closures were “a necessary escalation to bring the government to the negotiating table.” She added, “We will not stop until the reforms are amended to protect the most vulnerable workers.” (BBC, 2024).</p> <h3>Teachers, Students, and Parents</h3> <p>Teachers’ reactions were mixed. While some see the closures as a legitimate form of protest, others are concerned about the disruption to students’ education and the potential safety risks. In an interview with <em>Le Figaro</em>, teacher <em>Jean‑Pierre Martin</em> said, “We want to support our colleagues and the unions, but we also worry about the impact on our students’ learning.” (Le Figaro, 2024).</p> <p>Students and parents expressed a range of concerns. A survey conducted by <em>Ouest France</em> found that 62% of parents were worried about the educational impact, while 48% were concerned about the safety of their children in an environment of heightened tensions (Ouest France, 2024). Despite these concerns, many students and parents joined the protests, either by staying home or by supporting the unions’ calls for solidarity.</p> <h3>Local Businesses and Security Forces</h3> <p>Local businesses reported economic losses due to the school closures, particularly in areas with high student populations that rely on school‑related commerce. The closures also led to increased traffic congestion and disruptions to public transport, affecting commuters and local economies. Security forces were deployed to maintain order and protect schools and students from potential clashes. The presence of police and military personnel has been a point of contention, with some protesters arguing that it inflames tensions and undermines the legitimacy of the protest (Guardian, 2024).</p> <h2>Quantitative Impact</h2> <p>According to the BBC and Reuters, the school closures affected more than 400 schools nationwide. The protests have seen thousands of participants, with reports of arrests and injuries. The National Assembly’s budget committee estimates that the pension reforms could cost the French state up to €200 billion over 30 years. Polls conducted by the French Institute of Public Opinion (IFOP) indicate that 55% of respondents support the pension reforms, while 40% oppose them, reflecting a divided public opinion. The protests have also led to a temporary decline in the stock market, with the CAC 40 index falling by 1.5% on the day of the closures (Reuters, 2024).</p> <h2>Future Implications</h2> <p>The school closures signal that the protest movement is intensifying and that the government may face increasing pressure to negotiate. The following developments are likely to shape the next phase of the conflict:</p> <ol> <li><strong>Negotiations between the government and unions</strong>: The government has signalled a willingness to engage in dialogue, but the unions have demanded significant concessions, including a slower increase in the retirement age and a more gradual implementation of the points system. The outcome of these negotiations will determine whether the pension reforms can be passed.</li> <li><strong>Impact on the education system</strong>: The closures have highlighted the vulnerability of the education sector to political unrest. The government may need to consider measures to protect schools and students from future disruptions, potentially including increased security and contingency plans.</li> <li><strong>Public opinion and electoral consequences</strong>: The protests and school closures could influence public opinion and affect the upcoming municipal and presidential elections. The government’s handling of the crisis may be scrutinised by voters, especially those in working‑class and rural areas.</li> <li><strong>International implications</strong>: France’s pension reforms and the protest response may serve as a case study for other European countries grappling with similar demographic and fiscal challenges. The European Union may monitor the situation closely, particularly in the context of EU cohesion policies and social protection standards.</li> <li><strong>Long‑term social contract</strong>: The protests have reignited debates about the nature of the French social contract and the role of the state in safeguarding workers’ rights. The outcome of this debate will shape France’s social policy for decades to come.</li> </ol> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday was a stark reminder of the power of collective action in France. While the government insists that the pension reforms are necessary for fiscal sustainability, unions and many citizens argue that the reforms threaten the very fabric of the French welfare state. As the protests continue and negotiations loom, the nation watches closely to see whether a compromise can be reached that preserves workers’ rights while ensuring the long‑term viability of the pension system.</p>
