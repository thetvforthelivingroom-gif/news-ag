Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
The last four decades have seen a quiet, relentless loss of the planet’s frozen reservoirs. Satellite measurements now show that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice in the 47 years from 1979 to 2026, a figure that dwarfs earlier estimates and underscores the accelerating pace of climate change. The data, derived from a series of space‑borne gravimetric and radar missions, translate into a sea‑level rise that threatens to reshape coastlines worldwide.

What happened

In 1979, the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) launched the first satellite capable of measuring subtle changes in Earth’s gravitational field caused by mass shifts. By tracking the distance between twin satellites, GRACE could infer the amount of ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica. Its successor, GRACE Follow‑On (GRACE‑FO), continued this work, while missions such as CryoSat and ICESat‑2 added complementary radar and laser altimetry data. Together, these instruments have built a continuous record of ice mass loss that now totals more than 12 trillion tonnes over 47 years [T1].

The figure represents a cumulative loss that has accelerated in recent decades. Earlier satellite data suggested a loss of roughly 5 trillion tonnes by the mid‑2010s, but the most recent measurements indicate that the rate has more than doubled. This acceleration is reflected in the steepening slopes of the ice‑mass curves plotted by the satellite teams.

Background and context

The transition from ground‑based observations to satellite monitoring marked a watershed in glaciology. Before satellites, scientists relied on field measurements, satellite imagery, and indirect proxies such as sea‑level rise to estimate ice loss. Those methods were limited by sparse coverage and high uncertainty. GRACE and its successors revolutionised the field by providing global, high‑resolution, and continuous data on ice mass changes.

The 12 trillion‑tonne figure also highlights the distinct dynamics of Greenland and Antarctica. Greenland’s ice sheet has been losing mass at an average rate of about 0.5 km yr⁻¹, largely driven by surface melt and accelerated runoff. Antarctica, meanwhile, has experienced a complex pattern of gains and losses across its 16 regions, with the West Antarctic Ice Sheet showing the most pronounced retreat. The satellite data capture these regional variations, revealing that the combined loss is not merely a sum of two independent processes but a product of global climate drivers.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

From a scientific standpoint, the satellite evidence confirms a long‑standing hypothesis: rising global temperatures are eroding the world’s ice caps. The mass loss translates into a projected sea‑level rise of roughly 3 mm yr⁻¹, which, if sustained, could add several centimeters by 2100. This rise is comparable to the contributions from thermal expansion of seawater and meltwater from mountain glaciers, underscoring the importance of ice sheets as a major component of future sea‑level budgets.

Policy makers are taking note. The Paris Agreement’s goal of limiting warming to 1.5 °C hinges on reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions that drive ice melt. National adaptation plans increasingly incorporate satellite‑derived projections to guide infrastructure investment, flood‑risk zoning, and insurance premiums. In coastal cities, planners now use the data to model future shoreline changes, informing decisions on seawalls, zoning, and land‑use planning.

There is also a growing public awareness of the stakes. As media outlets report on the satellite findings, more people are recognizing that the loss of ice is not an abstract scientific issue but a tangible threat that could displace millions of people and disrupt global economies. The narrative has shifted from “climate change is real” to “climate change is already reshaping our world.”

What to watch (future implications)

The satellite record will continue to grow as GRACE‑FO and other missions extend their operations. Future instruments, such as the proposed GRACE‑Next, aim to improve spatial resolution and reduce uncertainties, potentially revealing finer‑scale patterns of ice loss. Researchers anticipate that the acceleration will continue unless global temperatures plateau or decline.

In the near term, the 12 trillion‑tonne loss underscores the urgency of mitigation. Even modest reductions in emissions could slow the rate of ice loss, buying time for adaptation. Conversely, if current trajectories persist, the cumulative loss could exceed 15 trillion tonnes by 2050, amplifying sea‑level rise and increasing the frequency of extreme coastal events.

In sum, satellite observations have turned the once‑silent retreat of the world’s ice sheets into a quantifiable, alarming metric. The 12 trillion‑tonne loss over 47 years is a stark reminder that the planet’s frozen stores are not merely passive archives of the past but active participants in the climate system, now rapidly depleting in response to human‑induced warming. The data, while already compelling, will only sharpen as new missions launch, demanding that scientists, policy makers, and the public alike confront the realities of a warming world. [T1]
