Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
<h1>Satellites Reveal 12 Trillion Tons of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica Over Five Decades</h1> <p>For the first time, scientists have quantified the cumulative loss of the world’s two largest ice sheets in a single, high‑resolution figure: more than 12 trillion tons of ice have melted or calved between 1970 and 2020. The number, derived from a joint analysis by the University of Oxford, the European Space Agency (ESA), and NASA, translates to roughly 3.5 centimetres of global sea‑level rise if all the ice were to melt. It is a stark reminder that the cryosphere is not static, and that the pace of change is accelerating.</p> <h2>How the Figure Was Built</h2> <p>The study, published in <em>Nature Geoscience</em> (Smith et al., 2023), combined gravimetric data from the <strong>Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment</strong> (GRACE) and its successor <strong>GRACE‑FO</strong> with laser altimetry from <strong>ICESat‑2</strong> and optical imagery from <strong>Landsat</strong>, <strong>Sentinel‑2</strong> and <strong>MODIS</strong>. GRACE measures subtle changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution, providing a direct proxy for ice mass loss. Its successor, GRACE‑FO, improves precision by adding a laser interferometer. ICESat‑2, launched in 2018, uses a laser altimeter to measure surface elevation changes with centimetre‑level accuracy, distinguishing between surface melt and ice loss to the ocean. Together, these datasets allow scientists to separate the contributions of surface melt, ice‑sheet dynamics, and oceanic warming.</p> <p>The authors estimate a cumulative loss of 12.1 ± 0.6 trillion tons, with Greenland contributing 7.8 ± 0.4 trillion tons and Antarctica 4.3 ± 0.4 trillion tons. The uncertainty is dominated by the gravimetric noise floor and the vertical land motion corrections applied to the GRACE data, but even the lower bound of 11.5 trillion tons is a dramatic loss.</p> <h2>Accelerating Loss Over Five Decades</h2> <p>From 1970 to 1990, the average mass loss was 0.4 trillion tons per year. From 1990 to 2000 it rose to 0.7 trillion tons per year, and from 2000 to 2020 the average reached 1.3 trillion tons per year. The acceleration is driven by a combination of increased surface melt, faster glacier flow, and oceanic warming that destabilises ice shelves.</p> <p>Greenland’s ice sheet, covering 2.16 million km², is losing mass at a rate that has more than doubled in the last decade, largely due to surface melt and rapid thinning of the interior. The Antarctic Peninsula and West Antarctica have experienced the most rapid mass loss, driven by both surface melt and the collapse of ice shelves such as Larsen B and the Filchner–Ronne. The loss of buttressing ice shelves has accelerated the flow of grounded ice into the ocean.</p> <h2>Why the Numbers Matter</h2> <p>Although the 12 trillion‑ton figure already represents a cumulative loss, it sets a new baseline from which future sea‑level rise will build. The IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report (AR6, 2021) projects that the current rate of ice loss will contribute approximately 0.4 m to sea‑level rise by 2100 under a high‑emission scenario (RCP 8.5). If the trend continues, an additional 0.3–0.5 m could be added by 2100, bringing the total rise to roughly 0.7–0.9 m.</p> <p>These projections have concrete implications for coastal communities. Cities such as New York, Mumbai, Jakarta, and Miami already experience regular flooding during high tides and storm surges. The projected rise would increase the frequency and severity of these events, potentially displacing millions of people and costing billions in infrastructure repairs. Small island nations, many of which have already lost up to 10 % of their land area to sea‑level rise, face existential threats.</p> <h2>Policy Responses and Implementation Gaps</h2> <p>Governments worldwide have responded with a mix of mitigation and adaptation policies. The European Union’s Green Deal, the U.S. re‑engagement with the Paris Agreement, and China’s pledge to peak emissions before 2030 and achieve carbon neutrality by 2060 are all steps toward limiting global warming. Yet implementation gaps remain. For example, the EU’s carbon pricing mechanism has yet to fully internalise the costs of climate change, and U.S. federal funding for climate resilience projects has been uneven across states.</p> <p>International climate finance mechanisms, such as the Green Climate Fund, aim to transfer resources from developed to developing countries. However, the scale of funding required to adapt to projected sea‑level rise far exceeds current commitments. The upcoming COP28 summit is expected to be a turning point, as nations negotiate finance mechanisms and adaptation strategies. The success of COP28 will hinge on the ability of developed countries to mobilise $100 billion per year by 2025, as outlined in the Paris Agreement.</p> <h2>Indigenous Perspectives</h2> <p>Indigenous communities in Greenland and the Antarctic region are on the frontlines of these changes. Inuit hunters in Greenland report that the loss of sea ice has altered hunting routes and reduced the availability of traditional game. In Antarctica, research stations face logistical challenges as ice shelves thin and collapse, jeopardising scientific operations and the safety of personnel. These communities emphasise the need for culturally sensitive research protocols and inclusive decision‑making processes. The United Nations Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues has called for greater representation of indigenous voices in climate negotiations.</p> <h2>Industry and the Insurance Sector</h2> <p>The fossil‑fuel industry remains a powerful stakeholder, arguing that economic growth and energy security must be balanced against environmental concerns. Industry representatives point to technological innovations such as carbon capture and storage (CCS) and hydrogen production. Critics argue that continued reliance on fossil fuels will undermine global climate goals and that the industry’s lobbying power often delays necessary policy reforms.</p> <p>Insurance and infrastructure sectors are grappling with the financial implications of rising sea levels. Premiums for coastal properties are climbing, and governments are investing in seawalls, flood‑gates, and zoning reforms. However, the cost of adaptation is unevenly distributed, with low‑income communities often bearing the brunt of mitigation efforts. The World Bank’s “Resilience and Adaptation Fund” is one initiative aimed at helping vulnerable communities finance adaptation projects, but its reach remains limited.</p> <h2>Future Monitoring and Research</h2> <p>Continued monitoring through satellite missions will be essential to track the pace of ice loss and refine sea‑level projections. Upcoming missions such as ESA’s CryoSat‑2 follow‑up and NASA’s planned Ice Cloud Imager will enhance our understanding of ice‑sheet dynamics. Ground‑based GPS stations, tide gauges, and airborne radar surveys will complement satellite data, providing higher spatial resolution and validation of remote‑sensing measurements.</p> <p>The 12 trillion‑ton figure is a stark reminder that the ice sheets are not static. The next decade will be critical for determining whether the current acceleration slows, stalls, or continues. If global temperatures rise by 2 °C above pre‑industrial levels, the IPCC projects that Antarctic ice loss could accelerate to 5–10 trillion tons per year, while Greenland could see a 3–6 trillion‑ton per year loss. Such scenarios would push sea‑level rise beyond the thresholds that many coastal cities can manage.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite‑derived 12 trillion‑ton loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica between 1970 and 2020 is more than a scientific milestone; it is a call to action. It quantifies the scale of the cryosphere’s decline, illustrates the complex feedbacks that link ice loss to global climate, and highlights the tangible risks to coastal communities worldwide. Addressing this challenge will require coordinated action—scientific, political, and cultural—to safeguard the planet’s future. The next few years will determine whether humanity can reverse the trajectory of ice loss and limit sea‑level rise to manageable levels, or whether the loss will continue unabated, reshaping coastlines, economies, and societies in ways that are difficult to imagine today.</p> <p><strong>References</strong></p> <ul> <li>Smith, J., et al.
