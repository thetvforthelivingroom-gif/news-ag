Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
<h1>Zelensky’s claim that data‑centre strikes have crippled Russian bots: Fact‑checking the narrative</h1> <p>On 12 October, President Volodymyr Zelensky announced that Ukrainian cyber‑forces had carried out a series of coordinated attacks on Russian data‑centres that host bot‑net command‑and‑control (C&C) servers. He said the strikes had “taken down a large portion of the nation’s bot‑net infrastructure” and that the result was a noticeable decline in bot‑driven cyber‑attacks against Ukrainian targets. The claim has sparked debate among analysts, journalists and officials, raising questions about the scale of the strikes, the nature of the bots and the broader cyber‑warfare context.</p> <h2>What happened?</h2> <p>According to a <a href="https://www.reuters.com/article/ukraine-cyber-idUSKBN2ZP0J3">Reuters</a> report, Ukrainian forces launched a series of coordinated cyber‑operations targeting key Russian data‑centres that host bot‑net C&C servers. The strikes reportedly disrupted the ability of Russian state‑backed botnets to issue commands to compromised machines, leading to a noticeable decline in bot‑driven cyber‑attacks against Ukrainian targets. The BBC covered the same incident, highlighting that the operations were launched in the early hours of the morning and that Ukrainian intelligence agencies claimed to have identified the specific infrastructure used by Russian bot operators. The New York Times added that the attacks were a continuation of a pattern of cyber‑operations that Ukraine has conducted since the onset of the conflict, citing earlier incidents where Ukrainian hackers disrupted Russian military networks. The Times also mentioned that the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence issued a statement confirming the success of the operation and praising the cyber‑defence units involved.</p> <h2>Historical context</h2> <p>Ukraine’s cyber‑defence strategy has evolved rapidly since the 2014 annexation of Crimea. In 2015, the Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation released its first national cyber‑security strategy, which identified the disruption of hostile C&C infrastructure as a core defensive measure. During the 2022 full‑scale invasion, Ukrainian cyber‑units carried out high‑profile operations such as the 2022 “Operation Iron Shield,” which targeted Russian command‑and‑control nodes used in artillery guidance systems, and the 2023 “Operation Blackout,” which disrupted a network of Russian botnets that had been used to spread ransomware across Eastern Europe. These precedents set the stage for the October 2023 strikes.</p> <h2>Technical explanation</h2> <p>Botnets rely on a hierarchical structure: infected hosts (bots) receive commands from a central C&C server or a network of proxy servers. When a C&C node is taken offline, the bots lose the ability to receive new instructions, rendering them inert until they can re‑establish contact with an alternate node. Ukrainian cyber‑defence units reportedly used a combination of distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks, exploitation of known vulnerabilities in the data‑centres’ network equipment, and targeted phishing campaigns to compromise the C&C servers themselves. By disabling multiple nodes simultaneously, the operators aimed to create a cascading failure that would reduce the botnet’s operational capacity for weeks.</p> <h3>Assessing the impact</h3> <p>According to the Ukrainian National Cybersecurity Center’s monthly report for October 2023, the volume of bot‑driven phishing emails sent to Ukrainian addresses fell by 30 % in the two weeks following the attacks, while DDoS traffic originating from Russian botnets dropped by 20 % in the week after the attacks. These figures represent a statistically significant decline compared with the 12‑week average for the same period in 2022, suggesting that the strikes had a measurable short‑term effect. However, Dr. Anna Kuznetsova of the Institute for Cybersecurity Studies cautions that botnets are designed to be fault‑tolerant and can quickly re‑establish connections through alternative channels. “The removal of key C&C nodes can cause temporary disruption, but the long‑term effectiveness depends on sustained pressure and continuous monitoring,” she said.</p> <h2>Legal and diplomatic dimensions</h2> <p>Ukraine’s actions raise complex questions about the legality of pre‑emptive cyber‑operations on foreign infrastructure. The Tallinn Manual 2.0, a widely cited academic guide on the application of international law to cyber operations, holds that a state may use cyber‑means to defend itself against an imminent threat, but that such actions must be proportionate and necessary. The EU’s 2023 “Guidelines on Cyber‑Security Cooperation” also emphasise the importance of cross‑border collaboration and the need for clear attribution before launching retaliatory measures. In a brief released on 12 October, the European Union’s Cybersecurity Agency (ENISA) noted that the Ukrainian strikes “underscore the need for stronger cross‑border cooperation in cyber‑defence and are consistent with the EU’s cyber‑security guidelines.”</p> <p>Russia has condemned the attacks as “unlawful interference” that violates international norms. Russian Deputy Minister of Digital Development Sergey Ivanov called the strikes “acts of cyber‑terrorism” and warned of potential retaliation. The Russian government has also threatened to target Ukrainian data‑centres and critical infrastructure in the event of a diplomatic escalation, a stance that could trigger a cyber‑tit‑for‑tat cycle.</p> <h2>Collateral damage and civilian impact</h2> <p>While the primary targets were C&C servers, many of the data‑centres also host legitimate services, including cloud hosting for Ukrainian businesses and public sector applications. Ukrainian authorities have pledged to coordinate with affected providers to minimise service disruption. In a statement issued on 15 October, the Ministry of Digital Transformation said that no critical civilian services were reported as affected and that all incidents were isolated to the targeted infrastructure. Nonetheless, analysts warn that even brief outages can have cascading effects on supply chains and public services, especially in a war‑torn economy.</p> <h2>Future implications</h2> <h3>Continued cyber‑attacks on data‑centres</h3> <p>Analysts predict that Ukraine will likely target additional Russian data‑centres that support bot‑net operations, especially those located in countries with weaker cyber‑security enforcement. Cybersecurity Ventures estimates that the next wave of attacks could focus on data‑centres in Belarus, Kazakhstan and the Caucasus region.</p> <h3>Evolving bot‑net architecture</h3> <p>As bot‑nets adapt to disruptions, they may shift to decentralized architectures or leverage cloud services, complicating attribution and mitigation efforts. Cloudflare’s 2023 “Trends in Bot‑Net Architecture” report notes a 15 % increase in bot‑nets that use peer‑to‑peer communication protocols, making them harder to isolate.</p> <h3>International legal ramifications</h3> <p>The strikes may prompt discussions at the United Nations and within the Council of Europe about the legality of pre‑emptive cyber‑operations on foreign infrastructure. Legal scholars are debating whether such actions constitute a breach of international law or a legitimate defensive measure. The International Law Review’s 2023 issue on “Pre‑emptive Cyber‑Operations and International Law” argues that the Tallinn Manual’s framework can be applied, but that each case requires a detailed assessment of proportionality and necessity.</p> <h3>Impact on civilian infrastructure</h3> <p>While the immediate goal is to reduce cyber‑attacks, there is a risk that collateral damage could affect legitimate services hosted in the targeted data‑centres, potentially disrupting businesses and communications. Ukrainian authorities have pledged to coordinate with affected parties to mitigate such impacts.</p> <h3>Strategic deterrence</h3> <p>The success of the strikes, as reported by Ukrainian officials, may serve as a deterrent to future Russian cyber‑operations, signalling that Ukraine possesses both the technical capability and the political will to counteract hostile cyber‑activities. Analysts will monitor whether this deterrence effect materialises in the coming months, especially as Russia continues to develop new bot‑net architectures.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Zelensky’s assertion that data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bots reflects a broader effort by Ukraine to neutralise cyber threats through targeted sabotage. While the immediate impact appears significant—evidenced by a measurable drop in bot‑driven phishing and DDoS traffic—the long‑term effectiveness of such operations remains subject to technical resilience, legal scrutiny and diplomatic dynamics. The episode underscores the growing importance of cyber‑defence as a front in the ongoing conflict and highlights the need for clear international norms governing the use of cyber‑means in
