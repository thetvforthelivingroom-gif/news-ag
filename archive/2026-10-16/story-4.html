Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
When President Volodymyr Zelensky said that Ukrainian cyber‑strikes had “taken down Russian bots,” the claim sparked debate across media, policy circles and the cyber‑security community. The statement sits at the crossroads of information warfare, international law and the challenge of verifying covert actions in a war zone. To assess its truth, we must explain the terms, review the evidence, and consider the wider implications for cyber‑warfare norms.

What happened

“Data‑centre strikes” refer to a series of targeted cyber‑operations launched by Ukrainian forces against Russian hosting and cloud infrastructure. On 12 September 2023, Reuters reported that Ukrainian hackers had disrupted services at several Russian data centres, causing outages that were visible on internet‑infrastructure monitoring tools such as BGPmon and the Internet Routing Registry (IRR) database [1]. The BBC echoed these findings, noting that the attacks were part of a broader strategy to undermine Russia’s digital capabilities during the ongoing war [2]. A press release from the Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation on 13 September 2023 claimed that the strikes severed the command‑and‑control channels of several botnets used for propaganda and automated cyber‑attacks [3].

The evidence trail is patchy. Independent monitoring of BGP announcements shows a temporary drop in routes originating from the affected Russian data centres for 12–15 September 2023, after which the routes were restored [4]. Traffic analysis on the Russian social‑media platform VKontakte (VK) revealed a 30 % reduction in bot‑generated posts during the same period, according to a report by The Washington Post [5]. However, the Post cautions that correlation does not equal causation and that other factors—such as increased counter‑measures by Russian operators—could also explain the dip [5]. A fact‑checking piece by Snopes (15 September 2023) notes that while there are credible reports of outages, concrete evidence linking them directly to Ukrainian cyber‑operations remains limited [6].

In contrast, Russian officials have denied any significant impact. A spokesperson for the Russian Ministry of Digital Development, Communications and Mass Media issued a statement on 16 September 2023 claiming that the data centres remained fully operational and that the reported outages were “isolated incidents caused by routine maintenance” [7]. Al Jazeera’s analysis of Russian cyber‑defence rhetoric stresses that the Kremlin has historically dismissed external claims of cyber damage to avoid admitting vulnerability, especially during wartime [8].

Background and context

The use of cyber‑operations to disrupt an adversary’s information infrastructure is not new. In 2007, Estonia suffered a massive wave of coordinated cyber‑attacks that knocked out government websites and banking services, prompting the country to accelerate its cyber‑defence posture [9]. Two years later, the 2016 U.S. election interference campaign revealed how state actors could leverage botnets to spread disinformation on a global scale, raising questions about the resilience of democratic institutions to cyber influence operations [10]. In 2022, as the Russian invasion of Ukraine unfolded, both sides engaged in a flurry of cyber‑attacks: Ukrainian forces targeted Russian military communications, while Russian hackers launched attacks on Ukrainian infrastructure, including power grids and transportation networks [11].

These historical parallels underscore a pattern: when conventional military options are limited or asymmetrical, states increasingly turn to cyber‑means to level the playing field. The Ukrainian claim that data‑centre strikes have “taken down Russian bots” fits into this broader strategy of leveraging digital asymmetry to offset Russia’s larger conventional military advantage. However, the effectiveness of such operations depends on the ability to maintain sustained pressure, to avoid retaliation, and to prevent collateral damage to civilian infrastructure—a concern that has become central to international discussions on cyber‑warfare norms.

Legal and ethical considerations

From a legal standpoint, cyber‑attacks that target civilian infrastructure raise complex questions under international humanitarian law. The United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) has begun to discuss the applicability of existing norms to cyber‑attacks, but no definitive treaty categorically forbids attacks on data centres that may also serve civilian purposes [12]. Lawfare’s analysis points out that while targeting purely military infrastructure is generally accepted, attacks that risk disrupting essential services—such as internet connectivity for civilians—could be viewed as disproportionate or unlawful if they cause undue harm [13].

Cyber‑security experts offer a more nuanced view. A Politico piece featuring analysts from the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) argues that while Ukrainian strikes may have temporarily disrupted Russian botnets, the long‑term impact is uncertain because bot operators can quickly re‑establish command‑and‑control channels elsewhere [14]. The same article notes that the Ukrainian strategy may be more about signalling deterrence than achieving decisive technical damage. Meanwhile, the Financial Times reports that Russian cyber‑defence units have been rapidly rebuilding their botnet infrastructure, suggesting that the damage inflicted may be short‑lived [15].

NGOs and civil society groups raise concerns about the broader societal impact. The European Union’s Cybersecurity Agency (ENISA) warns that large‑scale cyber‑attacks on data centres can inadvertently affect commercial services, potentially harming businesses and consumers who rely on cloud computing for everyday operations [16]. In a statement, ENISA called for a “code of conduct” that would limit the use of cyber‑attacks against civilian infrastructure, even in the context of armed conflict.

Russian response and the defensive spiral

Russian officials frame the Ukrainian cyber‑attacks as part of a broader “information warfare” campaign that seeks to destabilise the country’s digital ecosystem. The Moscow Times reports that Russian cyber‑defence agencies have responded by hardening their data centres, deploying additional intrusion detection systems, and accelerating the migration of critical services to more secure infrastructure [17]. This response illustrates how cyber‑attacks can provoke a defensive spiral, potentially escalating the conflict in the cyber domain.

Ukrainian officials maintain that the data centre strikes have delivered a strategic blow to Russia’s information operations. In a televised address, President Zelensky emphasised that the attacks had “disrupted the coordination of thousands of bots that spread propaganda and disinformation” [18]. He also highlighted that the strikes were part of a broader “information shield” aimed at protecting Ukrainian society from Russian influence operations. While the Ukrainian narrative frames the cyber‑operations as a necessary counter‑measure, independent observers caution that the lack of verifiable data makes it difficult to assess the true scale of the impact.

What to watch: future implications

The possibility of cyber escalation looms large. If Russian botnets recover quickly, Ukraine may feel compelled to launch further strikes, potentially targeting more critical infrastructure. This could trigger a retaliatory cycle that spills over into civilian sectors, raising the stakes for international norms on cyber‑warfare. The United Nations has already called for a “rules‑based approach” to cyber‑conflict, but the absence of enforceable treaties means that states can still pursue aggressive cyber tactics with limited accountability [19].

The broader implications for cyber‑warfare norms are significant. As more states adopt asymmetric cyber‑strategies, the line between legitimate military operations and unlawful attacks on civilian infrastructure blurs. The Ukrainian case could serve as a precedent, encouraging other nations to employ similar tactics. At the same time, the international community may push for clearer guidelines on acceptable conduct in cyberspace, especially regarding attacks that could disrupt essential services.

From a technical perspective, the effectiveness of data‑centre strikes depends on the ability to maintain persistent pressure and to prevent adversaries from quickly rebuilding their botnets. Analysts from CSIS suggest that the Ukrainian cyber‑team’s use of supply‑chain attacks and phishing may have temporarily disrupted command‑and‑control, but the long‑term resilience of Russian botnets remains uncertain [20]. If Russia can re‑establish its botnet infrastructure rapidly, the strategic advantage gained by Ukraine may be marginal.

The need for independent verification

The debate over whether Ukrainian data‑centre strikes have truly “taken down Russian bots” underscores the difficulty of verifying cyber‑operations in wartime. While monitoring tools such as BGPmon and traffic‑analysis platforms provide useful signals, they cannot alone confirm causality. Independent, third‑party verification—ideally from neutral cyber‑security researchers or international organisations—would lend greater credibility to claims of technical impact. Until such verification is available, the narrative remains a mix of plausible inference and unverified assertion.

In the meantime, the international community must grapple with the legal and ethical questions that arise when states target digital infrastructure that overlaps with civilian services. The Ukrainian case offers a cautionary tale: even well‑intentioned cyber‑operations can blur the line between legitimate defence and unlawful collateral damage. As the war continues, the stakes for cyber‑warfare norms will only rise, making transparent verification and robust legal frameworks more essential than ever.
