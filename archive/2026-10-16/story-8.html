SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
When Space X’s Starship prototype finally reached orbital velocity, the world cheered. Within minutes, however, the vehicle’s flight ended abruptly, reminding us that the path to reusable space remains fraught with risk. The event, which unfolded over roughly twelve minutes from launch to abort, was both a milestone and a cautionary tale for the growing commercial space industry.

What happened

On the morning of 15 March 2024, Space X launched Starship SN15 from its Vandenberg Space Force Base launch complex in California. The 120‑tonne, fully reusable vehicle accelerated under the combined thrust of its Super‑Heavy booster and Starship’s nine Raptor engines, reaching an orbital velocity of about 7.8 km/s and a peak altitude of roughly 400 km above Earth’s surface [T2]. The flight, lasting just over twelve minutes, was intended to demonstrate the vehicle’s ability to reach low‑Earth orbit and then return to a safe splashdown.

Sometime after the twelve‑minute mark, the vehicle’s onboard safety system triggered an abort sequence. The craft lost altitude and descended toward the Pacific Ocean, where it splashed down in a controlled manner. Telemetry and ground‑based tracking confirmed the abort, and the recovery team retrieved the craft without damage to its structure or propulsion systems [T3].

The cause of the abort remains uncertain. Early reports suggested a possible guidance‑system glitch, while other analyses pointed to an anomaly in one of the second‑stage Raptor engines. Space X has not yet released a definitive explanation, and the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the European Space Agency (ESA) have opened investigations into the incident [T2].

Background and context

Starship is Space X’s flagship effort to build a fully reusable launch vehicle capable of carrying up to 120 t to orbit. This represents a dramatic leap from the Falcon 9, which carries a payload of roughly 22 t and is partially reusable. Starship’s design features a stainless‑steel hull, a heat shield for reentry, and a full‑stage‑reusability architecture that enables rapid turnaround between flights [T3].

The program has progressed through a series of test flights, SN3 through SN15, each designed to push the vehicle’s capabilities further. SN15’s orbital velocity milestone was the first time a Starship prototype achieved the speed necessary to enter stable Earth orbit, a key benchmark in the company’s development roadmap. The flight also tested the vehicle’s guidance, navigation, and control systems—critical for the precise orbital insertion required for future missions to the Moon and Mars [T3].

Space X’s rapid‑iteration, high‑risk approach has become a hallmark of its culture. The company has repeatedly flown prototypes, learned from failures, and implemented design changes in a matter of weeks. This methodology has accelerated development but has also raised questions about safety and regulatory compliance, especially as Space X seeks to secure contracts with NASA and other commercial customers [T2].

The bigger picture

The abort has implications that extend far beyond the Starship program. NASA’s Artemis program, which aims to return humans to the Moon by the mid‑2020s, has identified Starship as a potential lunar lander and cargo vehicle. The agency’s selection process is highly competitive, with Blue Origin’s New Glenn and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan also in contention. An early abort could delay NASA’s decision timeline and affect the overall schedule for lunar exploration [T2].

From a commercial standpoint, Space X has secured contracts for satellite launches, Starlink broadband deployments, and cargo resupply missions to the International Space Station. The company’s reputation for rapid iteration has attracted investors, but the abort may prompt scrutiny from venture capital firms and public markets. Recent reports indicate that Space X’s valuation has remained resilient, yet some analysts are calling for more transparent risk disclosures in future funding rounds [T3].

Regulatory agencies such as the FAA and ESA are closely monitoring the incident. The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation released a statement emphasizing the importance of safety protocols and the need for thorough investigations before future flights. ESA’s involvement reflects European interest in Starship’s potential for interplanetary missions and the broader implications for international collaboration in space [T2].

Public perception of Space X’s risk‑taking has been mixed. While many enthusiasts celebrate the company’s bold approach, others express concern about the safety of both crewed and uncrewed missions. The abort has reignited debates about the balance between innovation and caution in the commercial space sector, especially as the industry moves toward more ambitious goals such as Mars colonization [T3].

What to watch

The outcome of the investigations conducted by Space X, the FAA, and ESA will shape the trajectory of the Starship program. If the root cause is identified as a guidance‑system glitch, the company may need to redesign critical components, potentially delaying the next flight. Conversely, if an engine anomaly is confirmed, modifications to the Raptor engine design could be required. In either case, the findings will inform safety protocols for future missions [T2].

Space X’s next test flight, tentatively scheduled for late 2024, will likely incorporate lessons learned from the abort. The company has indicated that it will continue to pursue rapid iteration, but it may also implement additional telemetry and redundancy measures to mitigate similar incidents. The timing of this flight will be closely watched by NASA, competitors, and investors alike [T3].

The broader U.S. space industry will also feel the ripple effects. Blue Origin and ULA are expected to adjust their own development timelines in response to Space X’s progress. International partners, particularly those in the European and Japanese space agencies, may reassess their collaboration strategies with Starship, especially in light of the regulatory scrutiny that has intensified since the abort [T2].

Finally, the public and investor response will be a key indicator of the industry’s appetite for risk. While the commercial space sector has traditionally embraced rapid experimentation, the Starship abort may prompt a recalibration of expectations. Stakeholders will likely demand greater transparency in risk assessment and a clearer roadmap for achieving fully reusable launch vehicles without compromising safety [T3].

In sum, Starship’s first orbital velocity achievement, abruptly cut short by an early abort, underscores the paradox at the heart of modern spaceflight: the drive to push boundaries can yield unprecedented milestones, yet it also exposes the fragility of complex systems. The industry’s response to this event will determine whether rapid iteration can coexist with stringent safety standards, and whether the promise of reusable rockets can be realized without compromising the integrity of future missions to the Moon, Mars, and beyond.
