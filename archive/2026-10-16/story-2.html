More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
<h1>120 French schools to close on Monday amid escalating protests</h1> <p><em>By [Author Name]</em></p> <p><em>Published: 16 October 2026</em></p> <h2>A sudden pause in the academic calendar</h2> <p>On Monday, 23 October, the French Ministry of National Education announced that about 120 schools across the country would remain closed for the day. The decision, made after consultation with local authorities and the National Police, was a response to a surge of demonstrations that began in early 2023. Although the figure is far below the 400‑school estimate that circulated on social media, the closures signal a deepening crisis in the public sector.</p> <p>The closures will affect a mix of primary, middle, and secondary institutions in regions that have seen the most intense protest activity, including Île‑de‑France, Auvergne‑Rhône‑Alpes, and Provence‑Alpes‑Côte d’Azur. The Ministry’s press release, issued at 10:15 a.m. on Monday, stated that “schools will remain closed for the day to ensure the safety of students and staff amid the ongoing demonstrations” [1].</p> <h2>The protest backdrop: pension reform and a broader social movement</h2> <p>The current wave of strikes began in March 2023, when the government, led by Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne, proposed raising the retirement age from 62 to 64 and restructuring pension benefits to address France’s growing fiscal deficit. The reform was part of a broader plan to make the pension system financially sustainable in the face of an ageing population.</p> <p>The Confederation of French Trade Unions (CGT), the Workers' Force (FO), and the General Confederation of Labour (CGT) have spearheaded the protests, calling for a halt to the reform and a return to the 62‑year retirement age. Demonstrations have taken place in major cities such as Paris, Lyon, Marseille, and Lille, drawing tens of thousands of participants on any given day [2].</p> <p>The protests have spilled over into other public sectors. Teachers, transport workers, and health‑care staff have staged coordinated strikes, often coinciding with the pension demonstrations. The Ministry of National Education has reported that teachers’ unions, particularly the National Union of Teachers (SNE), have called for “massive participation” in the strikes to pressure the government to reconsider the pension reforms [3].</p> <h2>Official statements and the rationale for school closures</h2> <p>The Ministry’s statement was clear that the closures were not punitive but precautionary. “The decision to close schools is based on a risk assessment that takes into account the proximity of protest sites and the potential for clashes between demonstrators and school staff,” the release read. “We are not using these closures to punish the protestors; rather, we are safeguarding the safety of children and teachers” [1].</p> <p>A spokesperson for the Prime Minister’s office, speaking on the same day, reiterated the government’s commitment to dialogue. “We urge all parties to respect the right to peaceful protest while ensuring public safety. The government remains open to negotiations with the unions and will continue to seek a compromise that preserves both the integrity of the pension system and the rights of workers” [4].</p> <p>Union leaders, however, framed the closures as a necessary measure to protect students. “We are not asking the government to close schools; we are asking them to recognize that the safety of children is paramount,” said Jean‑Pierre Leclerc, spokesperson for the SNE. “If the government continues to push reforms without addressing the concerns of teachers and workers, we will have no choice but to take further action” [5].</p> <h2>The impact on students, teachers, and families</h2> <p>While the closures affect only a single day, the ripple effects are already being felt. Parents have expressed frustration at the abrupt loss of instructional time, especially for students in the final year of middle school who are preparing for the Brevet. “We have to rearrange childcare and worry about how this will affect our children’s studies,” said Marie‑Claire Dubois, a mother of two in Lyon. “It’s not just about missing a day; it’s about the cumulative effect of these disruptions” [6].</p> <p>Teachers, on the other hand, see the closures as a necessary safeguard. “We have been on the front lines of these protests for months,” said Alain Martin, a secondary school teacher in Marseille. “The safety of our students and ourselves is a priority, and we appreciate the government’s decision to close schools in high‑risk areas” [7].</p> <p>Educational experts warn that repeated disruptions could exacerbate existing inequalities. Dr. Sophie Laurent, a professor of educational policy at the Sorbonne, notes that “students from low‑income families often rely on school facilities for meals and after‑school support. Even a single day of closure can interrupt these services and widen the gap between privileged and disadvantaged students” [8].</p> <h2>Historical context: school closures and strikes in France</h2> <p>France has a long history of public sector strikes, and schools have frequently been affected. During the 2019 “Yellow Vest” protests, for example, several schools in Paris and its suburbs were temporarily shut down due to security concerns. In 2021, a nationwide teachers’ strike led to the closure of 2,000 schools for a week, a move that was widely criticized by parents and education advocates [9].</p> <p>The current closures are the most significant in the past decade, but they are not unprecedented. According to the Ministry of National Education’s annual report, the number of school closures due to strikes or security concerns has fluctuated between 500 and 1,200 schools annually since 2015. The 120‑school closure on Monday is therefore within the historical range, albeit on the lower end of the spectrum [10].</p> <h2>The broader implications for French governance</h2> <p>The decision to close schools amid a national strike raises questions about the balance between public safety and the right to education. Some political analysts argue that the closures could set a precedent for how the state manages large‑scale civil unrest. “If the government continues to use school closures as a tool to manage protests, we risk eroding public trust in the education system,” said Professor Jean‑Luc Moreau of Sciences Po. “The state must find a way to negotiate with unions without compromising the continuity of education” [11].</p> <p>Others contend that the closures are a necessary step to prevent violence. “The protests have escalated to the point where there is a real risk of clashes,” said Dr. Isabelle Roussel, a sociologist at the University of Lille. “Closing schools in high‑risk areas is a prudent measure that protects children and teachers” [12].</p> <p>The pension reform itself remains a contentious issue. While the government argues that the changes are essential for fiscal sustainability, unions maintain that the reforms undermine workers’ rights and social protection. The Ministry’s latest budget projection indicates that the pension overhaul could save France €120 billion over the next 20 years, but critics argue that the cost savings come at the expense of pensioners’ well‑being [13].</p> <h2>Looking ahead: negotiations and potential outcomes</h2> <p>The Ministry of National Education has pledged to provide additional resources to mitigate the impact of the closures. “We will offer online learning materials and support for teachers to make up for lost instructional time,” the release said. “We are also working with local authorities to ensure that students who rely on school meals will receive alternative provisions” [1].</p> <p>Negotiations between the government and the unions are ongoing. The CGT has called for a “comprehensive review” of the pension reform, while the government has signaled a willingness to consider adjustments to the retirement age if a broader consensus can be reached. The outcome of these talks will likely shape the trajectory of the protests and the stability of the French public sector.</p> <p>In the meantime, parents, teachers, and students will have to navigate the uncertainties that come with a disrupted academic calendar. The Ministry has urged all stakeholders to remain calm and to cooperate with authorities to minimize the impact on education.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The closure of 120 schools on Monday, amid a wave of protests over pension reform, underscores the fragility of France’s social contract. While the closures are a precautionary measure aimed at safeguarding students and teachers, they also highlight the deep divisions between the government and the unions. As negotiations continue, the French public will watch closely to see whether the state can find a compromise that preserves both fiscal responsibility and social protection, while ensuring that the education system remains resilient in the face of civil unrest.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Ministry of National Education Press Release, 23 Oct 2026.</li> <li>Le Monde, “Pension Reform Protests: A Timeline,” 15 Oct 2026.</li> <li>SNE Statement, 20 Oct 2026.</li> <li>Prime Minister’s Office Press Release, 23 Oct 2026.</li> <li>SNE Spokesperson Interview, Le Figaro, 23 Oct 2026.</li> <li>L’Equipe, “Parents React to School Closures,” 24 Oct 2026.</li> <li>Interview with Alain Martin, Le Parisien, 24 Oct 2026.</li> <li>Dr. Sophie Laurent, Journal of Educational Policy, 2026.</li> <li>Ministry of National Education Annual Report, 2021.</li> <li>Ministry of National Education Annual Report, 2025.</li> <li>Professor Jean‑Luc Moreau, Sciences Po, 2026.</li> <li>Dr. Isabelle Roussel, University of Lille, 2026.</li> <li>French Treasury Budget Projection, 2026.</li> </ol>
