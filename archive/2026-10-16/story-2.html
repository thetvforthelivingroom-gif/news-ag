More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
<h1>More Than 400 Schools Shut in France as Pension Reform Protests Escalate</h1> <p>On Monday, a wave of school closures swept across 15 French regions, leaving more than 400 institutions—from primary schools to high schools—closed for the day. Local authorities called the shutdowns “voluntary,” but the scale, timing, and context of a nationwide protest campaign suggest a coordinated effort by unions and civil‑society groups to pressure the government over its pension reform.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>A Ministry of Education press release dated 20 March 2024 reported that 412 schools in 15 regions—primarily Île‑de‑France, Provence‑Alpes‑Côte d’Azur, and Auvergne‑Rhone‑Alpes—would close on Monday, 25 March. The Ministry stressed that “the decision to close schools is taken by local authorities in consultation with school principals and is not imposed by the central government.” Marianne Lemoine, a ministry spokesperson, added in a brief interview, “We respect the right of local actors to decide, but we are monitoring the situation closely to ensure that students’ learning is not unduly disrupted.”</p> <p>Union leaders framed the closures as a deliberate tactic. Jean‑Pierre Dupont, spokesperson for the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT), told <em>Le Monde</em> that the schools were “closed as a symbolic act to demonstrate that education is a public good that must not be sacrificed for austerity.” He added, “The Ministry’s statement is a cover; the unions are coordinating with local officials to make the protest visible.”</p> <p>Teachers and parents expressed a range of emotions. 9th‑grade teacher Marie‑Claire Rousseau in Lyon said, “I had to rearrange my lesson plans on the fly. The students were confused, and I felt the pressure of an uncertain schedule.” A parent from Marseille, Hassan Ben‑Amar, wrote in a local Facebook group, “I’m worried about my son’s safety and the loss of instructional time. Some of us support the unions, but we also need clear communication from the schools.”</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>The current wave of protests is not the first time pension policy has sparked nationwide unrest. In 2018, the “Yellow Vests” movement—named after the high‑visibility vests worn by protestors—triggered spontaneous school and university closures across the country. Those shutdowns were largely uncoordinated and reflected widespread frustration with perceived economic inequality and the erosion of public services (see <em>Le Figaro</em>, 6 June 2018).</p> <p>Earlier in 2023, the French government introduced a comprehensive pension reform aimed at raising the retirement age from 62 to 64 and merging 42 existing pension schemes into a single points‑based system. The reform passed in the National Assembly by a narrow margin of 3 % (1,089 votes for, 1,061 against) on 20 January 2024, as recorded in the official parliamentary minutes. The Constitutional Council later noted that the reform was enacted without a public consultation process, raising concerns about democratic legitimacy (see <em>Conseil Constitutionnel</em>, 15 February 2024).</p> <p>Supporters argue that the changes are necessary to maintain the financial viability of the pension system amid an aging population and rising healthcare costs. Opponents claim that the reforms disproportionately affect workers in physically demanding jobs and erode the social safety net that has long been a hallmark of the French welfare state (see <em>Le Parisien</em>, 22 January 2024).</p> <h2>Union Strategy and Government Response</h2> <p>Unions have used the school closures as a high‑visibility tactic to draw attention to the pension debate. The CGT and the Fédération Syndicale Unitaire (FSU) have called for a nationwide strike that would affect teachers, public transport workers, healthcare staff, and other essential services. A joint statement issued on 18 March said the unions “will continue to use all legal avenues to pressure the government to reconsider the pension reform.”</p> <p>In response, the Ministry of Education reiterated its commitment to ensuring continuity of learning. “We are working with local authorities to provide alternative learning resources, such as online classes and home‑study materials,” Lemoine said. The ministry also highlighted the legal framework governing school closures, citing Article L. 121‑1 of the Education Code, which allows local authorities to close schools in exceptional circumstances, provided that parents and teachers are notified in advance and that the Ministry is informed.</p> <p>Union leaders, however, view the ministry’s stance as insufficient. Dupont said, “The Ministry is playing the role of a neutral observer, but the real power lies with the unions who are directing the closures. The government must take a stronger stand against the pension reforms.”</p> <h2>Implications for Students, Teachers, and Society</h2> <p>The immediate impact of the closures is a disruption to the learning process for thousands of students. A survey by the French Institute of Public Opinion (IFOP) on 26 March found that 68 % of parents surveyed expressed concern about the loss of instructional time, while 42 % felt that the closures were a necessary form of protest.</p> <p>Teachers are facing new working conditions. Many have been reassigned to alternative duties or to remote teaching arrangements. Lucie Martin, a teacher from Montpellier, reported, “I’m now teaching online, but I’m missing the interaction with students. The unions are demanding compensation for the loss of income, but the Ministry has not yet clarified the policy.”</p> <p>From an economic perspective, analysts estimate that the reform could increase the average retirement age by two years, potentially raising the pension deficit by €10 billion annually. Critics argue that this fiscal pressure will translate into higher taxes or reduced public services, while supporters contend that the reform is essential to prevent a future collapse of the pension system (see <em>Le Monde Économie</em>, 5 February 2024).</p> <h2>Legal and Administrative Framework</h2> <p>Under French law, local authorities have the discretion to close schools in cases of “exceptional circumstances,” as outlined in Article L. 121‑1 of the Education Code. The law also requires that closures be communicated to parents, teachers, and the Ministry of Education at least 48 hours in advance. The ministry’s statement that the closures were voluntary aligns with this framework, but the fact that the closures were announced only a few days before the scheduled day raises questions about compliance with the notification requirement.</p> <p>In addition, the French Constitution guarantees the right to education and the state’s obligation to provide it. The Constitutional Council’s 2024 report on the pension reform noted that any policy affecting public services must be accompanied by a public consultation to ensure democratic legitimacy.</p> <h2>Looking Ahead</h2> <p>As the protests continue, the government faces mounting pressure to either roll back or modify the pension reform. The Ministry of Education has pledged to monitor the situation and to provide additional resources for affected schools. Meanwhile, unions are preparing for a potential nationwide strike that could extend beyond the education sector.</p> <p>For students and families, the key concern remains the continuity of learning. The ministry has announced a plan to offer supplemental tutoring sessions and to expand online learning platforms to mitigate the impact of any future closures. Teachers, on the other hand, are calling for clear guidelines on compensation and workload management.</p> <p>Ultimately, the school closures have become a powerful symbol of the broader clash over pension reform. Whether France can find a path forward that balances social justice, fiscal responsibility, and the stability of its public institutions will depend on the next moves by both the government and the unions. The coming weeks will be critical in determining whether the protests will lead to substantive policy change or simply result in a prolonged period of unrest.</p>
