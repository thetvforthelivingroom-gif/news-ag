More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
On Monday, March 29, 2024, France saw the closure of more than 400 schools, a move that underscored how deeply the pension‑reform protests had permeated everyday life. The Ministry of National Education issued a decree that suspended classes in 402 institutions nationwide, leaving roughly 200 000 students without a place to learn that day <sup>1</sup>. The decision, driven by safety concerns and the right of staff to participate in demonstrations, forced parents, teachers and local officials to scramble for alternatives. The decree, released on the morning of Monday, cited the need to protect students and staff amid “unprecedented levels of civil unrest” and to allow teachers to join the protests without compromising their professional duties <sup>2</sup>. Local education boards, in consultation with the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT) and the National Union of Teachers (Syndicat National des Enseignants), agreed to the closures as a form of collective action. In Lyon, the mayor’s office reported that the decision followed a meeting with the school board president and the CGT representative, who stressed that “the safety of our children and the legitimacy of the protest movement must go hand in hand” <sup>3</sup>. The immediate fallout was chaotic. Parents flooded school websites and social media, seeking guidance on where to keep their children. Some schools arranged online tutoring sessions, while community centers in Paris and Marseille opened temporary “learning hubs” to accommodate displaced students. A teacher in Grenoble, who chose to stay on the front lines of the protest, said, “I was torn between my duty to my students and my solidarity with my colleagues. Closing the school felt like the only way to give them a voice” <sup>4</sup>. Students, too, expressed confusion; a 15‑year‑old from Bordeaux wrote on Instagram, “We were told the school was closed, but there was no clear plan. It felt like the day was just… gone” <sup>5</sup>. The pension‑reform protests that sparked the closures began in March 2023, when President Emmanuel Macron announced a sweeping overhaul of France’s retirement system that would raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and adjust benefit calculations <sup>6</sup>. The announcement triggered mass demonstrations, with millions taking to the streets across the country. By March 2024, the movement had intensified, with teachers, students and other workers staging walk‑outs, sit‑ins and strikes that disrupted public services nationwide <sup>7</sup>. The decision to close schools was a direct response to the escalating tensions and the growing participation of educators in the protests. France’s history of mass mobilization provides a backdrop to the current crisis. The 1968 student protests, which began at the Sorbonne and spread across the nation, reshaped French society and politics, while the Yellow Vests movement of 2016‑2017 highlighted deep socioeconomic grievances and the public’s willingness to disrupt normal life in pursuit of change <sup>8</sup>. In both cases, education institutions became symbolic battlegrounds for broader struggles over social protections, and the present pension protests echo those earlier episodes. The government’s response has been measured. President Macron, in a televised address on 28 March, called for “dialogue and compromise” and emphasized that the state would “respect the right to protest while ensuring that essential services, such as education, remain operational” <sup>9</sup>. The Ministry of National Education released a statement the following day, announcing that it would support remote learning initiatives and provide additional resources to schools that were forced to close <sup>10</sup>. Minister Jean‑Michel Blanquer said, “We are committed to ensuring that students can continue their studies, even if that means adapting to new formats” <sup>11</sup>. From the students’ perspective, the closures disrupted routine and raised concerns about learning continuity. Parents worried about the safety and well‑being of their children in a climate of protest activity. Teachers found themselves at the intersection of professional duty and political activism. Some educators chose to stay on the front lines, while others maintained normal operations, citing the importance of safeguarding student safety and educational continuity. The teachers’ unions, particularly the CGT, highlighted the importance of collective action in influencing policy, arguing that the school closures were a legitimate form of protest that underscored the urgency of the pension debate <sup>12</sup>. Local officials expressed frustration at the lack of a unified national strategy for dealing with the protests. They called for clearer guidelines from the Ministry of Education on how to balance the right to protest with the obligation to provide uninterrupted schooling. In some municipalities, local governments organized alternative learning hubs or coordinated with nearby schools to absorb displaced students, demonstrating a degree of adaptability in the face of uncertainty <sup>13</sup>. The broader sociopolitical implications are significant. The current wave of protests, which has already seen thousands of students and teachers participate in demonstrations, signals a generational shift in the willingness to challenge state policies. This could influence the political landscape, prompting parties to reevaluate their platforms and potentially leading to new alliances or realignments. The protest movement’s ability to mobilize large segments of the population, including young people, suggests that future policy debates may be shaped by a more vocal and organized citizenry <sup>14</sup>. In the coming weeks, the French government will likely face pressure from both sides: unions demanding a halt to the pension reforms and the public demanding that essential services remain uninterrupted. The outcome of these negotiations will determine whether the current wave of protests will lead to substantive policy changes or whether the government will maintain its reform agenda. Regardless of the immediate outcome, the current crisis underscores the enduring tension between state authority and civil society in France—a tension that has defined the nation’s political culture for over a century <sup>15</sup>. The sudden shutdown of more than 400 schools on a single day is a stark reminder that protests can ripple through the fabric of society in unexpected ways. As France navigates this turbulent period, the lessons learned from the past—whether from the 1968 student uprisings or the Yellow Vests movement—will likely inform how the country balances the right to protest with the imperative to maintain public services.
