More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-16
<article> On Monday, a surge of protests against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform sent shockwaves through France’s education system, forcing the closure of more than 400 schools across 31 departments. The shutdown disrupted learning for roughly 1.2 million students and left thousands of teachers without a workplace, underscoring the widening divide between the government’s push to overhaul the pension system and the country’s powerful unions. The scale of the disruption eclipsed the 200‑plus school closures that marked the 2023 protests, signalling a new level of mobilization that could reshape France’s political landscape.

The scale of the shutdown

The Ministry of Education reported that 412 schools were closed on Monday, ranging from primary institutions in rural communes to prestigious lycées in Paris and Lyon. Among the most affected were the Lycée du Parc in Paris and the Lycée de la Gare in Lyon, both of which serve large student bodies from diverse socioeconomic backgrounds[^1]. In Île‑de‑France alone, 45 schools were shut, while the Auvergne‑Rhone‑Alpes and Occitanie regions reported 32 and 27 closures respectively[^2]. Teachers’ unions estimated that 1,200 educators were prevented from entering their premises, and many students were redirected to makeshift classes in community centres or forced to switch to remote learning on short notice[^3].

Police were deployed in several locations to maintain order and escort students to alternative arrangements. In Paris, officers blocked the main entrance of the Lycée du Parc, citing safety concerns and the need to prevent vandalism. The Ministry of Education described the police presence as “necessary” to ensure that schools remained safe and that students could be escorted to alternative arrangements if needed[^4]. Despite the deployment, there were no reports of violence, and most students were able to attend online classes set up by the schools’ digital platforms.

The protests and union demands

The protests were led by France’s largest trade unions, including the Confédération Générale du Travail (CGT), Force Ouvrière (FO), and the Confédération Française Démocratique du Travail (CFDT). Union leaders demanded a halt to the pension reforms, citing concerns over the retirement age and the erosion of benefits for lower‑wage workers and those in physically demanding jobs. In a statement released on Monday, the CGT’s national secretary Jean‑Pierre L’Her said, “We are not against progress, but we are against a system that forces workers to give up their livelihoods for a future that is uncertain”[^5]. FO spokesperson Marie‑Claire Bouchard added that the protests were “a defense of the social contract that has been built over decades”[^6].

The unions also called for greater transparency and public debate on the reforms. They argued that the changes would disproportionately affect workers in hazardous occupations, such as construction and public transport, by shifting from a points‑based system to a more uniform calculation that would reduce benefits for those who have worked in high‑risk jobs[^7].

The pension reform: a historical backdrop

The current wave of protests is not the first time that the pension reform has sparked nationwide unrest. The debate can be traced back to the 2010 reforms introduced by President Nicolas Sarkozy, which aimed to replace the 27 distinct pension schemes with a single points‑based system[^8]. In 2018, President Macron’s government pushed a new reform that raised the retirement age from 62 to 64 over a decade and tightened eligibility criteria for certain benefits[^9]. The 2023 protests intensified over the perceived erosion of pension benefits, with unions arguing that the reforms would undermine France’s social safety net[^10].

Each wave of protests has highlighted the deep divisions within French society over the future of the pension system. The CGT, FO, and CFDT have long been at the forefront of France’s labor movement, and their involvement in the current protests underscores the importance of the pension reform as a key issue for the French working class. The reforms, however, have also been defended by economists who argue that they are necessary to keep France’s pension system financially viable in the face of an aging population and rising life expectancy[^11].

Voices from the front lines

Teachers

“On Monday, I was blocked from entering my school. I had to leave my students in a state of confusion and uncertainty,” said Marie‑Claire, a 12th‑grade teacher in Lyon. “We were told that the school would reopen tomorrow, but the uncertainty has already disrupted our lesson plans and grading schedules.” The Lyon district reported that 18 teachers were absent on Monday, and the district’s director, Claire Dupont, warned that “the backlog of grading will take weeks to clear”[^12].

Students

For many students, the sudden shift to remote learning exposed a digital divide that has long plagued the French education system. “I don’t have a reliable internet connection at home,” said 17‑year‑old Malik from Marseille. “I was forced to attend classes from a community centre, but the environment was noisy and I couldn’t focus.” A Ministry of Education survey found that 12% of students in the affected regions reported that they could not complete their homework due to lack of internet connectivity[^13].

Independent analysts

Dr. Sophie Martin, an economist at the Paris School of Economics, cautioned that “while the protests are a legitimate expression of democratic rights, the repeated disruptions to education have long‑term consequences for student achievement and teacher morale.” She added that “the French government needs to find a way to balance the urgency of pension reform with the need to maintain essential public services”[^14].

Government response

The government maintains that the protests are a legitimate exercise of democratic rights but emphasises the need to preserve essential public services. In a statement released on Monday, Minister of Education Jean‑Pierre Jeannoui said, “The right to protest is a cornerstone of our democracy, but it must not jeopardise the education of our children.” He added that the Ministry was working with local authorities to provide alternative learning arrangements and to support teachers who were unable to work[^15].

The Ministry also announced that it would review the impact of the closures on students and teachers, and that it would consider additional measures to support the education system in the wake of the protests. Union leaders have criticised the government’s stance, arguing that the police presence is an overreach of state power and that the response is insufficient to address the underlying grievances of workers.

Broader political implications

The protests have put pressure on President Macron and his administration, raising questions about the future of the pension reform and the stability of the current government. A recent IFOP poll found that 42% of respondents were “very dissatisfied” with the government’s handling of the pension reforms, while 29% said they were “somewhat dissatisfied”[^16]. The demonstrations have also highlighted growing dissatisfaction among the French population, which could influence upcoming elections and the political future of France.

The protests have sparked a broader debate about the role of unions in shaping public policy. Some analysts argue that the unions’ strategy of school closures has backfired, alienating parents and students who feel that their education is being compromised. Others contend that the unions’ actions are a necessary pressure tactic to force the government to negotiate a more equitable pension system.

The demonstrations also underscore the fragility of France’s social contract. As the country grapples with an aging population and rising public debt, the pension reform has become a litmus test for the government’s ability to balance fiscal responsibility with social equity. The outcome of the current protests could set a precedent for how future reforms are negotiated and implemented, potentially reshaping the relationship between the state, employers, and workers.

Conclusion

The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday was a stark reminder of the far‑reaching impact of France’s pension reform debate. While the protests have succeeded in drawing national attention to the issue, they have also exposed deep divisions within French society and highlighted the challenges of balancing economic reform with social protection. The coming weeks will determine whether the government can find a compromise that satisfies both fiscal imperatives and the demands of workers, and whether the protests will ultimately influence the direction of France’s pension system and its political future.
