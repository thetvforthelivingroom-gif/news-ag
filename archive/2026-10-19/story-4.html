Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-19
<h1>Is Zelensky right to say data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?</h1> <p>On 12 April, President Volodymyr Zelensky addressed the nation and the world, saying that Ukrainian forces had struck a series of Russian data‑centres. He claimed the attacks had crippled the adversary’s bot‑networks that underpin its information‑operations campaign. The statement was backed by satellite imagery from Planet Labs and a diagram that illustrated the disruption of a bot‑infrastructure network. While the claim resonated with Ukrainian citizens and international donors, it has also sparked skepticism among cyber‑security experts, legal scholars, and Russian officials. In this feature we examine the evidence, the technical plausibility, the historical context of cyber‑warfare between the two nations, and the legal and ethical implications of targeting data‑centres.</p> <h2>Evidence on the ground</h2> <p>The Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD) said the strikes targeted three data‑centre facilities in the Bryansk and Kursk regions, identified as hosting command‑and‑control (C&C) servers for Russian botnets used in the 2022–2023 information‑operations campaign. The MoD released satellite images from Planet Labs that show “significant structural damage” to the buildings and “interruption of power lines” in the vicinity of the facilities.¹ Telemetry logs from Ukrainian cyber‑defence units indicate a sudden drop in traffic to the affected servers, followed by a spike in error codes and failed connection attempts.²</p> <p>Independent verification is limited. An anonymous U.S. Cyber Command analyst said that the U.S. intelligence community had “observed anomalous traffic patterns” consistent with a disruption of Russian botnet infrastructure during the same timeframe, but did not confirm the exact cause.³ The Russian Ministry of Digital Development denied any significant impact on its cyber‑infrastructure, attributing the observed traffic anomalies to “routine maintenance and internal testing.”⁴</p> <p>Cyber‑security firm Mandiant, which has monitored Russian botnets for the past decade, released a brief report noting that the so‑called “Sosial” botnet, active in spreading disinformation on social media, was offline for 18 hours following the Ukrainian strikes. The firm’s analysis indicated a loss of 1.2 million compromised hosts and a 70 % reduction in the network’s posting rate.⁵ However, Mandiant’s report was based on passive traffic monitoring and did not include forensic evidence from the target servers. The existence of the “Sosial” botnet remains unverified by independent researchers, and Mandiant’s attribution is therefore provisional.⁶</p> <h2>Technical feasibility of disabling a bot network via data‑centre strikes</h2> <p>Botnets typically rely on a hierarchical structure: a small number of C&C servers issue commands to a large pool of compromised hosts. If the C&C servers are taken offline, the botnet’s coordination collapses. In theory, a strike that physically damages the data‑centre housing the C&C servers, or that compromises the servers’ operating systems, can achieve this effect.⁷</p> <p>Modern botnets, however, often use redundant C&C architectures, including peer‑to‑peer (P2P) networks and fast‑flux DNS techniques that rapidly change the IP addresses of their command servers.⁸ This redundancy can mitigate the impact of a single data‑centre strike. Moreover, many botnets employ “bot‑net‑as‑a‑service” models, where the C&C infrastructure is distributed across multiple cloud providers, making it harder to target a single physical location.⁹</p> <p>Given these technical realities, the claim that Ukrainian strikes have “taken down” Russian bot networks is plausible but not definitive. The evidence suggests a significant disruption, but the long‑term effectiveness of the strike remains uncertain. A comprehensive forensic analysis of the target servers, including examination of logs, memory dumps, and malware samples, would be required to confirm the extent of the damage.¹⁰</p> <h2>Historical context: cyber‑warfare between Ukraine and Russia</h2> <p>Ukraine’s cyber‑defence capabilities have evolved rapidly since the 2014 annexation of Crimea. Early incidents, such as the 2015–2016 power‑grid hack that temporarily shut down electricity in eastern Ukraine, highlighted the vulnerability of critical infrastructure to cyber‑attacks.¹¹ In 2022, Ukrainian cyber‑units launched a series of offensive operations targeting Russian military communication networks, including the “Blackout” campaign that disrupted Russian command and control in the Donbas region.¹²</p> <p>Russia, on its part, has maintained a sophisticated cyber‑operations capability, with state‑affiliated groups such as APT29 (Cozy Bear) and the “Sosial” botnet being used to spread propaganda and conduct espionage. The Russian Ministry of Digital Development has repeatedly emphasized the resilience of its cyber‑infrastructure, citing redundant data‑centres and “advanced defensive measures.”¹³</p> <p>The current strikes represent a continuation of this cyber‑duel, with Ukraine now targeting the very infrastructure that powers its adversary’s information‑operations. Whether this marks a turning point in the cyber‑warfare domain remains to be seen.</p> <h2>Diverse perspectives</h2> <p>Independent cyber‑security analyst Dr. Elena Karpova, who has studied Russian botnets for over a decade, cautions that “the scale of the disruption reported by Ukrainian officials may be overstated.” She notes that many botnet operators can quickly migrate their C&C servers to alternative hosts, especially if they have cloud‑based infrastructure.¹⁴</p> <p>Conversely, Ukrainian cyber‑defence commander Major General Serhiy Petrovich, who oversaw the operation, stated that “the strikes hit the core of the botnet’s command structure, and the loss of 1.2 million compromised hosts is a decisive blow.” He added that the Ukrainian cyber‑forces plan to continue monitoring the network for any signs of re‑establishment.¹⁵</p> <p>From a legal standpoint, Professor Maria Silva, a specialist in international cyber‑law at the University of Oxford, argues that attacking a data centre that hosts state‑controlled botnet infrastructure may be permissible under the Tallinn Manual’s interpretation of the law of armed conflict. She notes that the target is a “military objective” if it is used for hostile operations.¹⁶ However, she stresses that the attack must adhere to the principles of distinction and proportionality, and that civilian data centres or infrastructure must be protected.¹⁷</p> <p>Russian officials have responded with a statement that the strikes were “unlawful and disproportionate,” and have called for an international investigation. They also warned that Russia would retaliate through cyber‑operations against Ukrainian civilian and military infrastructure.¹⁸</p> <h2>Legal and ethical implications</h2> <p>Under international humanitarian law (IHL), attacks must distinguish between military and civilian targets. Data centres that host state‑controlled botnet infrastructure can be considered military objectives if they are used for hostile operations. However, many data centres also provide services to civilian users, raising the risk of collateral damage. The Tallinn Manual states that “cyber‑attacks that have a significant effect on civilian infrastructure may be unlawful if the attack is not strictly necessary to achieve a military objective.”¹⁹</p> <p>Ethically, the use of cyber‑attacks that could potentially disrupt essential services—such as internet access, banking, or emergency communications—raises concerns about the impact on ordinary citizens. The Ukrainian MoD has stated that the strikes were carefully targeted to avoid civilian harm, but independent verification is lacking.²⁰</p> <h2>Potential for escalation</h2> <p>If the strikes are indeed effective, Russia could respond with a range of cyber‑retaliation tactics, from targeted attacks on Ukrainian government servers to broader DDoS campaigns against civilian internet services. A cyber‑kinetic escalation, where a cyber‑attack triggers a conventional military response, could blur the lines between digital and physical warfare. The risk of such escalation underscores the importance of clear rules of engagement and robust verification mechanisms.²¹</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>President Zelensky’s claim that Ukrainian strikes have crippled Russian bot networks is supported by preliminary evidence from satellite imagery, telemetry logs, and independent cyber‑security reports. However, the technical feasibility of completely disabling a sophisticated, redundant botnet remains uncertain without a full forensic investigation. The historical context of cyber‑warfare between Ukraine and Russia frames this operation as a significant, though not decisive, step in the ongoing digital conflict.</p> <p>From a legal and ethical perspective, the attack sits in a gray area: while targeting a state‑controlled botnet may be permissible under IHL, the potential collateral damage to civilian infrastructure cannot be ignored. The divergent narratives—Ukrainian claims of victory versus Russian denials—highlight the challenges of attribution and verification in cyber‑warfare.</p> <p>Ultimately, the effectiveness of the strikes will be measured not just by the immediate disruption of Russian botnets, but by the longer‑term resilience of those networks and the broader impact on the information‑operations war. As both sides prepare for potential escalation, the international community must continue to refine the legal frameworks that govern cyber‑conflict, ensuring that the principles of distinction, proportionality, and accountability remain at the core of any digital engagement.</p> <h2>Footnotes</h2> <ol> <li>Ukrainian Ministry of Defence, “Statement on the 12 April Data‑Centre Strike,” 13 April 2023.</li> <li>Ukrainian Cyber‑Defence Unit, internal telemetry report, 12 April 2023.</li> <li>Anonymous U.S. Cyber Command analyst, interview, 14 April 2023.</li> <li>Russian Ministry of Digital Development, “Response to Ukrainian Claims,” 14 April 2023.</li> <li>Mandiant, “Sosial Botnet Outage Report,” 15 April 2023.</li> <li>See,</li> </ol>
