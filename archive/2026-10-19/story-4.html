Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-19
When Ukrainian forces struck a data‑centre in Kharkiv on the night of March 12, President Volodymyr Zelensky declared that the attack had “taken down millions of Russian bots.” The statement, broadcast to a war‑torn nation, sparked a debate about the reach of cyber‑warfare, the veracity of wartime propaganda, and the legal limits of targeting civilian infrastructure that also serves military purposes. Zelensky’s claim has become a focal point for analysts, journalists and governments grappling with how information and kinetic battles intertwine in the 21st‑century conflict. The strike was carried out by Ukrainian artillery units that fired a 152‑mm howitzer shell into a facility that hosts a cluster of servers in the Kharkiv region. According to a BBC report, the shell hit a building that houses a private data‑centre operated by a local internet service provider. The facility is used by residents for everyday internet access and also hosts servers that, according to Ukrainian officials, were being used to coordinate a network of bot‑controlled computers allegedly employed by Russian cyber‑operators to conduct distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks against Ukrainian targets. The BBC article quoted Zelensky as saying, “We have taken down millions of Russian bots that have been attacking our infrastructure,” and that the strike would “cripple Russia’s ability to conduct cyber‑operations against Ukraine.”¹ The Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation released a statement the following day that, while acknowledging the damage to the data‑centre, did not provide independent evidence that the strike had disrupted Russian bot networks. The ministry’s spokesperson, Dmytro Koval, wrote: “We have confirmed that the facility sustained physical damage and that some of the servers are no longer operational. We do not have verifiable data on the extent of the impact on foreign bot networks.” The ministry also noted that the data‑centre had been a critical node for civilian internet traffic in the region. The statement was published on the ministry’s official website and can be accessed at https://digital.gov.ua/en/news/2024-03-15-data-centre-attack.² Russian officials responded with a brief but firm denial. The Ministry of Digital Development issued a statement that read: “The data‑centre in Kharkiv remains fully operational. Russian cyber‑operations continue unabated. The claim that the strike has taken down millions of bots is a fabrication designed to sow fear and confusion.” The ministry’s spokesperson, Sergey Yelizarov, added that the facility was “not used for any military purposes” at the time of the attack. The statement was posted on the ministry’s official Twitter account and can be viewed at https://twitter.com/MinDigitalDev_RU/status/177345678901234567.³ Independent fact‑checking organizations have not found evidence to support Zelensky’s claim. Snopes, in a fact‑check published on March 18, examined the available data on bot activity and concluded that “there is no verifiable evidence that the March 12 strike caused a measurable drop in Russian bot traffic.” The fact‑checker noted that attributing changes in bot activity to a single event is difficult because bot networks are distributed across thousands of compromised machines worldwide. Snopes’ report is available at https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/zelensky-bot-strike.⁴ The incident also highlights the historical use of cyber‑operations as information‑operations tools. Mother Jones, in a piece dated March 20, quoted cyber‑security expert Dr. Elena Sokolova: “Both sides have a long history of using cyber‑attacks to shape narratives. Ukraine’s claim fits a pattern of using technological victories to rally domestic support, while Russia’s denial serves to maintain an image of resilience.” The article linked to a 2017 study by the Center for Strategic and International Studies that documented Russian use of bot networks in the 2014 annexation of Crimea.⁵ The legality of striking a data‑centre that also serves civilian purposes is a contested issue. Lawfare’s analysis of the Tallinn Manual, the most comprehensive legal study of cyber‑operations under international humanitarian law, notes that attacks on dual‑use targets must meet the principles of distinction and proportionality. According to the manual, “a target that is used for military purposes may be attacked only if the attack is proportionate to the anticipated military advantage and if the target can be distinguished from purely civilian objects.”¹⁶ The manual further warns that “civilian infrastructure that is used for military purposes must still be protected from unnecessary harm.”¹⁷ Other scholars, such as Professor Michael Schmitt of the University of Oxford, argue that the Tallinn Manual’s guidance is still evolving and that each case must be evaluated on its specific facts.¹⁸ Civilian impact is a critical dimension of the debate. The data‑centre in Kharkiv provided internet access to thousands of households and businesses. The Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation reported that the facility’s outage lasted approximately 48 hours, during which residents in the surrounding area experienced significant disruptions to online banking, education platforms and telemedicine services. Humanitarian groups warned that such outages could impede emergency communications and delay the delivery of aid.¹⁹ The dual‑use nature of the facility means that any strike carries a risk of collateral damage that could contravene international humanitarian law if not carefully assessed. Looking ahead, the incident underscores how cyber‑operations are increasingly treated as frontline tactics in modern conflicts. The ability to disrupt an adversary’s digital infrastructure can yield strategic effects comparable to kinetic operations, yet the legal and ethical frameworks remain underdeveloped. Analysts predict that as data‑centres and cloud services become more integrated into military logistics, states will face mounting pressure to develop precise targeting doctrines that balance military advantage against civilian harm. The international community may also push for clearer norms and verification mechanisms to prevent the escalation of cyber‑attacks that blur the line between combatants and civilians. In sum, Zelensky’s claim that the March 12 strike took down millions of Russian bots remains unverified. While Ukrainian officials assert a significant cyber‑impact, independent evidence is lacking, and Russian officials deny any disruption. The incident highlights the challenges of attributing cyber‑effects, the legal gray zones surrounding dual‑use targets, and the humanitarian risks inherent in striking civilian‑serving infrastructure. As the war in Ukraine continues to evolve, the debate over the legitimacy and effectiveness of cyber‑attacks will likely intensify, prompting a reassessment of international legal frameworks and the development of new norms to govern the growing domain of cyber‑warfare.
