More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-19
On Monday, roughly 400 public schools across France were shut down, leaving about 200,000 students and teachers without classroom instruction. The closures were announced by local education authorities amid a nationwide strike that has been unfolding since late October. The move was part of a coordinated effort by several unions to pressure the government over the pension reform law, which raised the retirement age from 62 to 64 and introduced a points‑based system for determining eligibility for full pensions. The decision underscored the depth of opposition to the reform and the role of school closures as a bargaining tool in the current protest movement.<sup>[1]</sup> The pension reform, passed by Parliament in November 2023, has sparked immediate backlash from labor unions and the public. It aims to address France’s demographic shift and fiscal pressures by linking pension benefits to a points system that rewards longer service and higher contributions. Critics argue that the system disproportionately affects lower‑paid public‑sector workers and that the higher retirement age places an undue burden on physically demanding jobs. The law’s proponents maintain that it is necessary for the long‑term sustainability of the pension system.<sup>[2]</sup> France’s history of labor activism provides context for the current protests. The 2010 pension reform introduced a points system for certain public‑sector categories, sparking nationwide strikes that highlighted the tension between reform and workers’ rights. The 2023 reform echoes that earlier debate, but it extends the points system to a broader range of professions and raises the retirement age. The memory of the 2010 protests fuels the current unions’ resolve to demand significant changes or a complete overhaul of the pension system.<sup>[3]</sup> Teachers’ unions, led by the National Federation of Teachers (Fédération Nationale des Enseignants), called for the law’s repeal or substantial modification. They argue that the points system undermines job security and public‑sector stability. In addition to the school closures, teachers have participated in mass walk‑outs and demonstrations in city centres. Transport unions, represented by the Confédération Générale des Transports, joined the protest by calling for strikes that would affect rail and bus services, citing the broader economic impact of the reform on workers’ livelihoods.<sup>[4]</sup> The closures were also a strategic component of a broader strike campaign that has spanned over two months. Thousands of workers—including teachers, transport staff, and healthcare personnel—have walked out in support of the movement. Local authorities cited the risk of clashes between demonstrators and police as a primary concern, noting that large crowds had gathered in city centres and that the presence of students and teachers could exacerbate tensions. By shutting down schools, authorities aimed to reduce the likelihood of violent confrontations and to allow law‑enforcement agencies to manage the large crowds more effectively.<sup>[5]</sup> The impact on students and teachers has been profound. Many families faced logistical challenges in arranging alternative childcare or tutoring arrangements, and students were left without formal instruction for an entire day. Teachers, who are also union members, expressed frustration at the disruption of their professional duties and the uncertainty surrounding the future of the pension system that directly affects their livelihoods. Some teachers reported that the closures were a necessary sacrifice to protect their collective bargaining power and to demonstrate the seriousness of their opposition to the pension reform.<sup>[6]</sup> Government officials responded cautiously. Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne, in a televised address, acknowledged the right of workers to protest but emphasised the importance of maintaining public services and ensuring the safety of students and staff. She stated that the government was open to dialogue with union representatives but would not compromise on the core objectives of the pension reform, citing the need to address France’s demographic challenges and fiscal deficits. She also urged all parties to find a peaceful resolution that would avoid further disruptions to education and other essential services.<sup>[7]</sup> Union leaders have called for a national dialogue that includes all stakeholders, including teachers, transport workers, and healthcare personnel. The teachers’ union demanded a complete overhaul of the pension reform, arguing that the points‑based system is inherently unfair and that the retirement age increase disproportionately burdens lower‑paid public‑sector workers. Transport unions have demanded that the government reconsider the reform’s impact on workers in physically demanding jobs, citing health and safety concerns. The healthcare sector, represented by the National Federation of Health Professionals, has expressed similar concerns, noting that the reform could lead to a shortage of experienced staff in hospitals and clinics.<sup>[8]</sup> The closures have sparked a broader debate about the role of education during civil unrest. Some education experts argue that schools should remain open as a stabilising force, providing continuity and a sense of normalcy for students during periods of societal upheaval. Others contend that the presence of students and teachers in public spaces could inadvertently fuel tensions and that temporary closures are a necessary measure to protect all parties involved. The debate highlights the complex balance between maintaining essential public services and ensuring the safety and security of citizens during large‑scale protests.<sup>[9]</sup> Looking ahead, the closures are expected to be temporary, with schools scheduled to reopen on Tuesday once the protests have subsided and safety conditions are reassured. However, the broader strike movement continues, and the government faces mounting pressure to address the concerns of workers across multiple sectors. The pension reform remains a contentious issue, with the potential for further disruptions if a compromise is not reached. The government’s willingness to engage in dialogue, coupled with the unions’ steadfast demands, will shape the trajectory of the protests and the future of France’s pension system.<sup>[10]</sup> In conclusion, the closure of roughly 400 schools on Monday was a significant moment in France’s ongoing protest against the pension reform law. It underscored the depth of opposition from teachers, transport workers, and other public‑sector employees, and highlighted the strategic use of school closures as a bargaining tool. While the government has expressed a willingness to engage in dialogue, the unions remain firm in their demands for a comprehensive overhaul of the pension system.
