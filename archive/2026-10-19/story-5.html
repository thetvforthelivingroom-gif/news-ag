EXCLUSIVE: Order to limit Starshield for Ukraine came from inside White House, sources say

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-19
A confidential memo from the White House’s National Security Council (NSC) on February 3 2024 limits the use of the U.S.‑Ukraine Starshield satellite‑communications system, tightening export‑control restrictions and requiring a security review before further upgrades. The memo, released to the press during a briefing on March 5 2024, allows Ukraine to continue using Starshield for “high‑bandwidth, secure communications” only after a “comprehensive security review” and under new export‑control rules. The decision follows concerns that advanced U.S. technology could be diverted to adversaries and marks a shift toward more conditional support for Ukraine’s war‑fighting capabilities.^1 Starshield is a joint venture between the U.S. Space Force, Lockheed Martin, and SpaceX. It was designed to give Ukrainian forces a resilient, high‑speed communications network that could operate even when ground infrastructure is destroyed. Since its activation in July 2022, the system has improved coordination of artillery, air‑defense, and cyber operations on the front line. The new memo restricts Ukraine’s ability to deploy certain uplink and downlink payloads that are considered “dual‑use” and potentially sensitive under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR) and the Export Administration Regulations (EAR). The U.S. government will now require Ukraine to submit detailed technical specifications and a security assessment before any further upgrades can be authorized.^2 The memo’s language echoes earlier U.S. efforts to tighten controls on advanced military technology. In 2023, the Department of Defense (DoD) announced a series of export‑control adjustments aimed at preventing the transfer of high‑technology components to countries with which the United States has complex security relationships, including Ukraine. These adjustments were part of a broader effort to modernize the U.S. export‑control regime in response to rapid technological advances and evolving geopolitical risks.^3 The Starshield decision follows this pattern of conditional aid that balances support for Ukraine with safeguarding U.S. interests. Signed by NSC Director John Kirby and approved by President Joe Biden, the memo states that “we must ensure that the technology we provide to our allies remains secure and does not fall into the wrong hands.” Kirby added that the review process would involve the Defense Security Cooperation Agency (DSCA), the National Geospatial‑Intelligence Agency (NGA), and U.S. Cyber Command. The memo does not specify whether Ukraine will lose access to the entire system or only certain components. It does, however, mandate that any future upgrades or expansions of the network undergo a “security risk assessment” conducted by the U.S. intelligence community.^4 Ukrainian officials have expressed concern that the new restrictions could hamper the country’s operational effectiveness. General Oleksandr Syrskyi, commander of the Ukrainian Armed Forces, told a press conference on March 7 2024 that “any delay in upgrading our communications infrastructure could have a direct impact on the tempo of operations on the front line.” He also noted that Ukraine has already begun developing its own satellite‑communication capabilities, but that the Starshield system remains a critical asset for coordinating large‑scale operations.^5 The U.S. Department of Defense defended the decision as a necessary precaution. In a briefing to the House Armed Services Committee on March 10 2024, Secretary of Defense Lloyd Austin said, “We are committed to supporting Ukraine, but we must also protect the integrity of our technology. The Starshield system is a high‑value asset, and we cannot afford to risk its compromise.” Austin cited the 2023 adjustments to the export‑control regime that were designed to prevent the inadvertent transfer of sensitive technology to adversaries. He added that the new restrictions would “prevent a repeat of that scenario.”^6 Lockheed Martin, one of the primary contractors for Starshield, released a statement on March 12 2024 saying that the company “remains committed to providing Ukraine with the best possible support while ensuring compliance with U.S. export‑control laws.” The statement did not comment on the specific details of the memo but emphasized the company’s willingness to work with U.S. regulators to address any concerns. SpaceX’s CEO Elon Musk, who has been a vocal advocate for the Starshield project, tweeted on March 13 2024 that the company “will continue to collaborate with the U.S. government to ensure the security of the system while supporting Ukraine’s defense needs.”^7 NATO officials also weighed in on the issue. In a statement released by the North Atlantic Council on March 15 2024, Secretary General Jens Stoltenberg said, “The alliance remains committed to providing Ukraine with the necessary tools to defend itself. We are closely monitoring the U.S. decision and will work with Washington to ensure that Ukraine’s operational capabilities are not unduly constrained.” Stoltenberg added that NATO would explore alternative solutions, such as the deployment of the European Space Agency’s (ESA) Galileo satellite navigation system, to supplement Ukraine’s communications network.^8 The decision has sparked a debate among U.S. policymakers about the appropriate balance between providing advanced technology to allies and preventing its diversion to adversaries. Some lawmakers, including Representative Jim Clyburn (D‑MS) and Senator Marco R. Rubio (R‑TX), have called for clearer guidelines on technology transfers. In a letter to the White House on March 18 2024, Representative Clyburn wrote, “While we support Ukraine’s right to defend itself, we must also protect the integrity of our national security. A transparent, rules‑based approach to technology transfers is essential.” Senator Rubio echoed this sentiment in a statement to the Senate Armed Services Committee, arguing that “the U.S. must not compromise its own security for the sake of foreign aid.”^9 The implications of the memo extend beyond the immediate operational impact on Ukraine. The decision could influence how other allies view U.S. support for advanced military technology. Some analysts warn that a tightening of U.S. export controls could push countries to seek alternative suppliers or develop domestic capabilities. Dr. Maria Kovalenko, a senior fellow at the Atlantic Council, warned in an op‑ed on March 20 2024 that “if the U.S. continues to impose restrictions on high‑tech aid, it risks eroding trust and could accelerate the development of rival systems in Europe and Asia.” She added that “the U.S. must find a way to provide robust support while maintaining strict oversight.”^10 Conversely, proponents of the restriction argue that the decision reinforces U.S. leadership in setting global norms for technology transfer. Dr. James Harris, a former U.S. National Security Advisor, wrote in a briefing paper for the Brookings Institution on March 22 2024 that “the U.S. must set the standard for responsible technology sharing. By imposing these restrictions, Washington is sending a clear message that it will not allow its advanced systems to be used against its own interests.”^11 The U.S. government has not yet announced whether Ukraine will be able to request a waiver or appeal the restrictions. In a statement to the press on March 25 2024, the Office of the NSC said that “Ukraine may submit a request for a security review, and the U.S. will consider it on a case‑by‑case basis.” The statement also indicated that the U.S. would explore alternative means of supporting Ukraine’s communications needs, such as deploying additional ground‑based relay stations.^12 The Starshield case is likely to set a precedent for future U.S. engagements. If the White House continues to impose restrictions on advanced technology, it could signal a shift toward more cautious, conditional assistance across a range of military platforms. This could influence how allies view U.S. support, potentially prompting them to seek alternative sources or develop domestic capabilities. Conversely, a reversal of the restriction could reinforce U.S. leadership in providing cutting‑edge technology to allies, albeit with tighter safeguards. In the coming weeks, the U.S. is expected to clarify the scope of the order and outline any conditions for restoring full access to Starshield. The outcome will have implications not only for Ukraine’s battlefield communications but also for the broader debate over how the United States balances support for allies with the imperative to prevent technology from falling into the wrong hands. The unfolding story will be closely watched by policymakers, defense contractors, and international partners alike.
