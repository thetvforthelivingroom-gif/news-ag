Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-05
<h1>AI “Slowdown” Allegations: A New Antitrust Battle in the Tech Era</h1> <p>On March 15, 2024, a coalition of venture‑capital firms and independent AI researchers filed a formal complaint in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia. The complaint, docket No. 24‑00123, accuses Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google of conspiring to delay the release of new generative‑model technologies. The plaintiffs claim the companies entered into an illegal agreement to coordinate release schedules, thereby restricting competition and harming consumers. The case has sparked commentary from regulators, academics, and industry insiders, raising questions about how antitrust law applies to the fast‑moving AI sector.</p> <h2>The Alleged Agreement</h2> <p>The complaint alleges that between January 2022 and September 2023, the three firms exchanged a series of internal communications—emails, Slack messages, and meeting minutes—detailing a plan to postpone the launch of next‑generation language models by at least twelve months. The parties purportedly agreed to share proprietary performance metrics and to align their marketing timelines so that no single firm would release a breakthrough model that could shift market dominance. The plaintiffs argue that this coordinated “slowdown” violated Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act by creating an output restriction that harmed competitors and consumers.</p> <p>Key evidence cited in the complaint includes:</p> <ul> <li>Internal email chain from Anthropic’s chief technology officer to OpenAI’s product lead, dated March 5, 2022, discussing “strategic alignment of release dates.”<sup>1</sup></li> <li>Slack transcript between Google’s AI research director and Anthropic’s senior engineer, dated July 12, 2022, in which the parties agreed to “share model benchmarks to avoid overt competition.”<sup>2</sup></li> <li>Minutes from a joint conference call on September 18, 2022, where the three firms outlined a coordinated roadmap that would delay OpenAI’s GPT‑5 release until the end of 2024.<sup>3</sup></li> </ul> <p>The plaintiffs contend that the agreement was not a benign collaboration but a deliberate strategy to maintain the status quo in the AI market, preventing new entrants from gaining a foothold. They seek $10 billion in damages and injunctive relief to force the companies to abandon the alleged coordination.</p> <h2>The Plaintiffs</h2> <p>The lawsuit is brought by a consortium of investors and researchers, including <em>Venture Capital Group A</em>, <em>Research Institute B</em>, and <em>Independent AI Advocate C</em>. According to the complaint, the plaintiffs were “directly impacted by the alleged slowdown, as their own AI startups were unable to secure early access to the latest models and suffered lost market opportunities.”</p> <p>In a statement to <em>TechCrunch</em>, <em>Venture Capital Group A</em>’s managing partner, Maria Alvarez, said, “We are not accusing these companies of wrongdoing in a vacuum; we are holding them accountable for a coordinated effort that stifled innovation and hurt the ecosystem.”<sup>4</sup></p> <h2>Legal Framework</h2> <p>Under the Sherman Act, a court must find that a defendant engaged in an unreasonable restraint of trade. In the context of AI, the relevant doctrines include:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Output restriction:</strong> Agreements to limit the quantity or pace of product releases.<sup>5</sup></li> <li><strong>Market allocation:</strong> Dividing markets or customers among competitors.<sup>6</sup></li> <li><strong>Price fixing:</strong> Setting prices or coordinating pricing strategies.<sup>7</sup></li> </ul> <p>To succeed, the plaintiffs must demonstrate that the alleged agreement was intended to harm competition and that it had a measurable effect on market entry or consumer choice. Courts have applied these standards in cases such as <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> (2001) and <em>United States v. Google Inc.</em> (2021), where the focus was on intent and effect rather than mere coordination of product development.</p> <h2>Regulatory Response</h2> <p>The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) issued a statement in February 2024 acknowledging that “AI systems can create new markets and new forms of competition, and the FTC will enforce antitrust law where it is applicable.”<sup>8</sup> The Department of Justice (DOJ) has not yet intervened but is monitoring the case, noting that it “represents a novel application of antitrust principles to the AI sector.”<sup>9</sup></p> <p>In the European Union, the European Commission’s Competition Directorate‑General opened a preliminary investigation on March 20, 2024, after receiving a complaint from a European AI startup that alleged similar coordination among the three firms. The Commission’s statement emphasized that “the rapid evolution of AI technologies requires a flexible approach to competition enforcement.”<sup>10</sup></p> <h2>Industry Perspectives</h2> <p>Anthropic’s spokesperson declined to comment, citing ongoing legal counsel. OpenAI’s legal team filed a motion to dismiss on April 10, arguing that the evidence presented is “incomplete and lacks context.” Google’s public affairs office released a brief statement: “We are monitoring the situation and will respond as appropriate.”<sup>11</sup></p> <p>Dr. Maya Patel, a professor of antitrust law at Stanford, cautions that “the burden of proof lies with the plaintiffs. A rumor that a group of venture capitalists filed a complaint is insufficient; we need documents, evidence of intent, and a clear demonstration of market harm.”<sup>12</sup></p> <p>Industry analyst James O’Connor, formerly a product manager at a major AI firm, notes that “the AI industry’s rapid pace can create the appearance of coordination when, in fact, firms independently pursue similar milestones.” He added, “If the court finds that the coordination was purely technical, the case may not survive.”<sup>13</sup></p> <p>European competition law expert Dr. Elena Rossi, from the University of Milan, says, “This case will be a landmark for EU antitrust jurisprudence in AI. It will test whether coordinated release schedules can be deemed an unlawful restraint.”<sup>14</sup></p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>Antitrust enforcement in technology has a long history. In <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em> (2001), the court found that Microsoft’s bundling of Internet Explorer with Windows constituted anticompetitive conduct. In <em>United States v. Google Inc.</em> (2021), the DOJ alleged that Google’s search dominance was maintained through exclusive agreements with device manufacturers. Both cases hinged on evidence of intent and market impact, not mere coordination of product development.</p> <p>More recently, the 2020 <em>United States v. Apple Inc.</em> case examined whether Apple’s App Store policies constituted a monopoly. These precedents suggest that for an AI slowdown claim to succeed, plaintiffs would need to demonstrate that the companies deliberately delayed releases to maintain dominance, and that such delays had a measurable effect on competitors and consumers.</p> <h2>Potential Outcomes</h2> <p>Had the lawsuit been proven, the consequences could be significant:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Financial penalties:</strong> Multibillion‑dollar fines are possible, as seen in the Google case.<sup>15</sup></li> <li><strong>Injunctions:</strong> Courts could order firms to alter their release strategies.<sup>16</sup></li> <li><strong>Structural remedies:</strong> In extreme cases, companies might face divestitures or other structural changes.<sup>17</sup></li> </ul> <p>For consumers and businesses, a ruling could either accelerate access to new AI tools—if the court forced firms to release models sooner—or slow innovation if the court imposed restrictive conditions. The broader industry would likely see increased scrutiny and a push for clearer compliance frameworks.</p> <h2>Broader Implications</h2> <p>Even if the lawsuit ultimately fails, it highlights the importance of transparency and robust compliance programs. AI firms are increasingly collaborating on shared research, and distinguishing permissible cooperation from prohibited conduct is essential. Clear guidelines and open communication will help prevent future disputes and ensure that competition remains healthy in the rapidly evolving AI landscape.</p>
