More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-05
<p>On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will close as part of a nationwide protest that has turned the education system into an unlikely battleground for the country’s pension reform. The closures were announced by local municipal councils and regional education authorities after a wave of strikes that began last week, reflecting a deepening crisis that intertwines fiscal policy, labor rights, and the future of public education.</p> <h2>The Immediate Trigger</h2> <p>The decision to shut schools was made on the morning of Friday, 2 October, when the Syndicat National des Enseignants du Second degré (SNES) called for a national strike to oppose the pension reform that would raise the retirement age for civil servants from 62 to 64. Teachers in 18 regions walked out of their classrooms, leaving many schools empty. In response, the prefectures of 12 departments—most of them in Île‑de‑France, Auvergne‑Auvergne, and Grand Est—issued orders suspending school operations on Monday, 5 October. The Ministry of National Education confirmed the closures in a brief statement, acknowledging the unprecedented scale of the disruptions but declining to comment on long‑term implications.</p> <h2>Why the Pension Reform Matters</h2> <p>The reform, announced in 2022, was presented by the government as a necessary step to ensure the sustainability of France’s social security system amid an ageing population. It proposes raising the retirement age for civil servants from 62 to 64 and overhauling the calculation of pension benefits. Critics argue that the changes disproportionately affect low‑paid public sector workers, including teachers, and erode benefits built over decades. The reform has become a rallying point for labor unions across the country, and the teachers’ strike is the latest manifestation of that discontent.</p> <h2>The Role of Teachers’ Unions</h2> <p>The SNES, representing secondary‑school teachers, has led the protests. Its leadership calls for a halt to the pension reform, better working conditions, and smaller class sizes. In a statement released on 3 October, the union warned that higher retirement ages could lead to a shortage of experienced teachers, compromising education quality. The SNES demands a national dialogue with the government and a guarantee that reforms will not be implemented without a thorough review of their impact on teachers’ careers.</p> <h2>Student Activism</h2> <p>Students have joined the protests through the Union Nationale des Étudiants (UNE), the main national student union. The UNE called for a national student strike on 4 October, demanding that their voices be heard in the debate over pension reforms and broader educational policy. While the UNE’s demands are not directly tied to the pension reform, they reflect a broader concern that the reforms will indirectly affect the future of public education.</p> <h2>Parents and Local Authorities</h2> <p>Parents’ reactions are mixed. Some view the strikes as a legitimate defense of teachers’ rights; others worry about the disruption to their children’s education. A survey by the French Institute of Public Opinion (IFOP) on 4 October found that 58 % of parents supported the teachers’ demands, while 43 % were concerned about the impact on their children’s academic progress. Local authorities have been caught between teachers’ demands and the need to maintain public order. Many municipal councils have urged teachers to return to the classroom and to find alternative ways to continue instruction, such as online lessons.</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>The current wave of protests echoes the 2018 pension reform demonstrations, during which the government faced widespread strikes by teachers, civil servants, and other public sector workers. Schools were closed for several days, forcing the government to negotiate with unions. In 2023, nationwide strikes by public sector workers—including teachers—also saw schools shut down for extended periods. These precedents illustrate how pension reform has become a recurring source of tension between the state, teachers, and students, and how school closures have become a common tactic in labor disputes.</p> <h2>The Bigger Picture</h2> <p>The protests and school closures raise questions about social cohesion in France. They highlight deep divisions between the state, teachers, and students, and raise concerns about the future of the education system. The government’s insistence on proceeding with the reforms has met resistance from teachers and students, who view the reforms as a threat to their livelihoods and to education quality.</p> <h2>What to Watch</h2> <h3>Online Learning Arrangements</h3> <p>In response to the closures, many teachers and students have turned to online learning platforms. The government announced plans to expand digital learning resources and to provide additional support for students unable to attend school in person. However, the effectiveness of these arrangements remains uncertain, as many students lack reliable internet connections or devices. Teachers and students have expressed skepticism, arguing that digital resources cannot fully replace in‑person learning.</p> <h3>Impact on Academic Calendars</h3> <p>The closures disrupted the academic calendar for many students and parents. The government has announced plans to reschedule exams and to offer extra support for those unable to attend school. Yet, doubts persist about the feasibility of these measures, given the uneven access to technology and the need for adequate preparation time.</p> <h3>Political Stakes</h3> <p>The school closures and protests have challenged the legitimacy of the government’s reform agenda. They have also sparked debate about the role of teachers and students in shaping policy. The protests underscore the tension between fiscal sustainability and the protection of public sector workers’ rights.</p> <h3>Possible Policy Concessions</h3> <p>Under mounting pressure, the government has signaled a willingness to engage in dialogue with teachers and students. Whether the reforms will be modified or abandoned remains unclear. The outcome will depend on the ability of all parties to find a compromise that balances fiscal needs with the interests of educators and learners.</p> <h3>Long‑Term Implications</h3> <p>The protests and school closures will shape the future of France’s education system. The crisis highlights the need for a sustainable pension framework that does not undermine the quality of public education. How the government, teachers, and students navigate this crisis will determine whether a lasting solution can be reached.</p> <p>In sum, the current wave of protests and school closures reflects a broader debate about the state’s role, teachers’ rights, and students’ futures. The outcome will hinge on whether the parties can reconcile fiscal objectives with the need to preserve a robust, accessible education system.</p>
