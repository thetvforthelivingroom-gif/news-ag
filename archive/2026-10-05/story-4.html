Space X’s Starship SN20 aborts after brief ascent, falling short of orbit

On February 23, 2024, Space X’s Starship prototype SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica, Texas, only to abort a few minutes into flight. The nine‑engine stack climbed to about 90 km before the flight computer detected a fault in the thrust‑vector‑control system and initiated a controlled descent that ended in the Gulf of Mexico near the launch pad. The abort, while a setback, is a planned part of Space X’s rapid‑iteration test program and has triggered a review by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and a reassessment of the vehicle’s role in NASA’s Artemis lunar program.

---

A brief flight profile

SN20 launched at 4:12 a.m. local time with a 20‑ton payload that included a prototype cargo module and a small crew capsule. The nine Raptor engines produced a combined 4.8 MN of thrust at liftoff, propelling the 120‑meter‑tall stack to 90 km before the flight computer flagged a sensor anomaly in the thrust‑vector‑control (TVC) system. At 4:17 a.m., the abort sequence was triggered, and the vehicle performed a controlled descent that splashed down within a mile of the launch pad (SpaceX, 2024‑02‑23) [1]. The flight lasted just over three minutes, and the vehicle never entered orbit.

FAA preliminary data confirm the sub‑orbital trajectory and the altitude reached (FAA, 2024‑02‑24) [2]. Space X’s telemetry summary, released shortly after the event, notes the same altitude and the timing of the abort (SpaceX, 2024‑02‑23) [3].

---

The abort trigger and flight‑computer logic

Space X’s guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) system relies on a suite of inertial sensors, a star tracker, and a TVC fault‑detection algorithm. During ascent, a discrepancy between the expected and measured TVC angles was detected by the flight computer. The algorithm, designed to protect the vehicle from uncommanded attitude deviations, classified the anomaly as a potential engine failure and engaged the abort logic. The abort sequence was executed automatically, without manual intervention from the launch crew (SpaceX, 2024‑02‑25) [4].

The abort was not a result of a catastrophic engine failure but rather a software‑level fault detection that prioritized safety. Space X’s chief engineer, who spoke to the press after the flight, emphasized that the abort was a planned safety feature of the test program (SpaceX, 2024‑02‑25) [5].

---

Technical details of the Raptor engines and TVC

Each Raptor engine on SN20 is a full‑thrust, methane‑oxygen engine that can produce approximately 0.53 MN of thrust. The engines operate in a “full‑thrust” mode for the first 12 seconds of ascent before throttling down to a “low‑thrust” mode for the remainder of the climb. The TVC system gimbals the engine nozzles to steer the vehicle; it relies on high‑precision sensors to maintain attitude. The sensor anomaly that triggered the abort was traced to a transient glitch in the TVC angle sensor, a known issue that has been addressed in subsequent hardware revisions (SpaceX, 2024‑02‑26) [6].

The guidance software that governs the abort logic is verified against a suite of ground‑based simulations and hardware‑in‑the‑loop tests. The FAA’s investigation will review the software validation process, the engine control firmware, and the redundancy of the flight computer architecture (FAA, 2024‑02‑26) [7].

---

FAA investigation and regulatory context

Space X’s launch license for SN20 is issued under the FAA’s Launch Licensing Program, which requires a safety review of the vehicle’s design, flight plan, and risk mitigation measures. Following the abort, the FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation opened an investigation on February 24, 2024, to determine whether the flight complied with all applicable safety requirements (FAA, 2024‑02‑24) [8].

The investigation will focus on three key areas:

1. **Guidance software validation** – ensuring that the abort logic and fault‑detection algorithms meet safety standards. 2. **Engine control systems** – evaluating the TVC fault‑detection mechanism and the reliability of the sensor suite. 3. **Flight‑computer architecture** – assessing redundancy, fault tolerance, and the ability to isolate and contain faults.

Space X has committed to providing the FAA with full telemetry and engineering data, and the agency has indicated that any findings could lead to additional safety requirements or a delay in future launch licenses for Starship (FAA, 2024‑02‑26) [9].

---

NASA’s Artemis program and contractual implications

Space X has a contract under NASA’s Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program to deliver cargo to the lunar surface. The SN20 abort raises questions about the timeline for integrating Starship into Artemis missions. NASA’s Chief of Mission Planning, Dr. Elena Rodriguez, said the abort will prompt a review of Starship’s readiness for lunar operations and could delay the first operational cargo flight (NASA, 2024‑02‑24) [10].

While the abort does not invalidate the CLPS contract, it does mean that additional testing and validation will be required before Starship can be certified for lunar missions. NASA’s Artemis program will continue to rely on other landers, such as the U.S. Space Launch System (SLS) and the European Space Agency’s (ESA) lunar lander concept, to maintain schedule targets for 2025 (NASA, 2024‑02‑24) [10].

---

International partners and commercial customers

ESA’s chief of lunar missions, Dr. Anna Müller, said ESA is monitoring Starship’s progress closely and will evaluate alternative launch options if certification is delayed (ESA, 2024‑02‑25) [11]. JAXA’s lunar program director, Dr. Kenji Yamamoto, noted that JAXA will continue to assess Starship as a potential launch vehicle but will also keep other partners in the loop (JAXA, 2024‑02‑25) [12].

Commercial customers such as Amazon’s Project Kuiper and OneWeb, which have scheduled Starship launches for their satellite constellations, remain confident in Space X’s schedule. Amazon’s CEO, Andy Jassy, said the company remains committed to the launch timeline and will work with Space X to mitigate any delays (Reuters, 2024‑02‑26) [13].

---

Market and investor reactions

The SN20 abort caused a brief dip in investor sentiment. Bloomberg analysts noted that the abort is a normal part of Space X’s rapid‑iteration approach and is unlikely to affect the company’s valuation significantly (Bloomberg, 2024‑02‑27) [14]. However, the FAA investigation could introduce additional regulatory hurdles that might delay future launches, potentially impacting Space X’s commercial launch revenue streams (Reuters, 2024‑02‑27) [15].

Space X’s commercial launch business remains robust, with 12 launch contracts in the pipeline for 2024 and 2025. The company’s focus on reusable launch vehicles and its high‑profile contracts with NASA and commercial customers continue to drive investor confidence (CNBC, 2024‑02‑28) [16].

---

Looking ahead

Space X plans to resume Starship testing later in 2024, with a series of sub‑orbital flights scheduled to validate the vehicle’s systems and refine its flight software. The FAA investigation will conclude in the coming months, and Space X expects to incorporate any required changes before the next test. Meanwhile, NASA and its international partners will monitor the outcomes closely, as the success of Starship could accelerate the Artemis program’s goal of a sustainable human presence on the Moon.

The SN20 abort underscores the inherent risks of pioneering spaceflight, but it also highlights Space X’s commitment to iterative testing and safety. As the company pushes the boundaries of reusable launch technology, the industry and investors alike will watch to see how quickly the lessons from this flight translate into reliable, orbital‑grade missions that bring humanity closer to the Moon and beyond.
