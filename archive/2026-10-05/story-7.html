Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-05
When a new generation of radio telescopes turned their gaze toward a nearby star, a faint, polarized burst of radio waves appeared to originate from an orbiting planet. A team of astronomers has announced the first tentative detection of radio emission from an exoplanet, a finding that could open a new window onto planetary magnetism, atmospheric escape, and the prospects for life beyond the Solar System. The signal, reported in a preprint posted to arXiv, was captured with the upgraded Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array (VLA) in New Mexico and the Low‑Frequency Array (LOFAR) in the Netherlands. While the data are publicly available and the analysis pipeline is open‑source, the result remains unconfirmed by independent observations and is subject to ongoing scrutiny.

What the team claims

The preprint, authored by Smith, Zhang, and colleagues from the University of Cambridge, describes a 10–30 MHz burst that appears to be circularly polarized and temporally variable on timescales of minutes. The authors associate the signal with the hot Jupiter HD 189733 b, a 1.14 R<sub>J</sub> planet that orbits a K1 V star every 2.2 days. By applying a machine‑learning classifier trained on synthetic auroral spectra, the team isolated the burst from terrestrial interference and stellar radio noise. They report a signal‑to‑noise ratio of 4.2 σ and a spectral cutoff near 15 MHz, which, according to the cyclotron‑maser instability theory, implies a magnetic field strength of roughly 10 G at the planet’s magnetic pole. This would correspond to a magnetic dipole moment of about 0.5 M<sub>⊕</sub>, comparable to that of Earth.

The authors emphasize that the detection is the first time radio emission has been attributed to an exoplanetary magnetosphere, a milestone that would provide a direct probe of planetary magnetic fields—an otherwise elusive property. They have made the raw visibilities, calibration tables, and the Python code for the machine‑learning pipeline available on a public GitHub repository, inviting other groups to reproduce the analysis and test the robustness of the signal.

The scientific backdrop

The idea that exoplanets could emit detectable radio waves dates back to the 1970s, when Zarka and colleagues proposed that the cyclotron‑maser instability could generate decametric radiation from magnetized planets [1]. In the Solar System, Jupiter’s powerful radio bursts have been studied extensively with ground‑based and space‑borne instruments, revealing a close link between magnetic field strength, rotation, and stellar wind interactions [2]. Extrapolating these models to exoplanets suggested that hot Jupiters, with their rapid rotation and close proximity to their host stars, could be especially bright radio emitters [3].

Despite this theoretical promise, attempts to detect exoplanetary radio emission for decades have largely failed. The expected flux densities are orders of magnitude below the stellar radio background, and many low‑frequency arrays lacked the sensitivity or calibration stability to isolate such faint signals. Recent upgrades to the VLA’s L‑band receivers and the deployment of wide‑band, phased‑array feeds on LOFAR have improved sensitivity at 10–30 MHz by more than a factor of ten, creating a new window of opportunity [4]. Concurrently, advances in machine‑learning techniques have enabled automated identification of transient, polarized bursts in large data sets, a method that the HD 189733 b team has adopted.

Implications for planetary magnetism and habitability

If confirmed, the detection would constitute the first direct measurement of an exoplanet’s magnetic field. Planetary magnetospheres play a crucial role in shielding atmospheres from stellar wind erosion, a process that can strip volatile gases and render a planet uninhabitable [5]. For HD 189733 b, a magnetic field of ~10 G would suggest a modest protective envelope, potentially influencing the planet’s atmospheric escape rate and the structure of its ionosphere. Moreover, the ability to measure magnetic fields across a diverse sample of exoplanets would allow astronomers to test dynamo models that link interior composition, rotation, and magnetic activity [6].

The technique also offers a complementary diagnostic to optical and infrared observations. While transit spectroscopy can reveal atmospheric composition, radio observations provide insight into magnetic environments and the interaction between a planet’s magnetosphere and its host star’s wind. Together, these methods could help determine whether a planet’s magnetic field is strong enough to retain a substantial atmosphere over geological timescales.

Looking ahead

The community’s next steps will involve repeated observations of HD 189733 b with both the VLA and LOFAR, as well as targeted searches for similar bursts around other hot Jupiters and, eventually, smaller, potentially habitable planets. The upcoming Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will deliver unprecedented sensitivity at low frequencies, making it a powerful tool for confirming and extending these findings. In parallel, theoretical work will refine models of exoplanetary magnetospheres, incorporating the new observational constraints to improve predictions of radio emission strength and spectral characteristics.

While the current detection remains tentative, the study demonstrates that modern radio instrumentation, combined with sophisticated data‑analysis pipelines, can probe planetary magnetic fields beyond our Solar System. If future observations confirm the signal, it will mark a watershed moment in exoplanet science, opening a new avenue for exploring the magnetic lives of distant worlds and their potential to support life.
