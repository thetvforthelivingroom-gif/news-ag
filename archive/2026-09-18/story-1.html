Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-18
Satellites have revealed that the planet’s two largest ice sheets have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice in the past 47 years, a loss that is accelerating and already contributing to global sea‑level rise¹. The decline translates into a measurable rise of about 3–4 mm per year over the last decade², far exceeding the 1 mm yr⁻¹ estimate that appeared in earlier popular accounts. The consequences touch coastlines, ecosystems, and economies worldwide.

What the satellites have taught us

The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO have been the workhorses of ice‑mass monitoring. By tracking subtle changes in Earth’s gravitational field, they reveal how mass moves from the ice sheets to the oceans³. Complementary missions such as ESA’s CryoSat‑2 and NASA’s ICESat‑3 provide high‑resolution topographic data, allowing researchers to separate surface melt, ice‑flow acceleration, and basal melting beneath ice shelves⁴. Together, these datasets show that Greenland has shed about 8.5 trillion tons of ice, while Antarctica has lost roughly 3.5 trillion tons in the same period⁵. The loss is uneven: West Antarctica’s marine‑terminating glaciers retreat fastest, whereas parts of East Antarctica remain relatively stable.

These observations are corroborated by in‑situ measurements. Tide gauges along the world’s coasts record a consistent rise in sea level, while GPS stations on land record vertical land motion that must be accounted for when interpreting satellite data⁶. The convergence of gravimetric, altimetric, and ground‑based evidence paints a coherent picture: the cryosphere is in rapid decline, and the rate of loss is accelerating.

A historical perspective

Greenland and Antarctica together hold about 90 % of the world’s freshwater ice. Their mass balance has fluctuated in concert with natural climatic cycles. During the Little Ice Age (≈ 1300–1850 CE), the Greenland Ice Sheet expanded, while the Medieval Warm Period (≈ 950–1250 CE) saw a modest retreat⁷. However, the magnitude and speed of the current ice loss far exceed those of any natural cycle in the Holocene. Pre‑satellite estimates based on ice cores and geomorphology suggested a decline of only a few hundred thousand tonnes per year during the twentieth century, whereas satellite data now show losses of several trillion tonnes over three decades⁸. This stark contrast underscores the unprecedented nature of anthropogenic climate change.

The science behind the melt

Atmospheric warming drives surface melt on both ice sheets. In Greenland, higher temperatures increase the frequency and intensity of melt events, reducing snow accumulation and enhancing surface runoff⁹. In Antarctica, the situation is more complex. Oceanic warming is now the dominant driver of ice‑sheet mass loss in West Antarctica, as warmer waters erode ice shelves from below and destabilize grounded ice¹⁰. The interplay between atmospheric and oceanic forces is captured in the satellite data, which show both surface mass balance changes and internal ice‑flow acceleration. Climate models that incorporate these processes project that if global temperatures continue to rise, an additional 3–5 trillion tons of ice could be lost by 2100, potentially raising sea level by 0.5–1 m¹¹.

Human voices on a shared crisis

While the scientific consensus is clear, the human dimension of ice loss is equally complex. Indigenous communities in Greenland, such as the Inuit, have observed changes in sea ice extent and the availability of traditional hunting grounds for decades. They argue that the rapid loss of ice threatens their cultural heritage and food security¹². In Antarctica, the Antarctic Treaty System governs the continent’s use, but the retreat of ice shelves exposes new land that could be subject to mining or scientific research, raising questions about governance and environmental protection¹³.

Industry stakeholders are also taking notice. Shipping companies operating in the Arctic are lobbying for clearer regulations on ice navigation, while mining firms in Greenland are exploring the potential for resource extraction on newly exposed land. These perspectives highlight the need for a balanced approach that protects ecosystems while respecting economic interests.

Policy, finance, and the future

The World Bank’s 2023 report estimates that by 2050, the cumulative cost of sea‑level rise mitigation and adaptation could exceed $2 trillion globally, with developing nations bearing the brunt of the burden¹⁴. These costs include coastal defenses, relocation of infrastructure, and loss of agricultural productivity. Conversely, investment in renewable energy, carbon capture, and reforestation could offset some of these costs by curbing future ice loss¹⁵.

International policy efforts are underway to address the root cause of ice loss: global warming. The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, aims to limit the temperature rise to well below 2 °C, with a target of 1.5 °C. The most recent climate negotiations at COP28 in Dubai (2023) reaffirmed the commitment to the 1.5 °C goal, but also highlighted the need for more ambitious emissions reductions¹⁶. The next UNFCCC conference, COP29 in 2024, will be a critical juncture for setting the ambition of future global emissions targets. If the world fails to meet these targets, the acceleration of ice loss and sea‑level rise will likely continue unabated.

Looking ahead

Future satellite missions will refine our understanding of ice dynamics. ESA’s CryoSat‑2 has already been complemented by its successor, CryoSat‑3, which will provide higher spatial resolution and longer time series¹⁷. NASA’s upcoming Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission will also contribute valuable data on ocean circulation and sea‑level changes. Advances in data assimilation and machine learning are improving the predictive power of climate models, allowing scientists to attribute ice loss to specific climatic drivers with greater confidence.

Public awareness and engagement remain essential. Educational initiatives that translate complex satellite data into actionable information can empower communities to advocate for climate resilience. As the cryosphere continues to shrink, the collective response—scientific, political, and societal—will determine whether the current loss of 12 trillion tons becomes a historical footnote or a catalyst for transformative change.
