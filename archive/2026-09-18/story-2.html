Testosterone scripts for women rise, along with denials and delays

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-18
<h1>Testosterone Prescriptions for Women Surge, While Denials and Delays Persist</h1> <p>In recent years, the number of testosterone prescriptions written for women has risen sharply. The trend has drawn attention from clinicians, insurers, and patient advocates, all of whom are grappling with questions about safety, efficacy, and access. While some see the uptick as evidence that women’s hormonal needs are finally being recognized, others point to persistent barriers—denials and delays—that undermine the promise of this therapy.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>Over the past five years, the rate of new testosterone prescriptions for female patients has more than doubled, according to a series of reports in leading medical journals. The increase is especially pronounced among younger women and those with conditions such as hypogonadism and certain forms of depression [1].</p> <p>However, the rise in prescribing has been accompanied by a rise in insurance denials. Many insurers cite a lack of robust evidence for long‑term safety and therefore delay approvals, often requiring extensive prior‑authorization paperwork [2]. These delays have sparked patient‑led campaigns demanding clearer guidelines and faster access.</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>Historically, testosterone therapy has been reserved for men. Women were largely excluded from clinical trials and guideline recommendations. Early studies in the 1990s suggested potential benefits for women with low libido or post‑menopausal symptoms, but the evidence was limited and often confounded by small sample sizes [1].</p> <p>In the last decade, a growing body of research has clarified testosterone’s role in female physiology. Professional societies have begun to update their recommendations, but the medical community remains divided. Some clinicians advocate broader use, citing improved quality of life, while others caution against off‑label prescribing without long‑term safety data.</p> <p>Regulatory agencies have also grappled with the issue. The Food and Drug Administration has issued guidance encouraging further research but has not approved testosterone for routine use in women. Meanwhile, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services have piloted coverage models that could streamline access for patients with documented hormonal deficiencies [3]. These policy shifts reflect a broader trend toward personalized medicine, but they also highlight the tension between innovation and risk management.</p> <h2>Perspectives from Patients, Clinicians, and Insurers</h2> <p>Patient advocacy groups argue that denials reflect deeper biases within the healthcare system that question the legitimacy of women’s hormonal needs. “We’re told that our bodies are not ‘normal,’” one advocate says, noting that denial letters often reference a lack of “clinical evidence” that, in practice, reflects limited research on women [2]. These groups call for more rigorous, gender‑specific studies.</p> <p>From a clinical standpoint, some endocrinologists emphasize individualized assessment. They point out that testosterone can benefit women with certain endocrine disorders, but that the therapeutic window is narrow and side‑effects—such as hirsutism, acne, and mood changes—must be carefully monitored [1]. Others caution that the rush to prescribe may outpace the accumulation of safety data, potentially exposing patients to unforeseen risks.</p> <p>Insurance companies frame their denials as a matter of cost‑effectiveness. They argue that without definitive evidence of long‑term benefit, covering testosterone therapy could lead to unnecessary expenditures. Recent studies, however, suggest that improved hormonal balance may reduce healthcare costs associated with depression, chronic fatigue, and other comorbidities, raising the question of whether insurers’ risk assessments are fully aligned with patient outcomes [3].</p> <h2>What to Watch: Future Implications</h2> <p>The trajectory of testosterone therapy for women hinges on several key developments:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Clinical research</strong> – Ongoing randomized controlled trials are exploring dosage, delivery methods, and long‑term safety. Their results could inform both clinical guidelines and insurance policies.</li> <li><strong>Regulatory stance</strong> – Agencies may adjust their positions as new data emerge, potentially expanding approved indications or clarifying labeling requirements.</li> <li><strong>Policy initiatives</strong> – Medicare pilot programs and other coverage models could set precedents for balancing innovation with fiscal prudence.</li> </ul> <p>Patient advocacy will likely remain a powerful force, especially as social media amplifies personal stories of benefit and harm. These narratives can influence public opinion and, by extension, the political will to fund research or adjust coverage policies. Finally, the broader medical community’s willingness to engage in interdisciplinary dialogue—combining endocrinology, psychiatry, and health economics—will shape how testosterone therapy is integrated into comprehensive women’s health care.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The rise in testosterone prescriptions for women reflects evolving science, shifting policy, and persistent systemic challenges. While the potential benefits are significant, the accompanying denials and delays underscore the need for clearer evidence, more inclusive research, and equitable access mechanisms. The coming years will reveal whether the medical community can reconcile these competing priorities and deliver safe, effective hormonal care to women worldwide. [1][2][3]</p>
