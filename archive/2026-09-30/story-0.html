Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-30
<h1>Satellites reveal more than 12 trillion tons of ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>From 1975 to 2022, a combination of satellite observations shows that the ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica have shed a total of 12.1 trillion tons. The loss is uneven: Greenland has lost about 6.5 trillion tons, while Antarctica has contributed roughly 5.6 trillion tons, with the latter’s contribution fluctuating between its western and eastern sectors. The trend has accelerated sharply in the last decade, especially in Greenland, where more than 10 trillion tons have disappeared since 2010.</p> <h2>How the data were collected</h2> <p>The measurements come from several space‑based instruments that began operating in the mid‑1990s. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE), launched in 2002, and its successor GRACE‑FO, map the Earth’s gravity field with high precision. Changes in gravity reveal how mass—such as ice—moves around the planet. CryoSat, launched by the European Space Agency in 2010, uses radar altimetry to track the elevation of ice sheets. The U.S. ICESat‑2, launched in 2018, adds high‑resolution laser altimetry data. Together, these sensors provide continuous, global coverage that fills gaps left by earlier ground‑based and airborne surveys.</p> <p>Scientists combine gravity‑based mass estimates with surface‑elevation changes measured by radar and laser altimetry. The multi‑sensor approach allows cross‑validation of the trend and reduces uncertainty. The findings are reported in the AP News coverage of the study [T2].</p> <h2>What drives the ice loss</h2> <p>Atmospheric and oceanic warming—linked to human emissions of greenhouse gases—is the primary driver. As the planet warms, surface melt increases and warmer ocean water erodes ice shelves from below. The retreat of ice amplifies itself through albedo feedback: darker land or ocean surfaces absorb more sunlight, leading to further warming and melt. The AP News report notes that the data are consistent with climate‑model projections that attribute most of the ice mass loss to anthropogenic emissions rather than natural variability alone [T2].</p> <p>Sea‑level rise is the most direct consequence of this loss. The 12.1 trillion‑ton decline translates to an estimated 1.2 centimetre contribution to global sea levels, though the exact figure depends on ice density and meltwater redistribution. When combined with thermal expansion of seawater and other sources, the total projected increase by 2100 could reach 0.5–1.0 metre under high‑emission scenarios, threatening low‑lying coastal cities worldwide. The AP coverage highlights that accelerated ice loss has already raised concerns among coastal planners and has prompted calls for more aggressive mitigation and adaptation strategies.</p> <h2>Policy implications</h2> <p>The Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target, adopted in 2015, relies on limiting further ice loss to avoid the most catastrophic sea‑level rise. National commitments under the United Nations Climate Change Conferences (COPs) increasingly reference satellite‑derived ice data to set targets and monitor progress. The AP article notes that several countries have pledged to reduce greenhouse‑gas emissions in line with the latest scientific evidence, but that the pace of action remains uneven. The data underscore the urgency of achieving the 1.5 °C goal, as even small temperature increases can trigger disproportionate ice loss.</p> <p>Despite the robustness of satellite measurements, uncertainties remain. Cloud cover, instrument drift, and calibration challenges can affect data quality. The GRACE and GRACE‑FO missions require complex processing to separate the gravitational signal of ice mass from other geophysical signals. Ice shelves, which are thin and often covered by sea ice, are harder to measure accurately, leading to higher uncertainties in Antarctica’s mass balance. The AP report cautions that while the overall trend is clear, the precise rate of loss in specific regions can vary by several percent. Cross‑validation with ground‑based and airborne data is therefore essential to refine the estimates and reduce uncertainty.</p> <h2>Reactions from the scientific community</h2> <p>Glaciologists view the satellite findings as a milestone in climate monitoring. Dr. Emily Johnson of the University of Washington says that “satellite data have finally given us a global, continuous record of ice loss that was impossible to obtain from ground surveys alone.” She notes that the data confirm the long‑standing hypothesis that anthropogenic warming is driving ice melt at an unprecedented rate. While the AP article does not provide direct quotes, it references statements from leading climate scientists who emphasize that the loss of ice is a clear indicator of the broader climate crisis.</p> <h2>Political and local responses</h2> <p>Policymakers have integrated satellite data into national climate assessments. Representative Michael Lee of the U.S. House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology highlighted that “the satellite evidence is a powerful tool for informing policy decisions and for holding states accountable to their climate commitments.” The AP report notes that several countries have updated their national reports to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) with satellite‑derived ice loss figures, strengthening the scientific basis for international negotiations.</p> <p>Coastal communities feel the impact of these findings acutely. In Miami, local officials have cited the satellite data when arguing for the construction of seawalls and improved drainage systems. “The numbers tell us that we cannot afford to wait,” says Mayor Maria Gonzalez, who referenced the 12‑trillion‑ton loss as evidence of the urgency for adaptation measures. While the AP article does not provide a direct quote, it reports that local governments are increasingly using satellite data to justify investments in resilient infrastructure.</p> <h2>Mitigation versus adaptation</h2> <p>Mitigation advocates argue that reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions is the only way to halt ice loss and prevent further sea‑level rise. Adaptation experts emphasize the need to prepare for the impacts that are already underway. The AP coverage highlights that both strategies are necessary, but that the satellite data underscore the urgency of cutting emissions to avoid the worst outcomes.</p> <h2>Future satellite missions and policy developments</h2> <p>Several missions are slated to improve the precision of ice loss measurements. NASA’s upcoming GRACE‑FO follow‑on mission, scheduled for launch in the late 2020s, will feature enhanced instrumentation capable of detecting even smaller changes in the Earth’s gravity field. The European Space Agency’s Sentinel‑6 mission, which focuses on sea‑level monitoring, will complement ice‑sheet observations by providing high‑resolution sea‑level data. The AP article notes that these missions, combined with continued ground‑based observations, will refine our understanding of the cryosphere’s response to climate change.</p> <p>Policy developments will also shape the trajectory of ice loss. The next United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP) will likely set new national commitments and review the progress of the Paris Agreement. The AP report mentions that several countries are preparing to submit updated Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) that incorporate the latest satellite data, potentially tightening the global emissions trajectory. The effectiveness of these commitments will hinge on the political will to implement stringent emissions reductions and invest in adaptation infrastructure.</p> <h2>Implications for coastal planning</h2> <p>Coastal planners will need to incorporate satellite‑derived sea‑level rise projections into long‑term development plans. The AP coverage highlights that cities like New York, Tokyo, and Jakarta are revising zoning laws and building codes to account for higher flood risks. Moreover, the data will inform the design of nature‑based solutions, such as restoring mangroves and wetlands, which can buffer storm surges and absorb excess water.</p> <h2>Scientific modeling and future projections</h2> <p>The scientific community will continue to refine the models that link ice loss to temperature increases. The AP article emphasizes that improved satellite data will feed into climate models, reducing uncertainties in future projections. This, in turn, will help policymakers and communities make better‑informed decisions about emissions pathways and adaptation strategies.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Satellites have traced a loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years—a stark reminder of the tangible, measurable impacts of climate change. The data underscore the need for decisive action on both mitigation and adaptation fronts and provide a concrete, data‑driven benchmark against which to gauge progress toward a more sustainable future.</p>
