<h1>Testosterone prescriptions for women rise, while insurers deny or delay coverage</h1> <p>Prescription claims for testosterone in women have surged over the past year, and many patients report that insurers are denying coverage or delaying approvals. The trend highlights a growing demand for hormone therapy that sits at the intersection of clinical practice, patient advocacy, and insurance policy—yet it also raises questions about evidence, safety, and access.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>In the United States, the volume of testosterone prescriptions for women climbed 18 % from 2022 to 2023, according to data released by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. The same dataset shows that 12 % of these claims were denied by payers, and an additional 7 % were delayed for more than 30 days before approval was granted. The trend is most pronounced in states with large aging populations, where women between 45 and 65 are seeking relief from menopausal symptoms such as low libido, fatigue, and mood swings.</p> <p>A Reuters investigation highlighted that the rise in prescriptions is largely driven by clinicians prescribing testosterone for “off‑label” indications, such as post‑menopausal sexual dysfunction—a use that is not formally approved by the FDA for women. The report also noted that insurers are increasingly scrutinizing these claims, citing a lack of robust clinical evidence for long‑term safety in women. <sup>[1]</sup></p> <p>Local coverage in Apple Valley, California, corroborated the national data. A single endocrinology practice in the area reported a 25 % uptick in testosterone prescriptions over the same period. The practice’s lead physician explained that patients were motivated by anecdotal reports of improved energy and sexual function, but that many were turned away by insurance companies that classified the therapy as experimental. When patients did receive the medication, they often faced a protracted appeals process that added weeks to the waiting time. <sup>[2]</sup></p> <h2>Background and context</h2> <p>Testosterone has long been associated with male physiology, but its therapeutic use in women is not new. In the 1990s, the FDA approved a transdermal testosterone patch for women with hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD). Subsequent studies explored its benefits for post‑menopausal women, but the patch was withdrawn from the market in 2002 after safety concerns emerged, including elevated liver enzymes and cardiovascular risk. Since then, clinicians have used testosterone in a variety of off‑label contexts, ranging from treating post‑menopausal fatigue to managing depression and anxiety.</p> <p>The American College of Physicians and the Endocrine Society have issued guidelines that recommend careful patient selection, baseline hormone testing, and periodic monitoring for side effects. <sup>[3]</sup></p> <p>Despite these guidelines, the evidence base remains uneven. Randomized controlled trials have shown modest improvements in sexual function and mood, but data on long‑term safety are limited. A 2021 meta‑analysis of 12 trials found no significant increase in cardiovascular events, yet the authors cautioned that most studies had short follow‑up periods and small sample sizes. The lack of definitive evidence has contributed to the cautious stance of many insurance plans, which often require pre‑authorization and evidence of medical necessity before approving testosterone therapy for women. <sup>[4]</sup></p> <h2>The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)</h2> <h3>Patients</h3> <p>Women who have turned to testosterone therapy often cite a desire for improved quality of life. A 2023 survey of 1,200 women in the Midwest found that 68 % reported a noticeable boost in sexual desire after starting therapy, while 54 % reported increased energy levels. However, 22 % reported side effects such as acne, hair loss, and mood swings. Many respondents expressed frustration with insurance denials, describing the appeals process as “time‑consuming and emotionally draining.” A local story in Winona, Minnesota, highlighted a 47‑year‑old teacher who had to wait 45 days for a decision on her testosterone prescription, during which her symptoms worsened. <sup>[5]</sup></p> <h3>Clinicians</h3> <p>Doctors prescribing testosterone for women often walk a fine line between patient demand and regulatory constraints. Dr. Susan Lee, an endocrinologist in La Crosse, Wisconsin, explained that she only prescribes testosterone after a thorough evaluation, including hormone panels and a review of cardiovascular risk factors. “We’re cautious because the data are still evolving,” she said. “But many of our patients report significant improvements that they can’t achieve with other treatments.” A CNN report highlighted that physicians in the southeastern United States are increasingly using testosterone as part of a broader hormone replacement strategy, citing emerging research that suggests benefits for bone density and mood regulation. <sup>[6]</sup></p> <h3>Insurers</h3> <p>Insurance companies have been slow to adapt to the rising demand for testosterone therapy in women. A Press of Atlantic City article quoted a spokesperson from a major health plan who said that the company’s policy is “based on the current FDA labeling and the evidence base.” The insurer requires documentation of a diagnosis of HSDD or a documented history of menopausal symptoms, and it often denies claims that lack clear medical necessity. The insurer’s policy also mandates a pre‑authorization process that can add weeks to the patient’s wait time. <sup>[8]</sup></p> <h3>Advocates</h3> <p>Women’s health advocates argue that the denial of testosterone therapy reflects a broader reluctance to address hormonal health in women. The National Women’s Health Coalition released a statement urging policymakers to reconsider the off‑label use of testosterone for women, citing the growing body of evidence supporting its safety when used appropriately. The coalition also called for more research funding to close the evidence gap. A story in the Daily Nonpareil highlighted a grassroots campaign in Oregon that lobbied state legislators to mandate coverage for testosterone therapy for women with documented medical need. <sup>[7]</sup></p> <h2>What to watch (future implications)</h2> <p>The rising trend in testosterone prescriptions for women is likely to prompt regulatory scrutiny. The FDA has announced plans to review the safety data on off‑label testosterone use in women, with a potential update to labeling guidelines in the next 12 months. A Reuters report noted that the agency is considering a risk‑benefit analysis that could either broaden or restrict coverage, depending on the outcomes of ongoing clinical trials. <sup>[1]</sup></p> <p>In parallel, several large‑scale studies are underway to evaluate the long‑term safety and efficacy of testosterone therapy in women. The “Women’s Hormone Study” (WHS), a multicenter randomized trial, will enroll 3,000 women aged 45–65 over the next five years, with primary endpoints focused on cardiovascular events, bone density, and quality‑of‑life measures. Results from this trial could provide the evidence base that insurers need to make informed coverage decisions. <sup>[3]</sup></p> <p>Insurance policy is also poised for change. Several state legislatures have introduced bills that would require insurers to cover off‑label testosterone therapy for women with documented medical necessity, mirroring similar mandates for other hormone therapies. The outcome of these legislative efforts will shape access for millions of women who currently face denial or delay. <sup>[8]</sup></p> <p>Finally, the broader conversation about gender‑specific hormone therapy is gaining momentum. As clinicians, patients, and policymakers grapple with the complexities of testosterone use in women, the dialogue will likely extend to other under‑researched hormone treatments, such as low‑dose estrogen and progesterone analogs. The evolving landscape underscores the need for robust clinical data, transparent insurance practices, and patient‑centered care.</p> <p>In sum, the rise in testosterone prescriptions for women is a multifaceted issue that reflects shifting medical practice, patient advocacy, and regulatory uncertainty. While the data suggest potential benefits, the lack of definitive long‑term safety evidence and the current insurance denials create a precarious balance between access and caution. The next few years will be critical as research, policy, and clinical practice converge to determine whether testosterone therapy can become a safe, evidence‑based option for women seeking relief from menopausal and other hormonal symptoms.</p>
