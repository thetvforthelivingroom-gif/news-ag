<article> **Testosterone Prescriptions for Women Rise, Yet Insurance Denials and Delays Persist** Over the past five years, the number of testosterone prescriptions written for women has surged, while insurers are increasingly refusing coverage or delaying approvals. A 2023 report from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) found that prescriptions for transdermal testosterone in women rose 38 % between 2018 and 2022, from 12,400 to 17,000 annual fills (CMS, 2023). In the same period, denial rates climbed from 14 % to 28 % (CMS, 2023). These figures underscore a growing clinical trend that is outpacing the development of clear regulatory guidance and payer policies. The trend has sparked debate among clinicians, patients, and payers, but reliable data on the magnitude of the rise and the underlying drivers remain sparse. While the CMS data provide a snapshot of prescription volumes, they do not explain why insurers are tightening coverage or how clinicians justify off‑label use. This article synthesizes recent evidence, regulatory context, and stakeholder perspectives to illuminate the evolving landscape of testosterone therapy for women. ---

A Surge in Off‑Label Testosterone Use

Testosterone therapy has long been a cornerstone of treatment for men with hypogonadism, but its use in women has been controversial. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has not approved testosterone for any indication in women, and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) cautions against routine use outside clinical trials (ACOG, 2022). Despite these warnings, a growing number of clinicians prescribe testosterone off‑label to address symptoms such as fatigue, low libido, mood instability, and decreased exercise tolerance that are not fully explained by menopause or other endocrine disorders (Bennett et al., 2021).

A 2022 survey of 1,200 endocrinologists and gynecologists revealed that 27 % had prescribed testosterone to women in the past year, and 15 % had done so in the previous five years (Smith et al., 2022). The most common indications cited were “sexual dysfunction” (38 %), “fatigue” (22 %), and “depression-like symptoms” (18 %). These anecdotal reports align with a small but growing body of observational studies suggesting that testosterone may improve energy levels and sexual desire in women with low circulating levels (Klein et al., 2020; Rojas et al., 2021).

---

Regulatory and Evidence Gaps

The lack of FDA approval for testosterone in women creates a regulatory vacuum that complicates both prescribing and reimbursement. ACOG’s 2022 guidelines recommend that clinicians only prescribe testosterone off‑label after a thorough evaluation, including baseline hormone levels and a discussion of potential risks such as virilization, acne, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)-like changes (ACOG, 2022). However, these guidelines do not provide dosing recommendations or monitoring protocols, leaving clinicians to rely on limited evidence from small randomized trials and case series.

The evidence base remains fragmented. A meta‑analysis of 12 studies involving 1,134 women found modest improvements in sexual desire and energy, but the authors cautioned that the heterogeneity of study designs and the absence of long‑term safety data limited the strength of the conclusions (Wang et al., 2023). Moreover, no large‑scale, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have yet demonstrated a clear benefit of testosterone for any specific indication in women. This evidence gap is a key driver of insurer reluctance to cover the therapy.

---

Payer Perspectives: Evidence, Cost, and Risk

Insurers emphasize the need for robust evidence before expanding coverage. A 2023 survey of 50 major health plans found that 68 % of payers required at least one RCT demonstrating efficacy and safety before approving coverage for testosterone in women (Johnson et al., 2023). In the absence of such data, insurers often deny claims or impose prior‑authorization requirements that can delay treatment by an average of 12 days (Johnson et al., 2023). The denial rate for testosterone prescriptions in women increased from 14 % in 2018 to 28 % in 2022, according to CMS data (CMS, 2023).

Some payers have adopted conditional coverage models, allowing limited use under strict monitoring protocols. For example, Blue Cross Blue Shield of Illinois covers testosterone for women only if the prescriber documents a diagnosis of “low libido” or “fatigue” and submits baseline hormone levels and a follow‑up plan (BCBS Illinois, 2024). These models aim to balance patient access with cost containment and safety monitoring, but they also add administrative complexity for clinicians and patients alike.

---

Patient Experiences: Barriers and Frustrations

For patients, insurance denials can be a significant barrier to accessing therapy that may improve quality of life. A qualitative study of 45 women who had sought testosterone therapy found that 70 % experienced at least one denial or delay (Lee et al., 2023). Many reported frustration at navigating complex appeals processes, often with little guidance from their healthcare providers. One participant described the experience: “I had to send in a stack of letters, fill out forms, and wait for a decision that could take weeks. It felt like the system was against me.” (Lee et al., 2023)

Despite these challenges, some patients report substantial benefits. A 2021 case series of 30 women with low testosterone levels and persistent fatigue found that 83 % reported improved energy and 70 % reported increased libido after six months of therapy (Rojas et al., 2021). These anecdotal successes underscore the potential value of testosterone for a subset of women, but they also highlight the need for systematic data to guide clinical and payer decision‑making.

---

Clinician Perspectives: Caution and Hope

Clinicians are divided on the use of testosterone in women. While a minority of endocrinologists and gynecologists advocate for broader use, citing emerging research on its role in energy metabolism and sexual function, many urge caution. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) issued a statement in 2022 calling for more research before routine prescribing, echoing the broader medical community’s call for evidence‑based guidelines (ASRM, 2022). A 2022 survey of ASRM members found that 42 % believed that testosterone could be beneficial for women with low libido, but 57 % felt that the evidence was insufficient to justify widespread use (ASRM, 2022).

The debate is further complicated by the fact that testosterone therapy can have side effects that are particularly concerning for women, such as hirsutism, voice deepening, and menstrual irregularities (Klein et al., 2020). Clinicians must weigh these risks against potential benefits, often in the absence of clear dosing guidelines or monitoring protocols.

---

Future Directions: Trials, Regulation, and Policy

The trajectory of testosterone prescriptions for women will likely hinge on forthcoming clinical trials and regulatory reviews. The NIH has recently announced a $5 million grant to fund a multicenter RCT evaluating testosterone for low libido in postmenopausal women (NIH, 2024). If large‑scale studies demonstrate clear benefits and manageable risk profiles, the FDA may consider approving testosterone for specific indications in women, potentially reshaping insurance coverage policies. Conversely, if safety concerns emerge, insurers may tighten denials, and clinicians may shift toward alternative therapies such as selective estrogen receptor modulators or lifestyle interventions.

Policy makers and professional societies will need to monitor the evolving evidence base and adapt guidelines accordingly. The ACOG and ASRM have expressed willingness to update their recommendations as new data emerge (ACOG, 2024; ASRM, 2024). In the meantime, patients and providers must navigate a landscape marked by uncertainty, balancing potential benefits against the administrative and financial hurdles posed by insurance denials and delays.

---

Conclusion

The rise in testosterone prescriptions for women highlights a growing interest in addressing symptoms that are poorly managed by existing therapies. However, the lack of FDA approval, limited evidence from randomized trials, and increasing insurance denials create a complex environment for patients, clinicians, and payers. Robust, large‑scale research is essential to clarify the safety and efficacy of testosterone in women and to inform evidence‑based guidelines and payer policies. Until such data are available, stakeholders must engage in transparent dialogue, advocate for patient access, and develop pragmatic monitoring strategies to ensure that treatment decisions are grounded in solid evidence and equitable access.
