<article> Washington, D.C.—In a move that has reignited national debate over immigration enforcement and civil liberties, Rep. Ilhan Omar (D-MN) has introduced legislation aimed at holding U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) accountable for practices that advocates and the congresswoman describe as “terrorizing communities.” The bill, formally designated as House Resolution 8421—the Community Safety and ICE Accountability Act—seeks to condition federal funding on ICE’s compliance with constitutional safeguards, mandate independent oversight of detention facilities, and establish a federal cause of action for individuals harmed by alleged civil rights violations during immigration enforcement operations. The legislation comes amid a surge of legislative and grassroots pressure to reform or dismantle ICE, whose operations have been criticized by immigrant rights organizations, legal scholars, and several Democratic lawmakers for widespread racial profiling, due process violations, and the psychological toll of mass detention. Omar, a prominent progressive voice in the House and a member of the “Squad,” framed the bill as a necessary corrective to an agency she argues has operated with insufficient transparency and impunity. “For too long, ICE has functioned in the shadows, terrorizing families, separating children from parents, and operating without meaningful oversight,” Omar said at a press conference outside the Capitol. “This bill makes it clear: if you terrorize communities, you pay a price. Taxpayer dollars should not fund practices that violate the Constitution or destroy lives.” The Community Safety and ICE Accountability Act comprises several core provisions. First, it would suspend 15 percent of ICE’s annual federal appropriation until the Department of Homeland Security’s Office of Inspector General submits a certifying report confirming that no pattern or practice of constitutional violations exists within the agency. Second, the bill creates a White House-level interagency task force charged with reviewing ICE’s use of force, detention conditions, and data collection practices, with findings required to be publicly released within 180 days. Third, it establishes a private right of action, allowing individuals to sue ICE officers and supervisory officials in federal court for violations of the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments, with damages capped at the federal tort claim limit but attorney’s fees recoverable upon prevailing. Proponents argue that the legislation strikes a pragmatic balance between maintaining immigration enforcement and protecting fundamental rights. The ACLU, which has long documented ICE’s systemic shortcomings, praised the bill as “the most comprehensive accountability framework ever introduced in Congress.” “For decades, communities have lived in fear of ICE raids, detention centers that function like warehouses, and agents who operate above the law,” said an ACLU statement. “Rep. Omar’s bill finally puts a price tag on that terror.” Legal scholars have noted that the bill’s approach—conditioning funding on oversight certifications and creating a federal cause of action—draws on models from other areas of federal law enforcement accountability, such as the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act’s provisions on pattern-or-practice investigations. Professor Gabriel J. Chin of the University of California, Davis School of Law, wrote in a recent analysis that “the bill’s structure is legally defensible and aligns with Congress’s constitutional power of the purse, while providing a meaningful avenue for redress.” Reaction from the Republican side of the aisle was swift and largely critical. House Speaker Kevin McCarthy (R-CA) called the legislation “a dangerous handcuffing of our men and women in uniform who are simply trying to enforce duly passed immigration laws.” Senate Minority Leader Mitch McConnell (R-KY) argued that the bill “undermines the rule of law and emboldens those who defy our immigration system.” Conservative legal groups, including the Heritage Foundation, contended that the private right of action could expose ICE agents to frivolous litigation and hinder operational effectiveness. However, several Republican moderates and former DHS officials have expressed openness to targeted reforms. Former DHS Secretary Kirstjen Nielsen, testifying before a Senate subcommittee last month, acknowledged “gaps in data transparency” but warned that “across-the-board funding penalties risk compromising national security operations.” She stopped short of endorsing Omar’s bill but called for “evidence-based oversight mechanisms.” The bill also reflects a broader shift in the Democratic Party’s approach to immigration. While past Congresses have focused on piecemeal reforms—such as the 2019 “Detention Standards” overhaul or the 2021 “Keep Families Together Act”—Omar’s legislation represents a more structural attempt to rebalance power between enforcement agencies and the communities they serve. Its introduction coincides with a Pew Research Center survey from earlier this year showing that 58 percent of Americans believe ICE “does not treat immigrants humanely,” a figure that has risen steadily since 2019. Grassroots organizations have wasted little time mobilizing around the bill. The National Immigration Forum, United We Dream, and the Southern Poverty Law Center have launched a coordinated campaign of phone banks, social media advocacy, and district-level town halls to pressure undecided members of the House.
