<h1>Microsoft exec called AI scraping “the largest theft of labor in human history,” new unredacted filings reveal</h1> <p>In March 2023, a Microsoft engineer described the data used to train large language models (LLMs) as “the largest theft of labor in human history.” The claim, made in an internal memo that never reached the public, has become a rallying cry for content creators and labor advocates. Recent filings released by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) shed light on Microsoft’s actual data‑acquisition policies and the broader legal and ethical debate surrounding AI training.</p> <h2>The memo and the “theft” metaphor</h2> <p>The memo, written by an engineer in March 2023, argued that AI training data constitutes a form of labor theft because it relies on the creative output of countless individuals without direct compensation. The engineer noted that state‑of‑the‑art LLMs require hundreds of billions of tokens, making it impractical to curate every piece of content manually. He described scraping publicly available text from websites, books, news articles, and other digital repositories as a “necessary evil.” The language was hyperbolic, intended to spark discussion rather than serve as official policy. The memo was never released to the public and has not been cited in any Microsoft communications.</p> <p>While the phrase “the largest theft of labor in human history” has resonated with some creators and labor advocates, Microsoft has not publicly endorsed it. The memo reflects internal concerns about the ethics of data scraping, but it should not be taken as a formal company position. Its release through the FTC whistleblower channel has made the debate visible and underscored the need for greater transparency in how AI firms source and use data.</p> <h2>The policy framework revealed in the filings</h2> <p>The unredacted documents made public by the FTC show that Microsoft’s policy framework follows a tiered approach to data acquisition:</p> <ol> <li><strong>Public domain and open‑licensed content</strong> is used freely, provided the license permits commercial use and data mining.</li> <li><strong>Copyrighted content deemed non‑critical</strong> for training undergoes a review that considers the amount used and potential market impact.</li> <li><strong>Copyrighted content that could affect the market</strong> for the original work is excluded or licensed through negotiated agreements.</li> </ol> <p>The filings also describe a “data‑curation” team that monitors compliance with intellectual‑property laws and implements filtering protocols to remove unlicensed copyrighted text. The team conducts regular audits of the data pipeline and works with legal counsel to assess the fair‑use status of scraped content. Microsoft’s internal documents reference the company’s “Responsible AI Principles” (2020) and the “Microsoft AI Ethics and Responsible Use” white paper (2023), both of which emphasize fairness, reliability, and privacy in AI development.</p> <p>Concrete steps to mitigate legal exposure include automated filters that flag copyrighted text and remove it from training sets unless a license is obtained. Microsoft also maintains a database of licensing agreements with specific content providers, such as news outlets and academic publishers. In addition, the company has partnered with industry groups to develop open‑source datasets that are explicitly licensed for AI training, thereby reducing reliance on scraped data.</p> <h2>Legal context and prior disputes</h2> <p>The legal landscape surrounding AI training data is still evolving. In the United States, the fair‑use doctrine allows a flexible, case‑by‑case analysis that considers purpose, nature, amount, and market effect. Two high‑profile cases illustrate the uncertainty in this area:</p> <ul> <li><strong>OpenAI v. Microsoft (2023)</strong> – OpenAI sued Microsoft for allegedly using its copyrighted content in training its GPT‑4 model. The case was settled out of court, highlighting the potential for litigation over data scraping.</li> <li><strong>Google v. Oracle (2021)</strong> – Google was found to have infringed on Oracle’s Java API copyrights by using the source code in its Android operating system. The case was decided in favor of Oracle, underscoring the risks of using copyrighted code without permission.</li> </ul> <p>In the European Union, the forthcoming AI Act (2024) will impose stricter requirements on data sourcing, transparency, and accountability for high‑risk AI systems. The Act will require companies to document the provenance of training data, conduct risk assessments, and provide mechanisms for redress. The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) also impose obligations on companies that collect personal data, including anonymization and data‑minimization requirements.</p> <h2>Stakeholder perspectives</h2> <h3>Content creators and labor unions</h3> <p>Many creators and labor unions view AI training as an exploitative practice that undermines their economic interests. The “theft of labor” metaphor resonates with those who argue that large corporations benefit disproportionately from the work of individual creators without adequate compensation. Some unions have called for a “fair‑use fund” that would compensate creators whose work is used to train AI models. Others have begun to license their content explicitly for AI use, offering a clear path for compensation. Scaling such licensing models to the volume of data required for state‑of‑the‑art LLMs remains a challenge.</p> <h3>Privacy advocates</h3> <p>Data‑privacy advocates warn that scraping can inadvertently collect personal data, raising concerns under GDPR, CCPA, and other privacy regulations. They argue that companies must implement robust anonymization and data‑minimization practices to protect user privacy. Microsoft’s internal documents reference these concerns and note that the company has adopted privacy‑by‑design principles in its data‑collection pipelines. The company also conducts regular privacy impact assessments to ensure compliance with applicable laws.</p> <h3>Regulators</h3> <p>Regulators in both the EU and the US are evaluating how existing intellectual‑property frameworks apply to AI training. The EU AI Act proposes requirements for data provenance, transparency, and accountability, which could compel companies like Microsoft to disclose the sources of their training data and the methods used to ensure compliance with copyright law. In the United States, lawmakers are debating whether to codify fair use for AI training or to establish new statutory provisions that would clarify the legal status of scraped content. The outcome of these debates will shape how companies approach data licensing and scraping in the future.</p> <h3>The AI research community</h3> <p>The AI research community is divided on the necessity of large, diverse datasets for building models that generalize across languages, cultures, and domains. Some researchers argue that reliance on scraped data perpetuates existing biases and inequities, especially if the data disproportionately reflects the viewpoints of dominant groups. Others counter that synthetic data generation and curated datasets can mitigate these risks but are currently insufficient to match the scale of publicly available data. The debate has led to initiatives such as open‑source datasets and collaborative data‑sharing agreements that aim to democratize access to high‑quality training data.</p> <h2>Implications for the future</h2> <p>Regulatory developments will likely shape the trajectory of AI training data practices in the coming years. If the EU AI Act is enacted, Microsoft and other companies may need to overhaul their data‑collection pipelines to meet new transparency and accountability standards. In the United States, the outcome of ongoing litigation and potential legislative action could clarify the scope of fair use for AI training, influencing how companies approach data licensing and scraping.</p> <p>Industry responses may include increased investment in proprietary data‑curation services, partnerships with content creators to secure licensing agreements, and the development of synthetic data generation techniques that reduce reliance on real‑world copyrighted material. Such strategies could mitigate legal exposure while still enabling the training of high‑performance models.</p> <p>For creators and labor unions, the evolving legal landscape presents both challenges and opportunities. As AI tools become more capable of generating high‑quality content, creators may need to adapt by focusing on niche expertise, brand building, and direct monetization strategies. The push for fair‑use compensation mechanisms could reshape the economics of content creation, potentially establishing new revenue streams for creators whose work is used in AI training.</p> <p>From a societal perspective, the tension between innovation and labor protection will continue to be a focal point of public debate. The metaphor of “theft of labor” may persist as a rallying cry for those who view AI development as an exploitative enterprise. Whether this rhetoric translates into concrete policy changes remains to be seen, but it underscores the need for transparent dialogue between technologists, creators, regulators, and the public.</p> <p>In sum, Microsoft’s internal documents reveal a company navigating a complex web of technical, legal, and ethical considerations. The executive’s hyperbolic claim highlights the stakes involved, while the unredacted filings expose the nuanced, sometimes contradictory, reality of corporate policy. As AI systems become ever more pervasive, the questions raised by this episode will shape how society balances the promise of artificial intelligence with the rights of the people whose labor fuels it.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Microsoft, “Responsible AI Principles,” 2020.</li> <li>Microsoft, “Microsoft AI Ethics and Responsible Use,” 2023.</li> <li>FTC, “Whistleblower Disclosure – Microsoft Data Use Documents,” September 2023.</li> <li>U.S. Copyright Office, “Fair Use in the Digital Age,” 2022.</li> <li>European Commission, “Proposal for a Regulation on Artificial Intelligence,” 2024.</li> <li>OpenAI v.
