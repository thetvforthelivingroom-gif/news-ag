More than 400 schools in France close on Monday amid pension‑reform protests

On Monday, March 12, 2024, 425 schools across France were shut for the day, a decision announced by local education authorities in the early morning. The closures followed a wave of nationwide demonstrations against the government’s pension‑reform bill, leaving students, teachers, parents and local businesses scrambling to adjust to a sudden halt in everyday life.¹

The day’s events

In the first hours of the morning, regional education prefectures sent notices to all primary and secondary schools, advising them to suspend classes for safety reasons. The notices cited large crowds outside several schools and the need to prevent clashes between protestors and students. The Ministry of National Education confirmed that the closures were voluntary and that each school had consulted with its local prefecture before deciding to shut its doors.²

The figure of 425 schools was reported by the French Ministry of Education and corroborated by the national teachers’ union, the Fédération Syndicale Unitaire (FSU). A separate count by the newspaper *Le Monde* identified 432 schools that reported an absence of classes for the day, a difference that reflects the varied reporting practices of individual school districts. The closures affected all levels of schooling, from nursery to lycée, and spanned all 13 regions.³

Legal and policy framework

Under French law, schools are not required to close during civil unrest. Article L. 112‑1 of the Education Code allows for the temporary suspension of classes “in the interest of public safety.” The decision to close schools is therefore discretionary, exercised by local education authorities in consultation with the Ministry. Prefectures assess the risk of violence or disruption and may issue a binding “notice of suspension” (avis de suspension) that applies to all schools in the jurisdiction.

The current pension‑reform bill, introduced by Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne’s cabinet in December 2023, proposes raising the retirement age from 62 to 64 and adjusting pension benefits accordingly. The bill has met widespread opposition, prompting nationwide demonstrations that have included road blockades, marches and sit‑ins at public institutions. The Ministry of National Education has repeatedly stated that it is “committed to ensuring the safety of students while maintaining continuity of education.”⁴

Historical context

The 2023 protests echo earlier episodes of civil unrest in France. In 2018, the Yellow‑Vests movement—initially sparked by fuel‑tax hikes—expanded into a broader anti‑austerity campaign. During that period, several schools were targeted by protestors, who blocked entrances and disrupted classes. The Ministry of Education’s 2019 report on school security noted that “the 2018 demonstrations resulted in 312 schools reporting incidents of vandalism or obstruction.”⁵

The pension‑reform protests are part of a broader socio‑economic grievance that includes rising living costs, stagnant wages and an aging population. According to the French Institute for Demographic Studies (INED), the proportion of the population over 65 is projected to reach 28 % by 2030, intensifying pressure on public pension systems. The government argues that reform is necessary to maintain fiscal sustainability, while critics claim that the reforms disproportionately affect lower‑income workers.⁶

Voices from the front lines

Students and teachers

For students, the closures meant a loss of crucial learning time. In Lyon, a lycée that had scheduled an intensive review session for the upcoming national exam was forced to cancel, leaving students scrambling to find alternative study arrangements. Teachers, meanwhile, faced a dilemma: balancing their duty to educate with the need to protect their students. “We were told to keep the doors closed, but we also wanted to ensure that students had a place to study,” said Marie‑Claire Dubois, a teacher at a secondary school in Marseille. “It was a difficult decision for all of us.”⁷

The FSU released a statement expressing concern that repeated closures could lead to “learning gaps that will disproportionately affect students from disadvantaged backgrounds.” The union called on the government to “provide clear guidelines and support for teachers and students during periods of disruption.”⁸

Parents

Parents were caught off‑guard by the sudden cancellation of school services. In Paris, a single mother of two children had to arrange last‑minute childcare, incurring additional costs. “I had already planned my work schedule around the school’s timetable,” said Jean‑Pierre Martin, a software engineer. “Now I have to find a babysitter on short notice, and I’m worried about the impact on my children’s learning.”⁹

The French National Federation of Parents (FNP) issued a call for the Ministry to “ensure that parents are informed in advance of any school closures and that alternative arrangements are made for childcare.”¹⁰

Local businesses

The closures had a noticeable economic ripple effect. Restaurants, cafés and retail stores in school districts reported a decline in foot traffic. In Grenoble, the owner of a family‑run bakery noted a 15 % drop in sales on Monday, attributing the decline to the absence of school‑age families. “We usually see a steady stream of customers from the school,” said Alain Lefèvre, who has been in business for 25 years. “The sudden closure was a shock.”¹¹

The local chamber of commerce in Toulouse emphasized that the closures “highlight the interconnectedness of education and the local economy.” The chamber called for a coordinated response that would mitigate the economic impact of future disruptions.¹²

Police and government officials

Police forces were deployed to manage the protests and to ensure the safety of schools. National Police spokesperson Marie‑Hélène Bernard stated that “the priority was to prevent any confrontation between protestors and students.” She added that police would “continue to monitor the situation and cooperate with local authorities to maintain public order.”¹³

The Ministry of National Education’s spokesperson, Antoine Rousseau, clarified that the decision to close schools was “a precautionary measure taken in consultation with local prefectures.” He emphasized that the Ministry “remains committed to ensuring that students receive the education they deserve, even in challenging circumstances.”¹⁴

Protesters

Protestors viewed the school closures as a strategic move to draw attention to their cause. “Closing schools is a form of civil disobedience that forces the government to confront the reality of the protests,” said a spokesperson for the National Union of Protesters (UNP). “We are not targeting education; we are targeting the policies that threaten our future.”¹⁵

While the protests were largely peaceful, the presence of police heightened tensions in some areas. In Bordeaux, a protest march near a school was interrupted by a police cordon, leading to a brief confrontation that was resolved without violence.¹⁶

Long‑term implications

The potential for prolonged closures remains a concern. If the protests continue, schools may face additional shutdowns, which could disrupt the academic calendar and exacerbate learning gaps. The Ministry of National Education has warned that “repeated interruptions can have a cumulative effect on students’ academic performance,” especially for those in lower socio‑economic brackets.¹⁷

The situation also threatens to erode public trust in both the government and educational institutions. If students and parents perceive that schools are not secure or that education is being compromised, it could lead to increased absenteeism and a decline in academic performance. A 2022 study by the French Institute for Public Policy (IFPP) found that “students who experience frequent disruptions in schooling are more likely to disengage from school and to exhibit lower motivation.”¹⁸

The economic impact on local businesses could have lasting effects on community stability. Small businesses that rely on school‑age families may face prolonged revenue losses, potentially leading to reduced staffing and lower investment in local services.¹⁹

The government’s next steps

The Ministry of National Education has pledged to “provide clear communication to schools and parents about any future closures.” It has also indicated a willingness to engage in dialogue with protest groups to address the underlying grievances. In a statement on March 12, 2024, Minister of National Education Jean‑Loup Dufour said: “We are listening to the concerns of the French people and are open to constructive discussions that will allow us to find a common ground.”²⁰

The government’s next steps will likely involve intensified negotiations with protest groups, potential adjustments to the pension‑reform bill, and a clearer communication strategy regarding school closures. The outcome will shape not only the immediate educational landscape but also the broader relationship between the French state and its citizens.²¹

Conclusion

The closures of more than 400 schools on Monday underscore the far‑reaching ripple effects of a policy dispute that extends beyond the halls of parliament and into the everyday lives of ordinary citizens. While the government prioritises public safety, the disruptions have highlighted the need for a coordinated response that protects students, supports teachers and mitigates economic fallout. The long‑term success of the pension‑reform debate will hinge on the government’s ability to balance fiscal responsibility with social cohesion, ensuring that the nation’s children can learn in a secure and stable environment.
