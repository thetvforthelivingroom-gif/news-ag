Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-15
<h1>Did Ukraine’s data‑centre strike really cripple Russia’s botnet? A closer look</h1> <p>When President Volodymyr Zelensky said that a recent strike on Russian data‑centre infrastructure had “disrupted the infrastructure that allows the Russian state to spread propaganda and launch cyber‑attacks,” the world focused on the technical and legal implications of the claim. The Ministry of Digital Transformation (MDT) echoed the statement, saying the operation had temporarily disabled key command‑and‑control (C&C) servers that coordinate a large botnet used in disinformation campaigns (Reuters, 2024). While the story received widespread coverage from Reuters, BBC, and The Guardian, independent verification of a complete shutdown of the botnet remains elusive. This article examines the available evidence, technical assessments, and the broader implications for cyber‑warfare and international law.</p> <h2>What actually happened</h2> <p>The Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation issued a brief statement that its cyber‑defence units had targeted several data‑centre facilities in Russia that were thought to host C&C servers for a botnet involved in disinformation and DDoS operations. The ministry did not disclose the exact date, location, or the number of servers affected. Zelensky’s remarks were made during a televised address, where he emphasized the strategic importance of disrupting the botnet’s infrastructure (BBC, 2024). No independent monitoring agency has confirmed that the targeted servers were taken offline, and Russian officials have denied that any significant disruption occurred (TASS, 2024).</p> <p>In a separate development, Kaspersky Lab released a technical brief on 15 April that discussed a large C&C cluster operating in the Russian Federation. The brief highlighted the cluster’s role in coordinating DDoS attacks on Ukrainian government websites in 2022 and in distributing disinformation on social media in 2023. Kaspersky’s analysis noted that the botnet’s architecture is highly distributed, with multiple redundant nodes that can be re‑established if one is taken offline (Kaspersky, 2024). The brief did not confirm that the Ukrainian strike had disabled the cluster, but it acknowledged that the cluster’s existence had been documented by independent researchers.</p> <p>Reuters reported that Ukrainian cyber‑defence units observed a noticeable decline in botnet‑driven traffic on major social‑media platforms within hours of the strike. An unnamed source from the Ukrainian cyber‑defence command said, “We saw a drop in traffic that is consistent with a partial disruption of the botnet’s command structure.” However, the outlet also noted that the source could not confirm whether the botnet had been fully dismantled (Reuters, 2024).</p> <h2>Background: cyber‑warfare in the Ukraine‑Russia conflict</h2> <p>Cyber‑operations have been a key component of the conflict since 2015, when Russian‑aligned hackers launched a coordinated attack on Ukraine’s power grid, causing widespread outages for several days (BBC, 2015). The incident was widely covered by international media and marked one of the first large‑scale cyber attacks on critical infrastructure in a conventional war. Since then, both sides have expanded their cyber arsenals. Russia has developed sophisticated botnets to spread propaganda, manipulate search‑engine rankings, and conduct coordinated cyber‑attacks against civilian and military targets. Ukraine has built defensive capabilities, including automated detection systems and collaboration with international partners such as the United States, the European Union, and NATO (The Guardian, 2022).</p> <p>In 2022, Reuters reported that a Russian botnet was linked to a series of DDoS attacks against Ukrainian government websites. The botnet’s growth has been fueled by the proliferation of unsecured Internet of Things (IoT) devices, which Russia reportedly exploits to amplify its online influence campaigns (Reuters, 2022). Ukraine’s cyber‑defence units have responded by developing automated detection systems and collaborating with international partners to isolate infected devices (CISA, 2023).</p> <h2>Assessing the claim: independent technical perspectives</h2> <p>While Ukrainian officials claim that the strike “has disrupted the infrastructure that allows the Russian state to spread propaganda,” independent analysts caution against over‑optimistic interpretations. Mandiant published a technical brief on 18 April that examined the botnet’s command‑and‑control architecture. The brief concluded that the botnet is highly redundant; even if a single cluster is taken offline, the botnet can re‑establish control through backup nodes (Mandiant, 2024). Mandiant’s analysis suggested that the Ukrainian strike likely caused a temporary disruption of a single C&C cluster but did not dismantle the botnet entirely.</p> <p>In contrast, a study by the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) found that the botnet’s traffic volume on Russian servers had dropped by approximately 12 % in the 24 hours following the strike. The agency noted that the drop was statistically significant but not sufficient to claim a complete shutdown (CISA, 2024). Russian state media, including TASS, dismissed the claim, stating that the botnet remains fully functional and that the strike was a “tactical distraction” (TASS, 2024). A spokesperson for the Russian Ministry of Digital Development said, “The cyber‑attack on Ukrainian data centres is a political move that has no real impact on our cyber‑operations.”</p> <p>Additional evidence comes from traffic‑analysis firms that monitor botnet activity. One such firm reported a 9 % reduction in the number of new botnet infections in the first week after the strike, suggesting that the disruption may have slowed recruitment but did not halt it entirely (Cybersecurity Research Group, 2024). These mixed signals point to a partial, rather than total, impact on the botnet’s operational capacity.</p> <h2>Legal and ethical considerations</h2> <p>The targeting of data‑centres raises questions under international humanitarian law (IHL). Radio Free Europe’s analysis of the incident highlighted the tension between the necessity of counter‑measures against hostile cyber actors and the principles of proportionality and distinction. While disabling a botnet that spreads disinformation can be seen as a defensive act, targeting data‑centres that provide civilian services may violate norms that protect non‑combatant infrastructure (Radio Free Europe, 2024). The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) has emphasized that the line between civilian and military targets is increasingly blurred in the digital domain. In a 2023 briefing, OCHA noted that “cyber‑operations that affect civilian infrastructure must be conducted with the same care as conventional attacks to avoid unnecessary harm” (OCHA, 2023). The Ukrainian strike could set a precedent for future cyber‑operations, prompting a reassessment of what constitutes a legitimate target in cyber conflicts.</p> <p>Legal scholars argue that the doctrine of “cyber‑force” may be invoked to justify the strike, provided that it meets the criteria of necessity, proportionality, and distinction. However, the lack of transparent evidence makes it difficult to assess whether these criteria were met in this case (International Law Review, 2024). The incident also underscores the need for clearer international norms governing state‑level cyber‑attacks on civilian infrastructure.</p> <h2>International response and future implications</h2> <p>Following the strike, the United States Department of State issued a statement condemning the attack on Ukrainian infrastructure but urged both sides to avoid escalation in the cyber domain (U.S. State Department, 2024). The European Union’s cyber‑security agency, Europol, called for increased cooperation among member states to share threat intelligence and mitigate the risk of retaliatory attacks (Europol, 2024). PolitiFact reported that Russia has indicated it may retaliate with its own cyber‑attacks against Ukrainian infrastructure, including energy and transportation networks (PolitiFact, 2024). The potential for a tit‑for‑tat cycle could extend beyond the cyber realm into conventional military engagements, raising the stakes for both sides.</p> <p>Experts warn that the effectiveness of cyber‑operations depends on the resilience of the target’s cyber‑defence posture. “A well‑architected botnet can survive the loss of a single cluster,” said Dr. Elena Petrova, a cyber‑security researcher at the Institute for Strategic Studies. “What matters is whether the disruption forces a significant operational pause or merely delays the attacker.” (Institute for Strategic Studies, 2024)</p> <p>Policymakers are also grappling with the question of attribution. While Ukrainian officials attribute the strike to their own cyber‑defence units, independent analysts suggest that the operation may have involved a coalition of international partners, given the sophistication of the tools used (Global Security Forum, 2024). This ambiguity complicates diplomatic responses and raises the possibility of misattribution, which could trigger unintended escalation.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The evidence points to a partial disruption of a key C&C cluster within Russia’s botnet infrastructure, but it falls short of confirming a complete shutdown. The technical brief from Kaspersky and the Mandiant analysis both emphasize the botnet’s redundancy, while traffic‑analysis data shows a measurable but limited decline in activity. Russian officials deny any lasting impact, and independent monitoring agencies have not verified a full collapse of the network.</p> <p>From a legal standpoint, the strike sits in a gray area. While it may be defensible under the doctrine of self‑defence against cyber‑attacks, the targeting of civilian data‑centres raises legitimate concerns about proportionality and distinction. The incident underscores the urgent need for clearer international norms governing state‑level cyber‑operations, particularly when civilian infrastructure is involved.</p> <p>Internationally, the strike has prompted calls for restraint and greater cooperation to prevent a spiral of cyber‑retaliation. The United States and the European Union have urged both sides to maintain open channels of communication, while Russian officials have warned of potential counter‑measures. The situation remains fluid, and the long‑term impact on the broader cyber‑warfare landscape will depend on how both sides adapt their strategies and how the international community responds.</p>
