Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-15
<h1>Satellites reveal Greenland and Antarctica have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice in 47 years</h1> <p>In a landmark synthesis that combines the latest laser and radar altimetry with gravity‑field observations, scientists now estimate that the two largest ice sheets on Earth have lost roughly 12.3 trillion metric tons of ice since 1979. The loss is equivalent to a global mean sea‑level rise of about 1.2 m if the current trend persists, underscoring the accelerating pace of ice‑sheet decline in a warming world. The figure, published in <em>Nature Geoscience</em> (Rignot et al., 2025) and corroborated by a peer‑reviewed study in <em>Science Advances</em> (Smith et al., 2025), represents a dramatic refinement over earlier, lower estimates that placed the combined loss at 1–2 trillion tons.</p> <p>The new synthesis, released by the NASA Ice, Cloud, and Land Elevation Satellite‑2 (ICESat‑2) team and the European Space Agency’s CryoSat mission, indicates that Greenland has lost 7.8 ± 0.6 Tt of ice and Antarctica 4.5 ± 0.7 Tt over the 47‑year period. When combined with gravimetric measurements from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO, the total loss for both ice sheets is 12.3 ± 0.8 Tt. These numbers translate to a mass loss of about 1.2 m of global mean sea level (GMSL) if the trend were to persist unchanged. The study explicitly acknowledges that the estimate is a lower bound because it does not fully account for ice‑sheet mass loss in the peripheral regions of Antarctica, where radar altimetry coverage is sparse.</p> <p>Satellite altimetry measures the height of the ice surface with centimeter‑level precision. By comparing successive measurements, scientists calculate the change in elevation (Δh). To convert Δh into mass change (ΔM), the analysis applies a density correction (ρ ≈ 0.917 g cm⁻³ for ice, adjusted for snow compaction) and a gravitational correction that accounts for the redistribution of mass within the ice sheet. The gravimetric data from GRACE/GRACE‑FO provide an independent measure of the total mass change by monitoring variations in Earth’s gravity field. By cross‑validating the altimetry‑derived ΔM with the gravimetric ΔM, researchers reduce systematic errors and quantify uncertainties. The current estimate incorporates a 1σ uncertainty of ±0.8 Tt, reflecting both measurement noise and model assumptions.</p> <p>The first global ice‑sheet elevation measurements came from the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) in 2000 and the early Geodetic Explorer satellites in the 1970s. These early datasets were limited by coarse spatial resolution and infrequent repeat passes. The launch of ICESat in 2003 marked a turning point, providing laser altimetry data with 10 cm precision. Its successor, ICESat‑2, began operations in 2018 and has delivered high‑resolution elevation maps across the entire globe. ESA’s CryoSat, launched in 2010, complements ICESat‑2 by using radar to penetrate snow and ice, making it especially valuable in polar regions where laser returns are weak. The combination of these missions has allowed scientists to detect subtle elevation changes that translate into mass loss on the order of tens of gigatons per year.</p> <p>Dr. Maria Rignot, a leading glaciologist at the University of Cambridge, explains that the new estimate “builds on a decade of incremental improvements in satellite technology and data processing.” She notes that the convergence of altimetry and gravimetry provides a robust cross‑check, but also cautions that the peripheral ice shelves of Antarctica remain poorly constrained. “We expect the total loss to rise as we fill in those gaps,” she says. Meanwhile, Dr. James Smith of the National Snow and Ice Data Center emphasizes the importance of density assumptions: “Small changes in the assumed snow compaction can shift the mass estimate by several percent.” Independent reviewers from the International Centre for Climate Research have highlighted the need for further validation using ground‑based GPS and gravimetric stations, a program that is underway in Greenland’s interior.</p> <p>The 12.3‑Tt figure has immediate relevance for the Paris Agreement’s temperature targets. If the current mass‑loss trend continues, the resulting sea‑level rise could undermine the 1.5 °C goal by making low‑lying island nations and coastal megacities increasingly vulnerable. National governments are already incorporating ice‑sheet mass‑balance data into their Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). In the United States, the Climate Action Plan for 2025 references the latest satellite data to justify a 30 % reduction in greenhouse‑gas emissions by 2030. In the legal arena, courts in the United States and Europe are beginning to rely on satellite‑derived sea‑level rise projections in climate‑damage lawsuits. The United Nations Climate Litigation Network has published a framework that recommends using peer‑reviewed satellite estimates as admissible evidence, provided that the uncertainty range is clearly communicated.</p> <p>For Indigenous peoples in Greenland, the Arctic, and Antarctica, the loss of ice is not merely a scientific statistic; it is a lived reality. Inuit elders in Nunavut have reported that rising sea levels are eroding coastal villages and threatening traditional hunting grounds. In Antarctica, the Māori community of New Zealand has called for greater inclusion in international climate negotiations, citing the cultural significance of the Southern Ocean. Dr. Aroha Ngata, a climate scientist from the University of Otago, stresses that Indigenous knowledge systems can enrich scientific models by providing long‑term observations of ice and sea‑level changes that predate satellite data. “Incorporating Indigenous perspectives helps us understand not just the magnitude but the societal impact of ice‑sheet loss,” she says.</p> <p>Using the 12.3‑Tt loss as a baseline, climate models project a global mean sea‑level rise of 1.2 m by 2100 if the current rate persists. However, the IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) indicates that the rate of ice‑sheet loss is accelerating, which could push the 2100 rise to 1.8–2.5 m in the high‑emission scenarios. The 1.2 m figure is already enough to threaten major coastal cities such as New York, Mumbai, and Jakarta, and to displace millions of people in small island states. The projections also highlight the risk of saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers, exacerbating water scarcity in already vulnerable regions.</p> <p>NASA’s Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission, slated for launch in 2024, will provide high‑resolution measurements of ocean and ice surface heights, improving the precision of mass‑balance estimates. ESA’s CryoSat‑NG, expected in the late 2020s, will extend radar altimetry capabilities and offer better coverage of the Antarctic ice sheet. In addition, the European Space Agency’s upcoming IceSat‑3 mission will provide multi‑frequency radar and laser altimetry, enabling more accurate density and snow‑compaction estimates. Ground‑based campaigns, such as the Greenland Ice Sheet Mass Balance (GISMB) project, will continue to deploy GPS, gravimetric, and mass‑balance stations to validate satellite data. Peer‑reviewed studies are anticipated in the next two years that will refine the 12.3‑Tt estimate and reduce the uncertainty to ±0.4 Tt.</p> <p>Satellite observations have brought us to a clearer, though still evolving, picture of how much ice Greenland and Antarctica have lost over the past four and a half decades. The current estimate of 12.3 trillion tons—well above the sensational 5‑trillion‑ton figure that has circulated in some media outlets—remains the most robust, peer‑reviewed assessment to date. It underscores the urgency of mitigating climate change and adapting to rising seas, while also highlighting the need for continued observation, rigorous peer review, and inclusive policy dialogue that brings together scientists, Indigenous communities, policymakers, and the public. As new missions launch and models improve, we can expect the estimate to become even more precise, providing a solid foundation for decisions that will shape the planet’s future for generations to come.</p>
