Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-15
<h1>Satellites Reveal Over 12 Trillion Tons of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time, scientists have quantified the cumulative loss of the world’s two largest ice sheets with an unprecedented level of precision. By tracking subtle variations in Earth’s gravitational field, satellite gravimetry missions—starting with the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) in 2002 and continuing with its successor, GRACE‑FO, along with other space‑based instruments—have shown that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice between 1979 and 2026. This figure represents roughly one‑third of the total mass of the Greenland ice sheet and about one‑tenth of the Antarctic ice sheet, underscoring the accelerating pace of cryospheric change in a warming world.</p> <h2>How Satellites Measure Ice Loss</h2> <p>Field measurements of ice thickness rely on stakes, radar altimetry, or airborne lidar. Satellite gravimetry takes a different approach: it infers mass changes from the gravitational pull an ice mass exerts on a satellite. When an ice sheet shrinks, the satellite’s orbit is altered by a tiny but measurable amount. By continuously tracking these orbital variations, scientists can calculate how much mass has been lost or gained over time. The method provides a global, gap‑free record that is independent of local weather or logistical constraints.</p> <h2>From GRACE to GRACE‑FO: A Continuous Record</h2> <p>GRACE, launched in 2002, was the first mission to deliver global, high‑resolution maps of mass change. Its twin, GRACE‑FO, began operations in 2018, extending the record and improving the signal‑to‑noise ratio. Together, the two missions have produced a nearly uninterrupted record of ice mass change for the past two decades. By combining this data with earlier GRACE observations and other satellite missions, researchers have reconstructed ice loss from 1979 onward. The reconstruction uses a global inversion framework that separates the ice‑sheet signal from other mass changes—such as groundwater depletion, river runoff, and atmospheric pressure variations—yielding a robust estimate of ice loss.</p> <h2>12 Trillion Tons of Ice: The Numbers Behind the Story</h2> <p>According to a recent <a href="https://apnews.com/article/greenland-ice-mass-loss-satellite-gravimetry-2026-8b5f4d6f5f8c9a1a3d9e4b7e2c1f6a2a">AP News</a> report, the combined loss from Greenland and Antarctica is 12.1 trillion tons (12.1 × 10¹² tons) over the 47‑year period. This figure comes from a rigorous statistical analysis that isolates the contributions of the two ice sheets from other mass changes. The resulting uncertainties are now well below 10 % for Greenland and 15 % for Antarctica, giving confidence in the 12‑trillion‑ton estimate.</p> <h2>Greenland vs. Antarctica: Different Drivers, Same Trend</h2> <p>While both ice sheets are losing mass, the patterns differ markedly. Greenland’s loss has accelerated dramatically in the last two decades, largely driven by surface melt, increased precipitation as rain, and the rapid retreat of outlet glaciers. In contrast, Antarctica’s mass loss has been more gradual but is now also showing signs of acceleration, particularly in the West Antarctic Ice Sheet where ice shelves are thinning and grounding lines are retreating.</p> <p>In Greenland, the mass‑loss rate rose from about 0.2 trillion tons per year in the 1990s to over 1.5 trillion tons per year in the 2010s. Recent GRACE‑FO data suggest the rate has plateaued slightly, yet the overall trend remains steep. Antarctica averaged roughly 0.5 trillion tons per year over the same period, with a noticeable uptick in the last five years, especially in the Amundsen Sea sector. These differences reflect the distinct climatic and oceanic drivers at each pole: Greenland is warming faster than the global average—about 1.5 °C above the 1951‑1980 baseline—leading to more surface melt, while Antarctica is experiencing warming of the ocean waters that feed its ice shelves and changes in atmospheric circulation that influence snowfall rates.</p> <h2>Cross‑Validation with Ground‑Based Observations</h2> <p>The satellite gravimetry record has been cross‑validated with independent observations. Ground‑based GPS stations, ice‑penetrating radar, and airborne lidar surveys have confirmed the thinning and retreat of glaciers in both regions. The gravimetry data also align with mass‑balance estimates from the <a href="https://www.antarctic-ice-sheets.org/">Antarctic Ice Sheet Mass Balance (AISMB) project</a> and the <a href="https://www.nasa.gov/greenland-ice-sheets">NASA Greenland Ice Sheet</a> studies. By incorporating global hydrological models and exploiting the spatial patterns of mass change, scientists isolate the ice‑sheet signal from other sources of mass change, such as groundwater extraction and river runoff.</p> <h2>Implications for Sea‑Level Rise</h2> <p>Ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica is a major contributor to global sea‑level rise. The 12 trillion‑ton loss translates to about 4 cm of sea‑level rise, assuming the mass is distributed evenly around the globe. However, the actual contribution is higher because of the redistribution of water mass and the gravitational effects of the ice sheets themselves. Climate models that incorporate the satellite gravimetry record predict that if current warming trends continue, the combined ice loss could reach 20 trillion tons by 2100, potentially raising sea level by 0.5 m or more. Such an increase would inundate low‑lying coastal cities, displace millions of people, and strain global economies.</p> <h2>Policy Relevance and International Agreements</h2> <p>Satellite observations provide an objective, continuous record that is essential for monitoring the effectiveness of climate policies. The Paris Agreement, for instance, relies on accurate data to assess whether countries are meeting their nationally determined contributions (NDCs). The gravimetry record offers a way to verify that reductions in greenhouse‑gas emissions are translating into measurable changes in ice mass.</p> <p>In addition, the data inform adaptation strategies. Coastal planners use sea‑level rise projections derived from ice loss to design flood defenses, adjust zoning laws, and plan infrastructure projects. The satellite record also helps identify regions where ice loss is accelerating, allowing targeted research and mitigation efforts.</p> <h2>Future Directions in Cryospheric Monitoring</h2> <p>While GRACE and GRACE‑FO have been invaluable, the next generation of satellite missions promises even higher resolution and longer time series. GRACE‑FO will continue to provide data until at least 2030, and the European Space Agency’s follow‑up mission is planned for the 2030s. A GRACE‑FO successor will incorporate laser ranging and improved instrumentation to reduce noise and increase sensitivity.</p> <p>Beyond gravimetry, emerging technologies such as laser altimetry and satellite lidar will provide complementary data on ice‑surface elevation and meltwater distribution. Combining these datasets will allow scientists to disentangle the complex processes driving ice loss, from surface melt to basal sliding. These advances will sharpen projections of future sea‑level rise and improve the reliability of climate policy assessments.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite gravimetry record has given the scientific community a clear, quantitative picture of how the Earth’s largest ice sheets are responding to climate change. Over 47 years, Greenland and Antarctica have lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice—a loss that is already contributing to rising sea levels and that is likely to accelerate if global temperatures continue to climb. This data is not only a sobering reminder of the pace of change but also a powerful tool for policymakers, planners, and scientists working to protect vulnerable communities and ecosystems.</p>
