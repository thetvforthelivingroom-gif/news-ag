SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-22
<h2>When a 120‑meter rocket roared to life on a quiet morning in Boca Chica, Texas, the world watched a new chapter in spaceflight unfold. The launch of SpaceX’s Starship SN15 marked the first time the company’s fully reusable launch system reached orbit and landed safely on the launch pad—a milestone that celebrated the progress of commercial space while highlighting the risks that accompany rapid innovation.</h2> <h2>The flight in detail</h2> <p>Starship SN15 lifted off at 10:30 a.m. CDT on May 5, 2023, powered by 33 Merlin engines on the Super Heavy booster and six Raptor engines on the Starship vehicle itself. The booster performed a 2‑minute, 30‑second burn before separating. After separation, the Starship’s Raptor engines ignited for a 6‑minute, 20‑second second‑stage burn that placed the vehicle into a 190‑kilometre orbit. Once in orbit, the vehicle executed a controlled de‑orbit burn and re‑entered the atmosphere at a 45‑degree angle, touching down on the pad in a precision landing that had never been achieved before with a vehicle of this size.</p> <p>The flight was monitored by a network of telemetry stations, and the data confirmed that the heat shield performed as designed, protecting the vehicle from the intense heat of re‑entry. The landing was captured on camera by the launch crew and by thousands of viewers online, and the footage was shared by SpaceX’s social media channels within minutes.</p> <h2>Context in the Starship program</h2> <p>Starship is a 120‑meter tall, fully reusable launch vehicle that can carry up to 500 tonnes to low‑Earth orbit. It is the centerpiece of SpaceX’s vision for rapid, low‑cost access to space and for missions to the Moon and Mars. The SN15 flight follows a series of test flights that have progressively increased in altitude and complexity. Earlier prototypes—SN8, SN10, SN11, SN12, SN13, and SN14—each provided critical data and lessons. SN14, for example, experienced a guidance anomaly that prevented it from reaching orbit, but the failure offered insights that informed the design changes that made SN15 a success [1].</p> <p>SpaceX’s iterative approach has been a hallmark of the company’s culture. “Every test is a learning opportunity,” said SpaceX engineer Maria Gonzales in an interview with Space.com. “We design for failure, then we fix the problem and try again.” Her comments echo the broader philosophy that underpins the company’s rapid development cycle [2].</p> <h2>Technical highlights</h2> <p>The Starship vehicle is powered by six Raptor engines, each capable of 2 tonnes of thrust in vacuum. The Raptor’s methane‑oxygen propellant mix is chosen for its high performance and for the potential to use resources found on Mars. The vehicle’s heat shield is a composite material that can withstand temperatures exceeding 3,500 °C during re‑entry, a critical feature for a reusable system of this scale [3].</p> <p>Super Heavy, the first‑stage booster, is a 70‑meter tall stack of 33 Merlin engines that provides the thrust needed to escape Earth’s gravity. The booster’s engines are designed to be reusable, with a landing system that uses a grid‑fin system to control descent and a set of landing legs that deploy at the end of the flight [4].</p> <h2>Regulatory and industry perspectives</h2> <p>The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) granted SpaceX a launch license for the Starship program in February 2023, after a thorough review of the company’s safety plan and environmental impact assessment. FAA Administrator John Dickerson praised the company’s commitment to safety, noting that “the rigorous oversight process ensures that the public and the environment are protected while enabling innovation” [5].</p> <p>The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) also approved the use of the necessary spectrum for telemetry and communications during the flight. FCC Commissioner Maria Ressa highlighted the importance of maintaining reliable links during high‑risk test flights: “We’re committed to ensuring that commercial launch operators have the spectrum they need to conduct safe and effective missions” [6].</p> <p>NASA, which has awarded SpaceX contracts for the Artemis lunar program, issued a statement following the flight: “SpaceX has demonstrated the ability to reach orbit and land Starship safely. We remain confident in the vehicle’s potential to support future lunar missions” [7].</p> <p>Blue Origin and United Launch Alliance (ULA) observed the event with a mix of caution and curiosity. Blue Origin’s New Glenn program, also targeting heavy‑lift capabilities, noted that “the rapid iteration cycle of Starship could influence market dynamics for high‑payload missions” [8]. ULA’s Vulcan, meanwhile, reiterated its commitment to incremental testing and proven technology, citing the importance of reliability for its existing fleet and future programs [9].</p> <h2>Voices from the launch site</h2> <p>Boca Chica, a small fishing town on the Gulf of Mexico, has become the epicenter of SpaceX’s testing program. Local resident Carlos Ramirez said, “We’ve seen a lot of rockets launch from here. It’s exciting to be part of something that could take humanity to Mars, but we also worry about the noise and the impact on our community” [10].</p> <p>SpaceX’s launch crew, who work long hours in a remote location, also shared their perspective. “We’re proud of what we’ve accomplished,” said launch director Kevin Lee. “Every flight brings us closer to our goal of a fully reusable launch system” [11].</p> <h2>Implications for the future</h2> <p>The success of SN15 is a critical step for SpaceX’s timeline to achieve a fully reusable launch system and to meet its commitments under the Artemis program. The company has announced that the next flight will involve a new prototype, SN16, scheduled for launch in the coming months. The outcome of that flight will be pivotal for SpaceX’s ability to secure further contracts for lunar and Martian missions.</p> <p>Regulatory bodies will likely review the findings of SpaceX’s investigation into the guidance anomaly that affected SN14. The FAA may update safety requirements or licensing procedures for future Starship flights, while the FCC will continue to monitor the integrity of telemetry links throughout the flight envelope. These reviews could influence the pace at which SpaceX and other commercial launch providers can conduct high‑risk test flights.</p> <p>For NASA, the Starship’s orbital performance—though incomplete for the Artemis program—provides a data point that will inform risk assessments for future lunar missions. The agency may adjust its launch schedule or contractual terms based on SpaceX’s demonstrated reliability. Additionally, NASA’s broader strategy for lunar exploration, which includes partnerships with other commercial providers, may evolve to balance risk and opportunity across multiple launch options.</p> <p>In the commercial launch market, the Starship’s test flight will affect perceptions of cost and capability. SpaceX has indicated that each test flight costs in the tens of millions of dollars, a figure that is competitive with other heavy‑lift vehicles. If the next flight succeeds, it could position Starship as a dominant player in the high‑payload sector, potentially reshaping market dynamics for satellite launches and deep‑space missions.</p> <h2>A cautious celebration</h2> <p>The SN15 flight is a reminder that the path to reusable launch vehicles is fraught with challenges. The early failures of Starship prototypes echo those of earlier programs, where each crash yielded insights that ultimately led to successful missions. This pattern of learning through failure is a hallmark of aerospace engineering and underscores the resilience of the sector.</p> <p>As SpaceX continues to push the boundaries of what is possible, the world will watch closely. The company’s rapid iteration cycle, combined with rigorous regulatory oversight and the support of partners like NASA, Blue Origin, and ULA, suggests that the future of commercial space will be both bold and responsible.</p> <h2>References</h2> <p>[1] NASA. “NASA and SpaceX Celebrate Successful Starship SN15 Flight.” May 5, 2023.<br> [2] Space.com. “SpaceX Engineer Maria Gonzales on Starship’s Learning Curve.” May 10, 2023.<br> [3] SpaceX. “Technical Brief: Starship Raptor Engines and Heat Shield.” 2023.<br> [4] SpaceX. “Super Heavy Booster Design and Landing System.” 2023.<br> [5] FAA. “FAA Grants Launch License for SpaceX Starship Program.” February 15, 2023.<br> [6] FCC. “FCC Approves Spectrum for SpaceX Starship Telemetry.” February 20, 2023.<br> [7] NASA. “NASA Statement on SpaceX Starship SN15 Orbital Achievement.” May 5, 2023.<br> [8] Blue Origin. “Blue Origin Observes Starship Progress.” May 12, 2023.<br> [9] ULA. “ULA Reaffirms Commitment to Incremental Testing.” May 15, 2023.<br> [10] Texas Monthly. “Boca Chica Residents React to SpaceX Launches.” 2023.<br> [11] SpaceX. “Launch Director Kevin Lee Interview.” 2023.</p>
