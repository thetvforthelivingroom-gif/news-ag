Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-22
<h1>Ice Sheets Have Lost 6.8 Trillion Tonnes of Ice Since the Early 1990s, Accelerating Sea‑Level Rise</h1> <p>Satellite observations now show that Greenland and Antarctica have shed a combined 6.8 ± 0.6 trillion tonnes of ice since the early 1990s. That loss already contributes roughly 0.8 mm of global sea‑level rise each year and is accelerating the planet’s warming trend. While some media outlets previously cited a 12‑trillion‑ton figure, that estimate was based on older, less precise data and has since been superseded by the robust 6.8‑trillion‑ton figure derived from the GRACE and CryoSat‑2 missions.¹</p> <h2>What Has Happened?</h2> <p>Between 1992 and 2021, the mass of the two largest ice sheets dropped by 6.8 ± 0.6 trillion tonnes. Greenland contributed 5.4 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes, while Antarctica shed 1.4 ± 0.2 trillion tonnes. The loss is not uniform: Greenland’s mass loss accelerated from 0.3 trillion tonnes per year in the 1990s to 0.8 trillion tonnes per year in the 2010s, driven by increased surface melt and faster ice‑sheet flow. In Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet has been the most vulnerable, with a three‑fold increase in mass loss over the past decade, while the East Antarctic Ice Sheet remains largely stable but with growing uncertainties in the interior.²</p> <p>These numbers translate into a global sea‑level rise of about 0.8 mm per year from ice‑sheet mass loss alone, a contribution that is roughly 40 % of the total observed rise of 3.3 mm per year since 1993.³ The acceleration of ice‑sheet loss is now a critical driver of future sea‑level projections, underscoring the urgency of both mitigation and adaptation strategies.⁴</p> <h2>How Do We Measure It?</h2> <p>Satellite gravimetry is the gold standard for measuring ice‑sheet mass. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE), launched in 2002, and its successor GRACE‑FO, launched in 2018, map subtle changes in Earth’s gravitational field caused by the redistribution of mass. By integrating these anomalies over the ice‑sheet area, scientists derive the total mass change with an uncertainty of about 5 % for Greenland and 10 % for Antarctica.⁵</p> <p>Complementary data come from radar altimetry missions such as ESA’s CryoSat‑2, which measures surface elevation changes, and NASA’s ICESat‑2, which provides high‑resolution topography. Combining elevation changes with density estimates (derived from ice‑core data and glaciological models) yields volume loss, which can then be converted to mass. This multi‑sensor approach reduces systematic biases and allows cross‑validation of results.⁶</p> <p>Uncertainties arise from several sources: density variations within the ice, unmodeled glacial isostatic adjustment, and the limited spatial resolution of gravimetry over the vast Antarctic interior. Recent studies have refined error bars by incorporating in‑situ GPS measurements and improved ice‑sheet models, leading to the current consensus estimates.⁷</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>The first satellite observations of ice sheets began with ESA’s ERS‑1 in 1991, which used radar altimetry to map surface elevation. These early data revealed a clear thinning trend in Greenland, prompting the launch of NASA’s ICESat in 2003 and ESA’s CryoSat‑2 in 2010. The GRACE mission, launched in 2002, was a watershed moment because it provided the first global, continuous record of ice‑sheet mass change. Over the past three decades, the combination of these missions has transformed our understanding of the cryosphere from a static archive to a dynamic system that responds rapidly to climate forcing.⁸</p> <p>Pre‑satellite estimates of ice‑sheet mass loss date back to the 1970s, when ground‑based mass balance studies and early satellite altimetry from the Nimbus‑7 and SMMR missions suggested a modest decline of 0.2–0.3 trillion tonnes per decade in Greenland. By the early 1990s, the rate had increased to 0.5 trillion tonnes per decade, a trend that satellite gravimetry has now confirmed with higher precision.⁹</p> <p>Peer‑reviewed studies published in <em>Nature</em>, <em>Science</em>, and the <em>Journal of Glaciology</em> have consistently reported a net loss of ice from both Greenland and Antarctica since the early 1990s. The IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) synthesizes these findings, concluding that the ice sheets are in a state of net mass loss and that this trend is likely to continue unless global temperatures are limited to 1.5 °C.¹⁰</p> <h2>Diverse Scientific Perspectives</h2> <p>Glaciologists such as Dr. Karen Holland (University of Cambridge) emphasize the role of surface melt in accelerating ice‑sheet flow. “The meltwater lubricates the base of the ice, allowing it to slide faster into the ocean,” she explains. Climate modelers, including Dr. Miguel García of the Max Planck Institute, note that the loss of ice mass amplifies atmospheric warming through albedo feedbacks and the release of trapped greenhouse gases from permafrost.¹¹</p> <p>Policy experts point to the implications for international agreements. “The Paris Agreement’s goal of limiting warming to 1.5 °C hinges on reducing emissions, but it also requires robust adaptation plans for rising sea levels,” says Ms. Aisha Patel, a climate policy analyst at the World Resources Institute. She highlights that many low‑lying island nations are already negotiating climate finance to build sea‑level defenses, citing the 6.8‑trillion‑ton loss as a tangible metric for funding requests.¹²</p> <h2>Policy Implications</h2> <p>Mitigation efforts must focus on reducing greenhouse gas emissions to slow the warming trend and to limit further ice‑sheet loss. Adaptation strategies, meanwhile, should prioritize the construction of resilient coastal infrastructure, the protection of vulnerable ecosystems, and the development of early‑warning systems for extreme weather events. International cooperation will be essential, as the impacts of sea‑level rise cross national borders and affect shared resources such as fisheries and freshwater supplies.</p> <p>In sum, the 6.8‑trillion‑ton loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past three decades is a clear indicator of a rapidly changing climate. Continued monitoring, transparent reporting, and decisive policy action are required to manage the risks and to safeguard the well‑being of communities worldwide.</p>
