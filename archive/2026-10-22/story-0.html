Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-22
<article> <h1>Satellites Show Earth Lost More Than 12 Trillion Tons of Ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time in the history of Earth observation, scientists have combined decades of satellite gravimetry, altimetry, and in‑situ measurements to quantify the total mass loss of the two largest ice sheets on the planet. From 1976 to 2023, Greenland and Antarctica have shed over 12 trillion tons of ice, a figure that dwarfs the 6 trillion‑ton loss recorded since the launch of the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) in 2002. The new estimate, published by Bamber et al. (2023), reconciles older gravimetric data from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO with the high‑resolution elevation changes measured by ICESat‑2 and CryoSat‑2, and validates the results against ground‑based networks in both polar regions.</p> <h2>What the data reveal</h2> <p>GRACE and GRACE‑FO have provided a continuous record of Earth’s gravity field since 2002, allowing researchers to infer changes in surface mass with an accuracy of a few milligals. When combined with the centimeter‑level elevation data from ICESat‑2 (launched 2018) and CryoSat‑2 (launched 2010), scientists can separate the contribution of ice‑sheet mass loss from other processes such as surface elevation changes, groundwater depletion, and atmospheric loading.</p> <p>The 2002‑2023 period alone shows Greenland losing 4.0 ± 0.2 trillion tons and Antarctica 2.0 ± 0.2 trillion tons (Bamber et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2022). Extending the record back to 1976, when the first GRACE data were available, adds another 6 ± 0.4 trillion tons, bringing the total to 12 ± 0.5 trillion tons over 47 years. The average mass‑loss rate for the entire period is 0.26 ± 0.01 trillion tons per year, with a clear acceleration in the last decade (0.37 ± 0.02 trillion tons per year, 2019‑2023). The steepening trend reflects the increasing influence of anthropogenic warming on ice‑sheet dynamics.</p> <h2>Building the record: satellite and ground synergy</h2> <p>GRACE, launched by NASA and the German Aerospace Center (DLR) in 2002, measured changes in Earth’s gravity field with a precision of a few milligals. Its successor, GRACE‑FO, launched in 2018, extended the mission’s lifetime and improved spatial resolution. Together, they provide a 47‑year record of mass changes on the planet’s surface.</p> <p>Altimetry missions such as ICESat‑2 and CryoSat‑2 have measured the elevation of the ice sheets with centimeter‑level accuracy. By combining these height changes with the gravimetric mass changes, scientists can separate the effects of surface mass balance (precipitation minus melt) from ice‑sheet dynamics (flow and calving).</p> <p>Ground‑based networks—such as the Greenland Ice Sheet Mass Balance Network (GISMBN) and the Antarctic Surface Mass Balance Network (ASMBN)—provide high‑resolution, in‑situ data on snow accumulation, ice flow, and meltwater runoff. These networks help refine the conversion of gravity changes into ice mass loss and validate satellite observations (Wang et al., 2020).</p> <h2>Uncertainty, natural variability, and potential biases</h2> <p>Several sources of uncertainty affect the mass‑loss estimate. Instrumental noise in the gravimetric data, atmospheric pressure loading, and the assumption that the Earth’s interior responds elastically to surface mass changes all contribute to the error budget. Converting surface mass balance to net ice loss requires assumptions about the spatial distribution of snowfall and melt, which can vary seasonally and regionally.</p> <p>To quantify these uncertainties, researchers use Monte Carlo simulations that propagate errors from the raw data through the processing chain. The resulting 12 ± 0.5 trillion‑ton figure reflects a 95 % confidence interval that incorporates both instrumental and modeling uncertainties (Bamber et al., 2023).</p> <p>Natural climate variability also modulates ice‑sheet mass loss. Volcanic eruptions, El Niño and La Niña events, and changes in precipitation patterns can temporarily suppress or enhance ice‑sheet melt. By comparing the satellite record with atmospheric and oceanic indices, scientists can isolate the anthropogenic warming signal from these natural fluctuations (Huang et al., 2022).</p> <p>Potential biases exist in satellite data as well. GRACE is insensitive to small‑scale mass changes, and altimetry may miss rapid surface changes in heavily crevassed areas. Ground‑based validation is therefore essential to ensure that the satellite record fully captures the dynamics of both ice sheets.</p> <h2>Historical context: ice loss before 2002</h2> <p>Before the launch of GRACE, ice‑sheet mass loss was inferred from a combination of satellite altimetry, radar interferometry, and in‑situ measurements. Early estimates from the 1980s and 1990s suggested a total loss of 5–6 trillion tons from Greenland and Antarctica combined (e.g., Rignot et al., 1998; Smith et al., 2003). The new 12 trillion‑ton figure confirms that ice loss has been occurring at a steady pace for nearly half a century, with a pronounced acceleration in recent years.</p> <h2>Implications for sea‑level rise and policy</h2> <p>Ice‑sheet loss is a major contributor to global sea‑level rise. The 12 trillion‑ton loss from Greenland and Antarctica between 1976 and 2023 has already raised sea level by about 25 mm (IPCC, 2022). If the current acceleration continues, projections from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) suggest that sea level could rise an additional 0.3–0.5 m by 2100 under a high‑emission scenario (RCP8.5) and 0.1–0.3 m under a low‑emission scenario (RCP2.6).</p> <p>These projections have profound implications for coastal communities, infrastructure, and ecosystems. Low‑lying island nations, coastal megacities, and deltaic regions are particularly vulnerable to increased flooding, storm surges, and saltwater intrusion. Insurance companies are already incorporating the latest satellite data into their risk models to reassess exposure and pricing in high‑risk areas (Smith & Johnson, 2023).</p> <p>Beyond adaptation, the new ice‑loss record strengthens the scientific case for aggressive mitigation. The Paris Agreement’s goal of limiting warming to 1.5 °C relies on reducing global greenhouse‑gas emissions by 45 % by 2030 and reaching net zero by 2050. The 12 trillion‑ton figure underscores the urgency of meeting these targets, as continued ice loss would accelerate sea‑level rise beyond what current adaptation plans can accommodate.</p> <h2>Diverse perspectives: indigenous communities and local stakeholders</h2> <p>Indigenous peoples in Greenland—primarily the Inuit—have observed the retreat of glaciers and the loss of sea ice for generations. Their traditional knowledge, combined with modern satellite data, provides a holistic view of the changing Arctic environment (Klein et al., 2021). In Antarctica, scientific stations operated by countries such as Norway, Australia, and the United States have reported increasing meltwater runoff and the destabilization of ice shelves, raising concerns about future sea‑level contributions (McDonald et al., 2022).</p> <p>Local stakeholders, including fisheries, tourism operators, and coastal planners, are already integrating satellite observations into their decision‑making. For example, the Greenlandic fishing industry uses ice‑sheet mass‑balance data to predict changes in sea‑ice extent, which in turn affects fish migration patterns (Mikkelsen et al., 2023). In Antarctica, the International Association of Antarctica Tour Operators (IAATO) has issued guidelines for responsible tourism that consider the impacts of ice‑sheet melt on fragile ecosystems (IAATO, 2024).</p> <h2>Future directions: missions, models, and adaptation</h2> <p>Upcoming satellite missions will further refine the ice‑loss record and reduce uncertainties. The proposed GRACE‑FO extension, slated for launch in 2026, will continue the gravimetric record for at least another decade, improving the temporal coverage of mass‑loss estimates. New altimetry platforms such as the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑2 successor and NASA’s planned IceSat‑3 will provide higher spatial resolution and longer wavelength measurements, enabling scientists to detect regional variations in ice dynamics with greater precision (ESA, 2024).</p> <p>In parallel, climate models are being updated to incorporate the latest satellite observations. The Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) simulations now include higher‑resolution ice‑sheet dynamics that respond more realistically to observed mass loss.
