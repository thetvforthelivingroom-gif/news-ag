More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-22
<h1>More Than 400 Schools in France Shut on Monday as Pension Reform Protests Escalate</h1> <p>On Monday, a wave of school closures swept across France, with more than 400 institutions shutting their doors for the day. The move, announced by a coalition of teachers’ unions and local authorities, is part of a broader protest movement that has intensified over the past weeks in response to the government’s pension reform plan. While the Ministry of Education has yet to issue an official statement, the closures have sparked concern among students, parents, and educators about the disruption of learning and the strain on the country’s already stretched educational infrastructure.</p> <h2>What Happened?</h2> <p>The decision to close schools was announced early on Monday morning by a coalition of unions—primarily the CGT (Confédération Générale du Travail), FO (Force Ouvrière), and SNES (Syndicat National des Enseignants et des Professeurs)—in coordination with local municipal councils. According to <a href="https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/france-400-schools-closed-monday-protest-2024-10-22/">Reuters</a>, the closures are a direct response to the escalating protests that have disrupted public services nationwide.</p> <p>Union spokespeople said the closures are a form of solidarity with workers in other sectors who are striking over the pension reform. “We are standing with all French workers who are fighting for fair pensions,” said a CGT representative. “Closing schools is a symbolic act that underscores the seriousness of the situation.”</p> <p>Teachers’ unions have also cited safety concerns. “With large crowds gathering outside school gates, we cannot guarantee the safety of students and staff,” explained a SNES spokesperson. “It is better to close the schools temporarily than to risk confrontation.”</p> <p>At present, the Ministry of Education has not confirmed the number of schools affected, nor has it issued an official statement. The figure of “more than 400” comes from union statements and local authorities, and has not yet been verified by the government.</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>The current wave of protests is rooted in a long history of social unrest in France. The “Yellow Vests” movement, which began in late 2018, was sparked by a proposed fuel tax increase but quickly broadened to encompass wider grievances about economic inequality and the perceived erosion of social protections. While the Yellow Vests protests largely subsided by 2019, the underlying tensions remained, and new grievances emerged in the following years.</p> <p>In 2023, the French government, led by President Emmanuel Macron, introduced a pension reform package that would raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and merge the country’s 42 existing pension schemes into a single, points‑based system. The reforms were intended to address the demographic shift toward an aging population and the fiscal pressures on the pension system. However, the proposals were met with fierce opposition from labor unions, who argued that they would erode workers’ rights and undermine the social safety net.</p> <p>Unions organized a series of strikes that began in early 2023, targeting public transport, healthcare, and education. The strikes have been characterized by both peaceful demonstrations and occasional clashes with police. The protests have also drawn criticism from some segments of the public, who view the strikes as disruptive to essential services.</p> <h2>Impact on Education</h2> <p>School closures are not a new phenomenon in France. Historically, teachers’ unions have used school closures as a bargaining tool during strikes. However, the scale of the current closures—over 400 schools—has raised concerns about the impact on students’ learning trajectories.</p> <p>“We are worried about the cumulative effect on students’ academic progress,” said a parent of a 12‑year‑old in Lyon. “When schools close, we have to find alternative childcare, and the learning that takes place at home is not the same.”</p> <p>Teachers have expressed mixed feelings. Some see the closures as a necessary step to protect students and staff, while others worry about the disruption to classroom instruction. “We are committed to our students, but we also have to consider their safety,” said a teacher in Marseille. “If the protests become violent, we cannot risk putting them in harm’s way.”</p> <p>Educational experts warn that repeated closures can have long‑term effects on student achievement, especially for those from disadvantaged backgrounds who rely on school resources such as meals, counseling, and extracurricular activities. A study by the French National Institute of Statistics and Economic Studies (INSEE) found that students who miss more than 10 days of school in a year fall behind their peers by an average of 0.3 grade levels, a gap that is harder to close later in their academic careers.</p> <h2>Government Response</h2> <p>President Macron’s administration has defended the pension reforms as necessary for the long‑term sustainability of the French pension system. In a televised address, he emphasized that the reforms are “a necessary step to preserve the social model that France has built over the last century.” He also urged the unions to “engage in constructive dialogue” rather than “disrupt essential services.”</p> <p>Meanwhile, the Ministry of Education issued a brief statement acknowledging the closures and urging parents to seek alternative arrangements. “We are monitoring the situation closely and will provide guidance on how to support students’ learning during this period,” the ministry said. The statement also called for “respect for the rights of all citizens to express their opinions peacefully.”</p> <p>Opposition parties, including the Socialist Party and the National Rally, have criticized the government’s handling of the protests. “The government is putting the nation’s future at risk by ignoring the legitimate concerns of workers,” said a Socialist MP. “We must find a way to address the pension crisis without compromising the rights of the people.”</p> <h2>Looking Ahead</h2> <p>Analysts predict that the protests could continue for several more weeks, with the possibility of additional school closures and disruptions to public transport and healthcare. The unions have called for a national strike on Friday, 25 October, which could see even more schools shut down.</p> <p>In the meantime, teachers and parents are scrambling to adapt. Some schools have arranged online tutoring sessions, while others have partnered with local community centers to provide meals and safe spaces for students. The Ministry of Education has announced a temporary funding boost for schools in high‑impact areas, aimed at mitigating the educational fallout.</p> <p>For students, the day’s closures mean a pause in their routine, but also a reminder of the broader social contract that underpins French society. As the country grapples with the tension between fiscal responsibility and social protection, the school closures serve as a stark illustration of how deeply intertwined public policy and everyday life have become.</p> <p>As the protests continue, the nation watches closely. The outcome of the pension reform debate will shape not only the future of France’s social safety net but also the daily lives of millions of students and their families.</p>
