More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-22
<article> On Monday, more than 400 schools across France were closed as part of a nationwide protest against President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform. The closures, announced by local education authorities, were a direct response to escalating demonstrations that have intensified since the reform was passed in late 2023. While the exact number of affected schools remains unconfirmed, the shutdowns spanned both urban centers and rural districts, underscoring the depth of opposition to the policy and the lengths to which demonstrators are willing to go to voice their dissent.

What happened

School administrators in cities and towns from Paris to Lyon received directives to suspend classes for the day, citing safety concerns amid the rising tide of protests. The decision was part of a broader strategy by protest organizers, who have long used school closures as a tactic to amplify pressure on the government. According to multiple domestic news outlets that covered the day’s events, more than 400 schools were shut down [1][2][3][4]. The closures were not confined to a single region; they were reported in metropolitan areas as well as in smaller towns throughout the country. The immediate impact was clear: students were sent home, parents were notified, and teachers faced uncertainty about their schedules and compensation.

The protests themselves are part of a sustained campaign against the pension reform, which has been a source of contention since its announcement. Demonstrators have employed a range of tactics—from marches and sit‑ins to the strategic shutdown of public institutions—to draw attention to their grievances. The decision to close schools was seen as a direct challenge to the state’s ability to maintain normalcy in the face of civil disobedience. While the government has not yet issued a formal statement specifically addressing the school closures, it has reiterated its commitment to the reform and called for calm among the populace [2][3].

Background and context

The pension reform, approved in December 2023, sought to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and to adjust the calculation of pension benefits. The administration framed the policy as a necessary step to ensure the sustainability of France’s pension system amid demographic changes. However, it was met with fierce opposition from labor unions and left‑leaning political groups, who argued that the reform would disproportionately affect workers in physically demanding jobs and undermine social equity [2][4].

The use of school closures as a form of protest is not new in France. During the Yellow Vest (Gilets Jaunes) movement in 2018, schools were shut down on several occasions, disrupting the education of thousands of students and drawing international attention to the protest’s intensity [1][5]. Similarly, the 2019 pension strikes saw a wave of school closures that highlighted the intersection of labor disputes and public service disruption. In each instance, the closures served both as a symbolic act of solidarity with the striking workers and as a practical means of drawing government attention to the protestors’ demands.

The current wave of protests has been fueled by a combination of economic anxiety, perceived erosion of worker rights, and a broader sense of disenfranchisement among certain segments of the population. The pension reform is seen by many as a symbol of a broader neoliberal agenda that prioritizes fiscal austerity over social welfare. The protests have been organized through a mix of traditional union channels and digital platforms, allowing for rapid mobilization and coordination across the country.

The bigger picture

From the perspective of teachers and school administrators, the closures have introduced significant logistical challenges. Schools must arrange for alternative learning arrangements, manage unpaid work, and address the emotional well‑being of students who are suddenly deprived of their routine. Parents have expressed concern about the loss of structured learning time and the potential impact on their children’s academic progress [4][6]. Local authorities have voiced worries about the strain on public resources, citing the need to maintain safety and order during the protests.

Unions and protest organizers, on the other hand, view the closures as a legitimate form of civil disobedience. They argue that the state’s refusal to negotiate on pension terms justifies the use of public institutions as leverage. In interviews, union leaders have highlighted the historical precedent of school closures as a powerful tool to force policy reconsideration. They also emphasize that the protests are not aimed at harming education but at ensuring that the voices of workers and citizens are heard.

Domestic media coverage has largely focused on the immediate impact on education and the broader political implications. Le Monde, for instance, has reported on the logistical fallout for schools and the broader debate over the reform’s fairness. The coverage has highlighted the tension between the need to protect the rights of workers and the imperative to maintain public services. Other outlets have examined the role of digital platforms in coordinating the protests, noting how social media has amplified the reach of union messages and allowed for real‑time updates on school closures.

Looking ahead

The situation remains fluid. While the government has reiterated its commitment to the pension reform, it has also called for dialogue with unions and has pledged to address concerns about the transition period for affected workers. The closures, however, signal that the protest movement is still active and that further disruptions to public services are possible if the underlying issues are not resolved.

For students, teachers, and parents, the immediate priority is to manage the disruption to learning. Schools are exploring distance‑learning options and community‑based support to mitigate the impact of the closures. Teachers are negotiating with local authorities to secure compensation for the lost instructional time, while parents are seeking guidance on how to keep their children engaged at home.

In the broader context, the protests highlight a growing unease with the pace of economic reforms in France. The pension reform, while aimed at ensuring long‑term fiscal stability, has exposed deep divisions over how social protections should evolve. The use of school closures as a protest tactic underscores the lengths to which citizens will go to demand a more inclusive dialogue on policy changes that affect their livelihoods.

As the country moves forward, the balance between maintaining public services and addressing legitimate worker concerns will remain a central challenge. The outcome of the current protests will likely influence not only the future of the pension system but also the broader relationship between the French state, its citizens, and the labor movement.
