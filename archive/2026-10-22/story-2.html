More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-22
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France shut their doors as protests over President Emmanuel Macron’s pension and labour‑market reforms intensified. The closures left students, teachers and families scrambling to navigate an uncertain educational landscape. They are part of a broader strategy by teachers’ unions to pressure the government, echoing the massive strikes that once rattled the nation’s pension system and the Yellow‑Vests movement that shook the country’s political order.

What happened

In the early hours of Monday, the French Ministry of Education announced that over 400 schools would be closed for the day. The ministry described the decision as a measure of “temporary safety,” following a wave of protests that have intensified since Macron announced his reforms to the pension system and the labour market. Thousands of teachers and other public‑sector workers have joined the protests, coordinating school closures to increase pressure on the government. The unions say the closures are a strategic pressure tactic, not a safety measure, and that they continue the tactics used during the 2018‑2019 pension protests and the Yellow‑Vests demonstrations that followed in 2019‑2020 [1].

The closures have affected a broad swath of the country, from Paris to the rural departments of Brittany and the Alps. While the exact number of students and teachers impacted remains unclear, the sheer scale of the shutdowns—over 400 schools—suggests that tens of thousands of learners and educators will be disrupted. The unions have called for a nationwide strike that would extend beyond the school closures, demanding a halt to the pension reforms and a review of the labour‑market changes rolled out over the past year [2].

Background and context

Macron’s pension and labour‑market reforms have been the centerpiece of a long‑running debate over France’s social contract. The pension reforms aim to raise the retirement age and streamline the pension system, while the labour‑market reforms seek to increase flexibility for employers and reduce the cost of hiring and firing. Together, these changes are seen by many as a threat to the stability of France’s welfare state, prompting widespread unrest.

The teachers’ unions, led by the National Union of Teachers (Syndicat National des Professeurs), have long been a powerful force in French politics. Their strategy of using school closures as a form of protest dates back to the 2018‑2019 pension protests, when they shut down schools across the country to draw attention to the reforms. The unions also played a role in the Yellow‑Vests movement, which began in 2019 as a protest against fuel taxes and quickly expanded into a broader critique of Macron’s policies. The current wave of protests is the latest manifestation of the deep divisions that have emerged over the past decade.

France’s economic backdrop is also significant. The country has been grappling with a rising cost of living, high unemployment rates, and a growing debt burden. Many workers feel that the reforms will undermine job security and erode the social safety net that has been a hallmark of the French welfare state. The unions argue that the reforms will lead to higher pension contributions, lower benefits, and a more precarious labour market, especially for younger workers and those in low‑wage jobs [3].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Government’s position

The French government frames the school closures as a temporary safety measure, citing concerns about the safety of students and staff during the protests. A spokesperson for the Ministry of Education said the closures were necessary to protect all parties involved and would be lifted once the situation stabilized. The government has also reiterated its commitment to the pension and labour‑market reforms, arguing that they are necessary to modernise France’s economy and make the welfare state more sustainable in the long term [1].

Unions’ stance

Teachers’ unions view the closures as a strategic pressure tactic designed to force the government to reconsider its reforms. They argue that the safety concerns cited by the government are a pretext for undermining the educational system and that the closures are a form of collective action that has proven effective in the past. The unions have called for a national strike that would extend beyond the school closures, demanding a halt to the pension reforms and a review of the labour‑market changes rolled out over the past year [2].

Students and families

For students and families, the school closures have created uncertainty and disruption. Many parents have had to find alternative childcare arrangements or adjust their work schedules to accommodate the closures. Some students have expressed frustration at the lack of continuity in their education, while others have taken the opportunity to reflect on the broader political debate that has shaped their country’s future. The closures have also highlighted the importance of the school system as a social safety net, especially for vulnerable families who rely on school meals and other support services [3].

Local businesses and communities

Local businesses and communities have also felt the impact of the school closures. Some businesses report a decline in foot traffic, while others see an increase in demand for alternative childcare services. The closures have underscored the role of schools as community hubs, with many residents expressing concern about the loss of a safe and reliable space for children to learn and grow. The unions argue that the closures are a necessary form of protest that will ultimately lead to a more equitable and sustainable social contract for all French citizens [1].

International observers

International observers have closely monitored the situation. The Guardian, Reuters, and the Washington Post have provided extensive coverage of the protests and the school closures. These outlets highlight the broader context of the protests, including the economic and political factors that have contributed to the unrest. They also emphasize the importance of the school closures as a form of protest that has been used by teachers’ unions in the past to draw attention to their demands and influence policy decisions [2,3].

What to watch (future implications)

The school closures are just one manifestation of a broader wave of protests that could have lasting implications for France’s political and social landscape. Key areas to watch include:

1. **Negotiations between the government and unions** – The outcome of any negotiations will determine whether the pension and labour‑market reforms will be modified or halted. The unions’ willingness to continue striking and the government’s willingness to compromise will shape the next phase of the protests.

2. **Impact on students’ learning** – The closures have disrupted the academic calendar and raised concerns about the continuity of education. The government’s response, including potential compensation or support for affected students, will be closely scrutinised.

3. **Public sentiment** – The protests have exposed deep divisions within French society. The government’s ability to address the concerns of workers, students, and families will be a key determinant of its legitimacy and stability.

4. **International perception** – France’s political stability and its commitment to social reforms will be closely monitored by international investors, trade partners, and global institutions. The outcome of the protests could influence France’s standing on the world stage.

5. **Future protest strategies** – The effectiveness of school closures as a protest tactic will be evaluated by both unions and the government. The unions may consider expanding their tactics, while the government may seek alternative ways to address the underlying grievances.

In the coming weeks, the French government will need to balance the demands of teachers’ unions with the broader economic and political imperatives that underlie the pension and labour‑market reforms. The outcome of this confrontation will shape the future of France’s social contract and the role of the state in protecting the welfare of its citizens. The school closures, while a temporary disruption, are a stark reminder of the power of collective action and the enduring tensions that define French politics today.
