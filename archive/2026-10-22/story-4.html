Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-22
When President Volodymyr Zelensky said that Ukraine’s cyber‑strikes had “taken down” Russian bot networks, the world was left to wonder whether the claim was a tactical victory, a political statement, or a misreading of cyber warfare’s messy realities. The assertion sits at the intersection of military action, information operations, and the politics of perception that have defined the Ukraine conflict for years. In the following pages we trace the events that led to the statement, examine the technical and historical backdrop, weigh the competing narratives, and consider what this episode signals for the future of hybrid warfare.

What happened

In early March 2024, Ukrainian cyber‑defence units reported that a series of coordinated attacks had targeted data centres believed to host large botnets used by Russian state‑backed actors. According to the Ministry of Digital Transformation, the strikes disrupted command‑and‑control servers and severed the flow of automated propaganda and denial‑of‑service traffic. The ministry’s statement, released on the same day, was accompanied by a brief video montage of server logs and a graphic illustrating the “down” status of several key nodes. President Zelensky, speaking to the nation in a televised address, echoed the ministry’s claims, asserting that the cyber‑operations had “taken down” a significant portion of Russia’s bot networks and that the attacks would hamper Moscow’s information‑war capabilities for weeks to come [T3].

Russian officials dismissed the allegations outright. The Russian Ministry of Digital Development issued a statement that no data centres or bot networks had been affected, citing “robust cybersecurity measures” and “continuous monitoring” that had detected and mitigated any attempts at intrusion. Russian state media ran a series of counter‑stories portraying the Ukrainian claims as “propaganda” designed to boost morale among Ukrainian troops and the international community. Meanwhile, NATO’s cyber‑security division released a brief assessment noting that while Ukrainian forces had indeed conducted cyber‑operations against Russian infrastructure, the extent of the damage remained unverified by independent observers [T3].

Both sides have framed the incident as a symbolic victory in the broader contest of information dominance. For Ukraine, the narrative underscores the nation’s capacity to conduct sophisticated cyber‑attacks on a global scale, reinforcing its image as a modern, tech‑savvy state capable of counter‑ing a larger adversary. For Russia, the denial serves to maintain confidence among its populace and to signal that its cyber‑defence remains resilient despite external pressure. The lack of independent verification has turned the episode into a battleground of narratives, with each side leveraging the claim to influence domestic and international audiences.

Background and context

The claim that data‑centre strikes can cripple bot networks is not unprecedented in the Ukraine conflict. In 2015–2016, Ukrainian researchers and journalists reported that a sophisticated cyber‑attack had disrupted the Ukrainian power grid, causing widespread outages and exposing the vulnerabilities of critical infrastructure to state‑backed adversaries [T3]. In 2018, Russian state media outlets were targeted by coordinated cyber‑attacks that disrupted broadcasts and forced the removal of certain political content, illustrating how cyber operations can be used to manipulate media narratives [T3]. The Stuxnet worm, discovered in 2010, further demonstrated how state actors could infiltrate industrial control systems to cause physical damage, blurring the line between conventional and cyber warfare [T3].

Botnets—networks of compromised computers that can be remotely controlled to launch distributed denial‑of‑service (DDoS) attacks, spread malware, or disseminate disinformation—have become a staple of modern hybrid warfare. By aggregating the computational power of thousands of infected machines, state actors can amplify their reach and conceal the origin of their attacks. Data centres, with their high bandwidth and robust infrastructure, are attractive targets for both launching and hosting bot networks. Disrupting these facilities can theoretically reduce the operational capacity of botnets, but the scale and resilience of such networks often make a single strike insufficient to cause lasting damage [T3].

The Ukrainian Ministry of Digital Transformation’s claim that a series of strikes has taken down a significant portion of Russia’s bot networks is therefore grounded in a historical pattern of cyber‑operations that target critical infrastructure and information channels. However, the technical feasibility of completely dismantling a botnet through data‑centre strikes remains a subject of debate among cyber‑security experts. While a well‑executed attack can sever command‑and‑control links and cause temporary outages, botnets are typically designed with redundancy and fallback mechanisms that allow them to re‑establish control through alternate channels [T3].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Ukrainian perspective

From the Ukrainian viewpoint, the cyber‑strike is a strategic asset in the broader war effort. Ukrainian officials have repeatedly highlighted the role of cyber‑operations in complementing conventional military actions, arguing that they can degrade the adversary’s capabilities without escalating kinetic conflict. In the March 2024 address, President Zelensky emphasized that the cyber‑strike “demonstrates our ability to hit the enemy’s digital heartland” and that it would “disrupt the flow of propaganda and misinformation that fuels the war” [T3]. The Ministry of Digital Transformation’s brief assessment also noted that the attacks had forced Russian bot operators to divert resources to defensive measures, thereby stretching their operational bandwidth.

Russian perspective

Russia’s official narrative has been one of resilience and denial. The Ministry of Digital Development’s statement that no data centres were affected is consistent with a broader strategy of downplaying cyber losses to maintain domestic confidence. Russian state media have amplified this narrative, framing the Ukrainian claim as “psychological warfare” aimed at sowing doubt among the Russian public. Analysts in Moscow have also suggested that the cyber‑strike may have been part of a broader information campaign designed to create uncertainty about the efficacy of Russian cyber‑operations, thereby diverting attention from other strategic setbacks [T3].

International perspective

International observers, including NATO and EU cyber‑security bodies, have adopted a cautious stance. While acknowledging that Ukraine has conducted cyber‑operations against Russian infrastructure, they have called for independent verification of the extent of the damage. NATO’s cyber‑security division has emphasized the importance of transparency and the need for third‑party assessments to prevent misinformation. The European Union’s cyber‑security agency has highlighted the potential for escalation if cyber‑attacks are misinterpreted as acts of war, urging both sides to adhere to established norms and to engage in confidence‑building measures [T3].

Expert perspective

Cyber‑security analysts have expressed skepticism about the claim that a data‑centre strike can “take down” a large bot network. While acknowledging that attacks on data centres can disrupt command‑and‑control infrastructure, experts point out that botnets are typically distributed across multiple servers and can quickly re‑establish control through alternate channels. Analysts have also noted that the lack of independent verification makes it difficult to assess the true impact of the strikes. Some experts have suggested that the Ukrainian claim may be a strategic communication effort designed to boost morale and signal technological prowess, rather than a precise technical assessment of the damage inflicted [T3].

What to watch (future implications)

The March 2024 cyber‑strike episode highlights several key trends that will shape the future of hybrid warfare.

First, the increasing reliance on data centres as both launchpads and targets for cyber‑operations underscores the need for robust cyber‑defence mechanisms within critical infrastructure. Nations will likely invest in hardened data‑centre designs, redundant command‑and‑control pathways, and real‑time monitoring systems to mitigate the risk of botnet disruption.

Second, the episode illustrates the blurred line between cyber and conventional warfare. While Ukraine’s cyber‑strike did not result in physical destruction, it had the potential to disrupt the flow of information and to degrade the adversary’s operational capabilities. This raises questions about the applicability of existing international law to cyber‑operations that do not involve kinetic damage but still influence the conduct of war. Future diplomatic negotiations may need to address the legal status of cyber‑attacks on data centres and the thresholds for escalation.

Third, the role of narrative and information control remains central to modern conflict. Both sides have used the claim to shape domestic and international perceptions, and the lack of independent verification has amplified uncertainty. This underscores the importance of transparent verification mechanisms, such as third‑party audits and open‑source intelligence, to provide objective assessments of cyber‑attack impacts. International bodies may consider developing frameworks for independent cyber‑attack verification to reduce the risk of misinterpretation and escalation.

Finally, the incident signals a continued evolution of botnet architecture. As state actors seek to maintain operational resilience, botnets are likely to incorporate more sophisticated redundancy, encryption, and decentralized control mechanisms. Cyber‑defence strategies will need to adapt accordingly, focusing on early detection, rapid isolation, and the development of counter‑botnet tools that can neutralise compromised nodes without relying solely on data‑centre strikes.

In sum, Zelensky’s claim that data‑centre strikes have taken down Russian bot networks is emblematic of the complex interplay between technology, strategy, and perception that defines hybrid warfare today. While the technical feasibility of completely dismantling a botnet through data‑centre attacks remains contested, the episode has underscored the importance of verification, the evolving nature of cyber threats, and the enduring power of narrative in shaping the course of conflict.
