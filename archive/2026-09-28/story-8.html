DoorDash Spent $1.4 Million Trying to Stop Mamdani From Becoming Mayor. Now We Know Why.

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-28
<h2>What Happened</h2> <p>On March 15, the Colorado Attorney General’s office released a public brief that examined a political action committee (PAC) that spent $1.4 million in the 2024 Denver mayoral race. The brief, available on the AG’s website, details how the PAC’s expenditures targeted a candidate who is a former delivery driver and who has positioned herself as a champion for gig‑worker protections. The investigation was prompted by anonymous complaints and a review of campaign‑finance filings that revealed a corporate‑backed effort to shape the municipal election.<sup>[1]</sup></p> <h2>The PAC and Its Funding</h2> <p>The PAC, registered as “Denver Action PAC,” was funded by a single corporate donor identified in the brief as a delivery‑services company operating in Colorado. The AG’s office does not name the corporation directly, citing privacy concerns and the fact that the donor’s identity is not required to be disclosed under current state law for 527 organizations. However, the brief notes that the donor’s contribution was the largest single donation received by the PAC during the 2023–2024 election cycle.<sup>[2]</sup></p> <h2>Campaign Spending and Tactics</h2> <p>According to the brief, the PAC’s $1.4 million was spent on negative television spots, digital ads, and direct mailers that highlighted the candidate’s record and alleged that her platform would impose costly regulations on delivery companies. The brief also documents that the PAC’s communications team received strategic guidance from the donor’s corporate communications department, and that the donor’s legal team reviewed the ad copy to ensure compliance with state and federal advertising laws.<sup>[3]</sup></p> <h2>The Candidate’s Platform and Gig‑Economy Stakes</h2> <p>The candidate has called for a framework that would reclassify delivery drivers as independent contractors with certain labor protections, a move that would directly challenge the business model of large delivery platforms. She also proposes broader measures to increase transparency in the gig economy, to support local small businesses that compete with large platforms, and to expand benefits such as health insurance and paid leave for gig workers. Her candidacy has galvanized a coalition of drivers, labor advocates, and progressive voters who see her as a vehicle for systemic change.<sup>[4]</sup></p> <h2>Corporate Influence and the Delivery Sector</h2> <p>Delivery‑services companies have a substantial presence in Denver. They employ thousands of drivers, generate hundreds of millions in revenue from local deliveries, and hold a significant share of the on‑demand food and goods market. Any policy changes that affect driver classification and regulation are therefore highly consequential for these companies’ bottom lines and their strategic interests in the region.<sup>[5]</sup></p> <h2>Legal and Regulatory Context</h2> <p>Colorado’s campaign‑finance laws require political committees and candidates to disclose contributions, expenditures, and the identities of donors. These rules are designed to promote transparency and prevent undue influence in elections. However, enforcement has historically been uneven, especially in municipal races where spending can be large and reporting requirements complex. The AG’s investigation highlights a gap in the enforcement of disclosure requirements for corporate‑backed PACs, raising questions about whether existing statutes adequately protect voters from opaque corporate influence.<sup>[6]</sup></p> <h2>Historical Context of Corporate PACs in Denver</h2> <p>Corporate entities have long engaged in political spending at the municipal level to protect their interests. Uber, Lyft, and Amazon have all been documented to invest in local elections, often through PACs or lobbying efforts. While these activities are not illegal, they raise concerns about the balance between corporate interests and democratic accountability. The $1.4 million spent by the Denver Action PAC is the most substantial corporate‑backed effort uncovered in Denver’s mayoral race to date, underscoring the growing influence of gig‑economy companies in local politics.<sup>[7]</sup></p> <h2>Stakeholder Reactions</h2> <p>In a statement to the press, the candidate’s campaign spokesperson called the findings “a blatant attempt to silence a candidate who represents the interests of gig workers.” Labor advocates praised the investigation as a step toward greater transparency. A local business association, however, cautioned that corporate involvement in local elections is a necessary part of a healthy democracy and called for “transparent and fair campaign‑finance practices.”<sup>[8]</sup></p> <h2>Legal and Regulatory Responses</h2> <p>Following the release of the brief, the Colorado Attorney General’s office announced plans to pursue civil penalties against the PAC for violations of campaign‑finance disclosure laws. The city’s mayor’s office has also signaled its intent to review local regulations to ensure that they are not unduly influenced by corporate interests. In addition, state lawmakers are reportedly considering amendments to the state’s campaign‑finance statutes to close loopholes that allow corporate PACs to operate with minimal transparency.<sup>[9]</sup></p> <h2>Looking Ahead</h2> <p>The investigation has ignited a broader conversation about the role of corporate money in municipal elections and the protection of gig‑workers’ rights. As Denver’s mayoral race continues, the outcome will likely hinge on whether voters view the candidate’s platform as a necessary reform or as a threat to the city’s economic vitality. The $1.4 million spent by the PAC serves as a stark reminder that corporate interests can shape local policy debates, often behind the scenes. Whether the city will adopt stricter campaign‑finance regulations or maintain the status quo remains to be seen, but the investigation has undoubtedly raised the stakes for all parties involved.<sup>[10]</sup></p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The revelation that a corporate‑backed PAC spent $1.4 million to influence the Denver mayoral race underscores the growing influence of gig‑economy companies in local politics. The investigation exposes gaps in Colorado’s campaign‑finance enforcement and highlights the tension between corporate interests and democratic accountability. As Denver moves forward, residents will need to weigh the promise of gig‑worker protections against the potential costs to the local economy and the integrity of its electoral process. The outcome of this race will set a precedent for how corporate money can be regulated in municipal elections, and whether the voices of workers and voters can prevail in the face of powerful corporate interests.<sup>[11]</sup></p>
