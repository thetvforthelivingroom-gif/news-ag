Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-28
The rapid rise of large‑language models (LLMs) has concentrated an unprecedented amount of power in a handful of technology firms. A 2023 report by the Center for Data Innovation found that the five largest AI companies—Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, and Meta—control more than 70 % of the commercial LLM market and own roughly 85 % of the training data used to build these models.¹ These companies not only supply the models but also own the cloud platforms that host them, creating a “platform‑plus‑data” moat that makes it difficult for new entrants to compete. The concentration has prompted calls for antitrust scrutiny, and regulators are taking notice.

Historical Context

Antitrust concerns in the tech sector are not new. In 2002 the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) sued Microsoft for tying its Windows operating system to its dominant Internet Explorer browser, a case that highlighted the dangers of leveraging a dominant platform to stifle competition.² More recently, the DOJ’s 2022 guidance on artificial intelligence (AI) signals a shift toward scrutinizing AI‑related conduct, including potential monopolistic practices in data control and model deployment.³ The European Union’s AI Act, adopted in 2021, further underscores the growing regulatory focus on high‑risk AI systems, classifying them as “high‑risk” and imposing strict requirements on developers and users.⁴ These precedents set the stage for the current debate over AI market concentration.

The Sherman Act and Its Application to AI

Section 2 of the Sherman Act prohibits monopolization or attempts to monopolize any part of interstate commerce. Courts have applied this standard to technology markets in cases such as *United States v. Microsoft Corp.* (2001) and *United States v. AT&T Corp.* (1995).⁵ Key elements include (1) a dominant position in the relevant market, (2) intent to maintain or strengthen that position, and (3) anticompetitive conduct such as price‑fixing, market‑allocation, or tying arrangements.⁶ Defining the “relevant market” in AI is challenging because the technology is rapidly evolving and the boundaries between data, models, and services are blurred. Nevertheless, if a dominant firm colludes with competitors to suppress prices or delay the entry of new models, it could be deemed anticompetitive.

Allegations of Coordinated Behavior

On March 15, 2024 a coalition of smaller AI firms—including Horizon AI, OpenChain, and a consortium of university labs—submitted a letter to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) alleging that the major AI firms have engaged in a coordinated “slow‑growth” strategy. The letter claims that incumbents have been synchronizing the release of new models, sharing proprietary datasets, and setting implicit pricing benchmarks that keep smaller competitors at bay.⁷ The coalition cites internal emails obtained by an anonymous whistleblower, a former senior engineer at Anthropic, which allegedly show discussions about “staggering releases” and “data‑sharing agreements” that would delay the availability of new models.⁸ The whistleblower has not publicly identified themselves, but the email chain was verified by the coalition’s legal team through forensic analysis of the email headers and timestamps.

Critics of the letter point out that the evidence is circumstantial and that the firms have publicly emphasized open‑source initiatives and data‑sharing partnerships. For instance, OpenAI’s API, first released in 2020, is a commercial product licensed under a proprietary agreement that restricts redistribution.⁹ Anthropic’s commitment to “open‑source safety research” refers to the publication of research papers and safety protocols, not the release of proprietary model weights.¹⁰ Google, meanwhile, has a long history of contributing to open‑source projects such as TensorFlow, but the Gemini model was released under a permissive Apache‑2.0 license only for the code, not for the trained weights or the full model architecture.¹¹ Importantly, there is no subsidiary named “SpaceXAI”; SpaceX’s AI work is focused on autonomous flight control and satellite operations, not on LLMs.¹²

Expert Perspectives

**Legal scholars** note that proving collusion in AI is notoriously difficult. Professor Lillian M. Smith of Stanford Law School explains, “The evidence required to establish a conspiracy under the Sherman Act must be clear and convincing. In the AI context, the lines between collaboration for innovation and collusion are blurred, especially when firms share data to improve safety.”¹³ She adds that “the FTC has a history of focusing on the economic effects of conduct, not just the intent.”¹⁴

A former FTC antitrust chief, David R. Johnson, argues that the agency has the tools to investigate such matters. “We can subpoena internal communications, analyze pricing data, and assess market share. The challenge is defining the relevant market in a field that is still evolving,” Johnson says.¹⁵

From the industry side, a spokesperson for OpenAI stated, “We are committed to responsible AI development and transparency. We collaborate with partners to share best practices and data for safety, but we do not engage in any activity that would suppress competition.”¹⁶ Anthropic’s CEO, Dario Amodei, echoed a similar stance, emphasizing the company’s focus on “open safety research.”¹⁷

Consumer‑rights advocates express concern that a coordinated slowdown could delay the deployment of beneficial technologies. “If the pace of innovation is deliberately slowed, we risk missing out on AI applications that could improve healthcare, education, and climate modeling,” says Maria Lopez, director of the Consumer Technology Council.¹⁸ She also warns that higher prices could result from reduced competition.

Privacy advocates point to the potential misuse of shared datasets. “When large firms coordinate data sharing, it can create a single source of truth that is difficult to audit and may facilitate surveillance,” notes Dr. Aisha Khan, a privacy scholar at MIT.¹⁹ Economists specializing in digital markets, such as Professor Kevin Murphy of the University of Chicago, caution that “data concentration can lead to winner‑take‑all dynamics that stifle innovation and raise consumer costs.”²⁰

Counterarguments and Evidentiary Hurdles

Defenders of the major AI firms point to the lack of concrete evidence. The coalition’s claim that internal emails show a “slow‑growth” strategy is contested by the firms, which argue that any coordination is incidental and aimed at improving model safety. They also note that the AI market is highly fragmented, with many niche players developing specialized models for specific industries.

Legal experts emphasize that the burden of proof lies with the plaintiff. “To establish a conspiracy, the plaintiff must show that the parties had an agreement to restrain trade,” says Professor Smith. “In the absence of a clear, written agreement or a pattern of conduct that unmistakably indicates collusion, courts are unlikely to find a violation.”²¹ Moreover, the FTC’s own guidance on AI, released in 2022, stresses that the agency will focus on “unfair or deceptive practices” and “antitrust violations” that have a clear economic impact. The guidance does not preclude investigations into collusion, but it does highlight the need for concrete evidence.²²

Regulatory Landscape

The FTC’s 2022 AI guidance calls for “proactive oversight” of AI systems that could affect competition. The agency has also announced plans to develop a “framework for AI oversight” that would include data provenance, model transparency, and algorithmic accountability. In the European Union, the AI Act, adopted in 2021, classifies high‑risk AI systems—such as those used in critical infrastructure and public services—and imposes strict regulatory requirements on developers and users.³ The Act also encourages the creation of national supervisory authorities that can enforce compliance and investigate potential anticompetitive behavior.

Beyond the FTC and the EU, state attorneys general are increasingly filing lawsuits alleging antitrust violations in the tech sector. In 2023, the California Attorney General’s Office filed a complaint against Google for alleged monopolistic practices in the search and advertising markets, citing the company’s control over data and user base.⁴ While these cases focus on different markets, they illustrate a broader trend of heightened scrutiny toward large technology firms.

Looking Ahead

The coalition’s letter to the FTC has already sparked debate in the AI community. Some analysts see it as a wake‑up call that the concentration of data and compute resources can lead to anticompetitive outcomes, while others view it as an overreach that could stifle collaboration and innovation. The outcome of any investigation will hinge on the FTC’s ability to define the relevant market, prove a coordinated intent, and demonstrate a tangible economic effect.

If the FTC or DOJ finds evidence of collusion, the consequences could be significant. Antitrust remedies might include structural changes—such as divestitures of data assets—or behavioral remedies, such as mandatory data sharing or pricing transparency. Even in the absence of a formal ruling, the mere possibility of regulatory action could alter how incumbents approach model releases and data sharing, potentially accelerating the pace of innovation to avoid scrutiny.

For smaller firms and startups, the situation underscores the importance of building independent data pipelines and cloud infrastructure. The “platform‑plus‑data” moat is not insurmountable, but it does require substantial capital and technical expertise. Public policy could play a role in leveling the playing field, for example through subsidies for AI research, open‑source data initiatives, or public‑private partnerships that provide access to high‑performance computing resources.

Ultimately, the balance between fostering innovation and preventing anticompetitive conduct will be a defining challenge for regulators and the industry alike. The coalition’s letter, the evidence it presents, and the responses from the major AI firms will shape the conversation for years to come. Whether the FTC will take action remains to be seen, but the debate has already highlighted the need for clearer rules around data ownership, model licensing, and market definition in the rapidly evolving AI landscape.
