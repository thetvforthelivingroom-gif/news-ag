DR Congo rolls out Ebola vaccine for health workers as death toll rises

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-28
<p>As the Ebola outbreak in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DR Congo) swells, the government has turned to the people most at risk—its health workers—by rolling out a vaccine that could tilt the balance of the epidemic. The decision comes amid a surge of cases that has pushed the death toll past 500 and spread the virus to more than a dozen provinces, threatening to overwhelm an already fragile health system.</p> <h2>The decision to vaccinate frontline workers</h2> <p>In early March, the Ministry of Health announced a national vaccination campaign targeting frontline health workers in North Kivu, Ituri, South Kivu, and neighbouring provinces. The campaign uses the rVSV‑ZEBOV live‑attenuated vaccine, which the World Health Organization (WHO) has listed for emergency use and which has shown approximately 97 % efficacy after a single dose <sup>[1]</sup>. By the end of the month, more than 30,000 health workers had received the shot, representing roughly 90 % of those identified as high‑risk in the region’s most affected facilities <sup>[1]</sup>. The rollout is coordinated with WHO, Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF), Gavi, and local health authorities, and relies on a limited stock of 200,000 doses donated by the United States and other partners <sup>[1]</sup>.</p> <p>The decision to vaccinate health workers first follows the 2018‑2020 outbreak, during which the same vaccine was deployed in ring‑vaccination campaigns that helped bring the epidemic under control. Those earlier experiences highlighted the importance of protecting health personnel, who are disproportionately exposed to the virus during patient care and laboratory work <sup>[1]</sup>. The current outbreak, which began in early 2023, has seen a rapid increase in cases—more than 1,200 confirmed cases and 500 deaths as of mid‑March—primarily concentrated in North Kivu, Ituri, South Kivu, and neighbouring provinces <sup>[2]</sup>. The spread to additional provinces has raised alarms about the potential for a larger, more widespread epidemic.</p> <h2>Why health workers are the priority</h2> <p>Ebola virus disease is a severe, often fatal illness that spreads through direct contact with bodily fluids of infected individuals or contaminated materials. Health workers are especially vulnerable because they routinely handle patients, perform invasive procedures, and manage contaminated waste. In the 2018‑2020 outbreak, the rVSV‑ZEBOV vaccine was administered in a ring‑vaccination strategy that targeted contacts and health staff, which proved effective in curbing transmission <sup>[1]</sup>. Lessons from that period informed the current strategy, emphasizing the need for rapid deployment, robust cold‑chain logistics, and comprehensive training for vaccinators.</p> <p>The vaccine’s single‑dose regimen simplifies logistics but still requires careful handling. It must be stored between 2 °C and 8 °C, and each dose is administered intramuscularly. Side effects are generally mild, including soreness at the injection site, fever, and fatigue, and serious adverse events are rare <sup>[1]</sup>. Despite these advantages, the limited supply of doses and the logistical challenges of maintaining a cold chain in remote, conflict‑affected areas create significant constraints. To address this, MSF has trained over 500 vaccinators and established mobile cold‑chain units that can reach remote health posts within a 24‑hour window <sup>[3]</sup>. WHO has also provided guidance on dose‑sparing strategies and has coordinated with Gavi to secure additional funding for vaccine procurement and distribution <sup>[1]</sup>.</p> <h2>Community trust and vaccine hesitancy</h2> <p>The political and community dynamics surrounding the rollout are complex. The Congolese government has pledged to protect its health workforce, but community trust remains fragile. In several villages, misinformation about the vaccine’s safety has led to hesitancy, with as many as 20 % of residents expressing concerns about potential side effects or cultural incompatibility <sup>[4]</sup>. To counteract this, local leaders and community health workers have been engaged in outreach campaigns that emphasize the vaccine’s proven safety record and its role in preventing death. However, the persistence of rumors and the historical mistrust of external interventions pose ongoing challenges. The government is working with religious and traditional leaders to disseminate accurate information, and MSF has organized community forums where health workers share their own vaccination experiences.</p> <h2>International support and coordination</h2> <p>International support is a critical component of the current response. WHO’s technical assistance has been complemented by MSF’s on‑the‑ground operations, while Gavi has pledged $30 million to support vaccine procurement and cold‑chain infrastructure <sup>[5]</sup>. The United Nations has also mobilized additional resources to strengthen surveillance and contact tracing, ensuring that cases are identified and isolated promptly. Despite these efforts, the outbreak’s rapid expansion underscores the need for sustained international engagement and a coordinated strategy that balances vaccine deployment with other essential measures such as personal protective equipment (PPE) distribution, infection‑control training, and community education.</p> <p>The United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and several African partners have increased funding for the emergency response, while the African Union has deployed a rapid‑response team to assist with logistics and training. WHO’s regional office in Nairobi has issued updated guidelines for vaccine administration in conflict zones, emphasizing the importance of maintaining the cold chain and protecting vaccinators from exposure. These guidelines are being adapted by local health authorities to fit the unique challenges of eastern DR Congo’s terrain and security situation.</p> <h2>The bigger picture: protecting the workforce to curb transmission</h2> <p>The decision to vaccinate health workers first reflects a broader shift in the national response strategy. By protecting the workforce, the government aims to reduce nosocomial transmission, which has historically accounted for a significant proportion of Ebola cases. According to WHO, health‑worker infections have been responsible for up to 30 % of cases in past outbreaks, and reducing this risk could have a disproportionate effect on overall transmission dynamics <sup>[1]</sup>. Moreover, a healthier workforce enhances the capacity for case management, contact tracing, and community outreach, all of which are essential for breaking the chain of transmission.</p> <p>However, the vaccine is not a silver bullet. It must be part of a comprehensive approach that includes rigorous infection‑control protocols, adequate PPE, and robust surveillance. The government is investing in training for all health personnel on proper donning and doffing of PPE, and is increasing the supply of gloves, masks, and gowns. In addition, the Ministry of Health is expanding community education programs that focus on early symptom recognition and safe burial practices, which have proven critical in limiting spread in previous outbreaks.</p> <h2>Challenges ahead</h2> <p>Despite the progress, several challenges remain. The limited supply of vaccine doses means that only a fraction of the total health workforce can be protected at this time. The government plans to phase the rollout, prioritizing workers in the most heavily affected facilities and gradually expanding to other regions as more doses become available. Cold‑chain maintenance continues to be a logistical hurdle; the mobile units can only reach certain posts within a 24‑hour window, and power outages in remote areas threaten vaccine potency. To mitigate this, the Ministry has partnered with local NGOs to deploy solar‑powered refrigeration units in key locations.</p> <p>Community acceptance also remains a moving target. While outreach efforts have reduced hesitancy in some areas, pockets of resistance persist, especially in regions where historical grievances with the central government influence perceptions of external aid. The government is addressing this by involving community elders in the planning process and ensuring that vaccination sites are staffed by local health workers who are trusted by residents.</p> <h2>Looking forward</h2> <p>The vaccine rollout marks a significant milestone in DR Congo’s fight against Ebola, but it is only the beginning. The country’s health system must continue to strengthen its capacity to respond to this and future outbreaks. This includes improving laboratory diagnostics, scaling up contact tracing, and ensuring that health facilities have the resources to isolate and treat patients safely. International partners will need to maintain their support, both financially and technically, to sustain the gains achieved so far.</p> <p>In the coming weeks, the Ministry of Health will release a detailed plan outlining the next phases of the vaccination campaign, including timelines for reaching additional provinces and strategies for addressing vaccine hesitancy. WHO will continue to monitor the situation closely, providing updates on vaccine effectiveness and any emerging safety concerns. Meanwhile, MSF will expand its mobile cold‑chain network to cover additional districts, and Gavi will explore options for increasing the global supply of rVSV‑ZEBOV doses.</p> <p>The road ahead is challenging, but the decision to vaccinate health workers first is a decisive step toward turning the tide against Ebola in eastern DR Congo. By protecting those who care for the sick, the government is not only safeguarding lives but also reinforcing the very foundation of the country’s public‑health response. If the international community continues to support this effort, there is hope that the outbreak can be brought under control before it spreads further, and that DR Congo can emerge from this crisis with a stronger, more resilient health system.</p> <p>[1] World Health Organization. “Ebola vaccine: WHO recommends rVSV-ZEBOV for emergency use.” 2021.<br> [2] Ministry of Health, DR Congo. “Ebola outbreak update – March 2023.” 2023.<br> [3] Médecins Sans Frontières. “Cold‑chain solutions for Ebola vaccination in eastern DR Congo.” 2023.<br> [4] Local Health Report. “Community perceptions of Ebola vaccine in North Kivu.” 2023.<br> [5] Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance. “Funding for Ebola vaccine procurement and cold‑chain infrastructure.” 2023.</p>
