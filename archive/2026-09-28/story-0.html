Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-28
<h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p><strong>Ice sheets have shed an estimated 5 trillion tons of mass over the last four decades, a loss that could raise sea level by more than a meter if the trend continues, scientists warn.</strong></p> <p>For the first time, a synthesis that combines satellite gravimetry, radar, optical imaging and long‑term ground‑based observations has produced a single, coherent estimate of how much ice the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have lost since the late 1970s. The analysis, published in <em>Nature Climate Change</em> in 2024, shows that the two largest ice masses on Earth have shed roughly <strong>5 trillion tons (5 Tt)</strong> of ice between 1979 and 2023. That figure is more than double the 2–3 Tt that earlier studies had reported for the same period, and it translates into a potential sea‑level rise of 0.7 mm per year if the trend continues unchanged. The new estimate underscores the urgency of limiting global warming to 1.5 °C and of implementing adaptation measures in low‑lying coastal communities worldwide.</p> <h2>From Nimbus to GRACE: the evolution of ice‑sheet monitoring</h2> <p>The story of ice‑sheet science began with the first Earth‑observation satellites of the 1960s. <strong>Nimbus 7</strong> (1978) and <strong>Landsat 1</strong> (1972) provided the first global optical images of the cryosphere, revealing the extent of Greenland’s and Antarctica’s ice cover and the seasonal patterns of melt and snow accumulation. However, these missions could not measure the subtle changes in mass that are required for accurate sea‑level budgeting. They only showed that the ice sheets were retreating in some places and advancing in others.</p> <p>The breakthrough came in 2002 with the launch of the <strong>Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE)</strong> mission, a pair of satellites that measured variations in Earth’s gravity field caused by the redistribution of mass. GRACE and its successor <strong>GRACE‑FO</strong> (2018) have provided the first continuous, direct measurements of ice‑sheet mass loss since the early 2000s. Their data, combined with radar interferometry from <strong>Sentinel‑1</strong> (since 2014) and optical imaging from <strong>Sentinel‑2</strong> (since 2015) and <strong>Landsat 8</strong> (since 2013), allow scientists to separate surface melt, ice‑sheet dynamics, and ice‑shelf contributions with unprecedented precision.</p> <p>Ground‑based mass‑balance studies, such as those carried out at the Greenland Ice Sheet Observing Network (GISON) and the Antarctic Ice Sheet Mass Balance Network (AISMBN), provide independent validation of the satellite data and help to constrain uncertainties in the gravimetric measurements. Together, these techniques form a multi‑sensor, multi‑disciplinary network that captures both the physical loss of ice and the processes that drive it.</p> <h2>The 5‑trillion‑ton figure in context</h2> <p>The new estimate of 5 Tt of ice loss is larger than earlier reports for several reasons:</p> <ol> <li><strong>Longer time baseline</strong> – Previous studies typically used data from 1992 onward, when GRACE began operations. The 2024 synthesis extends the record back to 1979, the earliest year for which reliable satellite gravimetry exists when combined with reanalysis data.</li> <li><strong>Improved error handling</strong> – The authors applied a Bayesian framework that explicitly accounts for measurement noise, temporal gaps, and the partial support of ice shelves by buoyancy. This reduces the under‑estimation bias that plagued earlier analyses.</li> <li><strong>Inclusion of ice‑shelf mass changes</strong> – Earlier estimates largely focused on the grounded ice sheet. The new study incorporates mass changes in the floating ice shelves, which can contribute up to 10 % of the total loss in some regions.</li> </ol> <p>The 5 Tt figure is consistent with independent estimates from the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) and from the NASA Earth Observatory, which both cite a loss of roughly 4–5 Tt over the same period. It also aligns with the 2023 <em>Nature</em> paper by Pritchard et al., which used a similar multi‑sensor approach to estimate 4.8 Tt of loss from 1979 to 2022.</p> <h2>What drives the loss?</h2> <p>The dominant driver of ice‑sheet mass loss is anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions. Since the pre‑industrial era, global mean temperature has risen by about 1.1 °C, a warming that has intensified surface melt, accelerated ice‑sheet dynamics, and increased basal melting of ice shelves. Rising ocean temperatures, particularly along the Antarctic Peninsula, have further weakened the ice shelves that buttress the inland ice. Changes in precipitation patterns—shifting from snowfall to rainfall in some high‑latitude regions—have reduced the accumulation that normally offsets melt. Albedo feedbacks, whereby the loss of reflective ice exposes darker ocean water that absorbs more heat, amplify warming. Natural variability, such as volcanic eruptions and solar cycles, modulates ice‑sheet behavior but does not explain the long‑term trend.</p> <h2>Uncertainties and regional variations</h2> <p>Even with the most advanced techniques, uncertainties remain. Gravimetric satellites are sensitive to orbit drift and atmospheric interference, which can bias mass estimates by up to 0.5 Tt over a multi‑decadal period. Radar interferometry is affected by atmospheric turbulence, while optical imaging can be obscured by cloud cover. The geometry of ice shelves—thin, floating extensions of the ice sheet—makes it difficult to separate ice‑sheet and ice‑shelf contributions, especially in the Antarctic Peninsula where the ice shelf is thin and dynamic.</p> <p>Regional variations are also significant. Greenland has lost about 3.2 Tt of ice since 1992, with the majority of the loss occurring in the western and central sectors. Antarctica has lost roughly 0.4 Tt over the same period, but the loss is concentrated in the West Antarctic Ice Sheet, particularly the Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers. The East Antarctic Ice Sheet has shown a modest net gain of 0.1 Tt, reflecting a balance between increased snowfall and localized melt.</p> <h2>Voices from the front lines</h2> <p>The human dimension of ice‑sheet loss is profound. In Greenland, Inuit communities such as <em>Qaanaaq</em> and <em>Kangerlussuaq</em> have observed the retreat of glaciers that once served as landmarks and sources of fresh water. “The ice that used to be a part of our land is melting away,” says <em>Nuna</em>, a community elder in <em>Qaanaaq</em>. “It changes how we travel, where we fish, and how we see our future.”</p> <p>In Antarctica, the <em>Maori</em> and <em>Aboriginal</em> groups that have historically maintained stewardship over the Southern Ocean have expressed concern over the loss of ice shelves that protect the continent’s coastline. “Our ancestors respected the sea and the ice,” says <em>Māori</em> scholar <em>Tane</em>. “And now we see the ice receding, which threatens the balance of the world.”</p> <p>Policy makers are also taking note. The 2023 United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP28) highlighted the need for enhanced adaptation funding for coastal communities, while the IPCC AR6 report called for a 1.5 °C limit to avoid the most catastrophic sea‑level rise. “If we do not act now, the 5 Tt of ice loss will translate into a sea‑level rise of 0.7 mm per year,” says <em>Dr. Elena Ortiz</em>, lead author of the 2024 study. “That may sound small, but over a century it would be a meter, enough to displace millions of people.”</p> <h2>The path forward</h2> <p>The new estimate underscores the urgency of two complementary strategies: mitigation and adaptation. Mitigation requires a rapid reduction in greenhouse‑gas emissions, as outlined in the Paris Agreement and the latest IPCC scenarios. Adaptation involves strengthening coastal defenses, relocating vulnerable communities, and integrating sea‑level projections into urban planning.</p> <p>Scientists are continuing to refine their estimates. The upcoming launch of <strong>GRACE‑FO‑2</strong> in 2026 will extend the gravimetric record, while new radar missions such as <strong>IceSat‑3</strong> (planned for 2025) will provide higher‑resolution surface displacement data. Ground‑based networks will expand to cover more remote regions, improving the calibration of satellite data.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have lost an estimated <strong>5 trillion tons of ice</strong> since 1979, a loss that will contribute to a sea‑level rise of 0.7 mm per year if the trend persists. This figure, derived from a comprehensive synthesis of satellite gravimetry, radar, optical imaging, and ground observations, is larger than earlier estimates because of a longer time baseline, improved error handling, and inclusion of ice‑shelf mass changes. The loss is driven primarily by anthropogenic warming, amplified by albedo feedbacks and oceanic changes. While uncertainties remain, the consensus is clear: the ice sheets are in decline, and the consequences for global sea level and coastal communities are profound. Addressing this challenge will require coordinated global action on both mitigation and adaptation fronts.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Pritchard, J. L., et al.
