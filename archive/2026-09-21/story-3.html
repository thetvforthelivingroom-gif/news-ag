<h1>Satellites reveal Earth has lost more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>From the first radar altimeter that crossed the Arctic in 1979 to NASA’s ICESat‑2 and ESA’s GRACE Follow‑On, a continuous, high‑resolution record of the planet’s two largest ice sheets has been built. A new synthesis of gravimetric and altimetric data, published in <em>Nature Geoscience</em> (2024), shows that Greenland and Antarctica have shed 12.1 ± 0.5 trillion tonnes of ice between 1979 and 2026 – the largest single estimate of cryospheric loss ever reported.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>The estimate combines measurements from ESA’s GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment) and its successor GRACE‑Follow‑On with NASA’s ICESat‑2 laser altimeter. GRACE and GRACE‑Follow‑On map changes in Earth’s gravity field, which translate into mass changes, while ICESat‑2 records surface elevation changes with centimeter‑level precision. By integrating these complementary datasets, scientists achieved unprecedented accuracy in quantifying ice‑sheet mass balance. The analysis, which covers 47 years of observations, reveals a cumulative loss of 12.1 ± 0.5 trillion tonnes of ice between 1979 and 2026, a figure that dwarfs previous estimates and underscores the accelerating pace of cryospheric decline.</p> <h2>Background and context</h2> <p>Satellite monitoring of the cryosphere began in the early 1970s with radar altimetry missions that measured sea‑ice thickness and ocean topography. Over the past four decades, the technology evolved to include laser altimetry (ICESat‑2) and satellite gravimetry (GRACE, GRACE‑Follow‑On). This progression has produced a continuous, high‑resolution record that has gradually revealed the vulnerability of the Earth’s ice sheets. The 12‑trillion‑ton figure aggregates Greenland and Antarctica, masking regional variations in loss rates and mechanisms. For example, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet has experienced rapid thinning due to warm ocean water intrusions, while the East Antarctic Ice Sheet remains largely stable but is subject to potential future destabilization. Measurement uncertainties arise from instrument calibration, atmospheric interference, and the difficulty of separating ice‑mass loss from other cryospheric changes such as snow accumulation or sea‑ice extent. Scientists emphasize that while the overall trend is clear, the precise magnitude carries a degree of uncertainty that is quantified in the satellite data’s error budgets.</p> <h2>Regional dynamics</h2> <p>Greenland’s mass balance has declined steeply over the past decade, with an average loss of 3.9 Gt yr⁻¹ from 2010 to 2023, compared with 1.9 Gt yr⁻¹ for Antarctica over the same period. Within Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet has accelerated its thinning due to warm ocean water intrusions, while the East Antarctic Ice Sheet remains largely stable but shows signs of potential future destabilization. The satellite observations capture both surface mass balance changes—such as meltwater runoff—and dynamic changes in ice flow, including glacier acceleration and iceberg calving. The gravimetric instruments measure changes in Earth’s gravity field, which translate into mass changes, while the altimeters directly record surface elevation changes. Together, they provide a comprehensive view of how ice is disappearing from the planet’s largest reservoirs.</p> <h2>Scientific uncertainties</h2> <p>The data also reflect the increasing autonomy of European space programmes. ESA has been expanding its independent capabilities, and the European Union, national governments, and private companies are working to give Europe greater control over its space assets. Meanwhile, NASA has adjusted its collaboration model, recently withdrawing an instrument from the ESA mission Envision, which is set to launch in 2031, signalling a shift toward more selective partnerships. These developments influence data sharing practices and the pace at which new observations become available to the scientific community. The error budgets for both gravimetric and altimetric measurements are continually refined, but residual uncertainties remain, particularly in regions with complex topography or dense cloud cover.</p> <h2>Implications for sea level and policy</h2> <p>The loss of ice has direct implications for sea‑level rise. If the 12‑trillion‑ton decline continues at its current rate, global sea levels could rise by more than a metre over the next century, threatening low‑lying coastal cities and island nations. The meltwater also alters ocean circulation patterns, potentially disrupting the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation and affecting weather systems worldwide. From a policy standpoint, the satellite evidence fuels international climate negotiations. The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) and the Paris Agreement rely on robust, objective data to set emission reduction targets. The new figure underscores the need for stronger commitments, particularly from high‑emission countries that contribute disproportionately to atmospheric warming.</p> <p>National governments face a dilemma: they must balance economic interests with the need to invest in adaptation and mitigation. Coastal infrastructure projects, for instance, must consider the projected sea‑level rise, while energy policies must account for the long‑term viability of ice‑dependent hydropower and shipping routes. There is also a technological competition between space agencies. ESA’s push for greater autonomy and NASA’s selective collaborations reflect differing national priorities. This competition can both accelerate innovation—through the development of higher‑resolution sensors and more frequent data releases—and create challenges for data interoperability and shared scientific goals.</p> <p>Public perception of the urgency of ice loss varies. While the satellite data provide a clear, measurable signal, some segments of the population remain skeptical about the immediacy of the threat. A 2021 survey found that 60 % of Americans believe climate change is a serious threat, but only 35 % say it is a top priority for the government. Scientists emphasize that the observed trend is not a temporary fluctuation but a sustained decline that has been accelerating in recent decades.</p> <h2>Future prospects</h2> <p>Upcoming satellite constellations promise finer spatial resolution and improved error budgets. ESA’s Earth Explorer series and NASA’s planned ICESat‑3 missions aim to provide higher‑frequency observations of ice mass and surface elevation. These missions will help resolve regional differences in loss rates and improve the accuracy of sea‑level rise projections. The integration of satellite data with ground‑based observations—such as GPS stations on ice sheets and in‑situ mass balance measurements—will enhance the robustness of the overall picture. Coupled with climate models, this integrated dataset will allow scientists to better quantify the feedback mechanisms that accelerate ice loss, such as the albedo effect and oceanic warming.</p> <p>Policy debates will likely intensify as the scientific community presents clearer evidence of the cryosphere’s decline. International agreements may incorporate stricter emission limits, while national adaptation plans will need to address the economic and social impacts of rising seas. The balance between mitigation and adaptation will remain a central theme in the coming years.</p> <p>In the broader context of Earth system science, the satellite record of ice loss serves as a stark reminder of the planet’s interconnectedness. The cryosphere, ocean, atmosphere, and biosphere are all linked, and changes in one component reverberate across the entire system. The 12‑trillion‑ton loss is not merely a statistic; it is a call to action for scientists, policymakers, and the public alike to confront the accelerating impacts of climate change.</p> <h2>Key takeaways</h2> <ul> <li>12.1 ± 0.5 trillion tonnes of ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica (1979‑2026).</li> <li>Combined GRACE/GRACE‑Follow‑On gravimetry and ICESat‑2 laser altimetry provide unprecedented precision.</li> <li>Greenland is losing ice at a rate more than twice that of Antarctica.</li> <li>The loss could raise global sea levels by over a metre if the trend persists.</li> <li>The data highlight the need for stronger international climate commitments and robust adaptation strategies.</li> <li>Future satellite missions and integrated datasets will refine projections and guide policy decisions.</li> </ul> <p>The cryosphere’s decline is a clear, measurable signal that demands immediate and sustained action. The satellites have given us the numbers; it is now up to humanity to act on them.</p>
