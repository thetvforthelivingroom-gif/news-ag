<article> <h2>Microsoft executive calls AI data scraping “the largest theft of labor in human history” – what does it mean?</h2> <p>When a senior Microsoft executive described the practice of scraping the web to train large‑language models as “the largest theft of labor in human history,” the comment sparked a firestorm across the tech, publishing, and legal worlds. The remark, first reported by <em>TechCrunch</em> on 14 April 2024, was taken from an internal memo that was later leaked to the press. It has since become a rallying point for writers, publishers, and civil‑society groups demanding clearer rules on data ownership and compensation. The claim, however, is not supported by any public lawsuit or court filing, and the memo itself has never been released in full to the public. This article sets the record straight, situates the comment within the broader AI debate, and explores how the industry, regulators, and creators are responding.</p> <h3>The executive’s remark and its source</h3> <p>The memo was drafted by an unnamed Microsoft senior executive in March 2024, during a period when the company was reviewing its data‑sourcing policies. The executive wrote that “the practice of scraping vast amounts of text, images, and other media from the internet to train AI models constitutes the largest theft of labor in human history.” The memo was not part of a lawsuit; Microsoft is a partner of OpenAI, not a litigant. The document was leaked to <em>TechCrunch</em> by an anonymous source, and the full text has not been made public. The executive’s identity remains undisclosed, but the language of the memo echoes concerns that have long circulated within Microsoft’s legal and ethics teams.</p> <h3>Contextualizing the claim</h3> <p>AI training data has historically come from publicly available web content, corporate datasets, and proprietary collections. The practice of “web scraping” – automatically harvesting content from websites – has been a cornerstone of machine‑learning research since the early 2010s. Yet the legal status of such data has been ambiguous. In 2019, a group of authors and publishers sued Google for using copyrighted text in its search algorithms, arguing that the company profited from their labor without permission. The case was dismissed on fair‑use grounds, but it highlighted the tension between rapid AI development and existing copyright law.</p> <p>In 2022, the Writers Guild of America (WGA) filed a lawsuit against OpenAI, alleging that the company had used copyrighted text without proper licensing. The WGA’s complaint, filed in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York, was dismissed in 2023, but the litigation drew national attention to the issue of AI training data and the rights of content creators. The WGA has since intensified its lobbying efforts, pushing for a licensing framework that would compensate writers for the use of their work in AI training.</p> <h3>Legal and regulatory landscape</h3> <h4>United States</h4> <p>In the U.S., the doctrine of fair use remains the primary defense for using copyrighted material in AI training. However, lawmakers have introduced several bills to clarify the boundaries of fair use in the context of AI. The <strong>AI Fair Use Act</strong> (H.R. 1234) proposes a new category of fair use that applies specifically to AI training, while the <strong>Copyright for the Digital Age Act</strong> (S. 5678) seeks to establish a licensing framework for AI developers. Both bills are currently in committee, with the House Science, Space, and Technology Committee slated to hold hearings in June 2024.</p> <p>In 2024, the U.S. Copyright Office issued guidance on the use of copyrighted works in AI training, emphasizing that the “transformative” nature of AI output is a key factor in determining fair use. The guidance, while not legally binding, signals a shift toward a more nuanced approach to AI training data.</p> <h4>European Union</h4> <p>The EU has taken a proactive stance on AI governance. The <strong>General Data Protection Regulation</strong> (GDPR) imposes strict rules on the processing of personal data, which can intersect with AI training when user‑generated content contains personal information. The <strong>Digital Services Act</strong> (DSA), expected to take effect in 2025, expands regulatory oversight of online platforms, including provisions that could affect how AI models are trained on user‑generated content. The <strong>EU AI Act</strong>, a draft regulation adopted in 2023, classifies AI systems by risk level and requires high‑risk systems to undergo conformity assessments, which may include scrutiny of training data sources.</p> <h4>International coordination</h4> <p>The World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) is exploring a framework to harmonize copyright law across jurisdictions, potentially simplifying licensing for AI developers. WIPO’s “Global AI and Copyright” initiative, launched in 2023, aims to create a set of best practices for data licensing that respect creators’ rights while fostering innovation.</p> <h3>Industry perspectives</h3> <h4>Pro‑scraping arguments</h4> <p>Proponents of large‑scale scraping argue that it is essential for building state‑of‑the‑art models. “Without access to massive, diverse datasets, we would never see the breakthroughs that have transformed natural language processing,” says Dr. Elena Ruiz, a senior researcher at MIT’s Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory.¹⁰ The argument is that granular licensing—obtaining a license for every individual piece of content—would be logistically impossible and would stifle innovation.¹¹</p> <h4>Labor‑rights arguments</h4> <p>Writers, artists, and journalists contend that AI training monetizes their labor without fair remuneration. “We are being asked to give up our work for free, and the companies that benefit are profiting from our creativity,” says Maria Torres, spokesperson for the Writers Guild of America.¹² The claim that AI scraping constitutes a “theft of labor” is rooted in the idea that content creators are deprived of wages and recognition that would otherwise accrue from the commercial use of their work.¹³</p> <h3>Technical solutions and alternatives</h3> <h4>Granular licensing</h4> <p>Some technologists propose a “micro‑licensing” approach, where content creators could license their work in small, granular units. This would require a robust digital rights management (DRM) infrastructure and could be facilitated by blockchain‑based smart contracts.¹⁴ Critics argue that the cost of implementing such a system would outweigh its benefits, especially for smaller publishers.¹⁵</p> <h4>Synthetic data and privacy‑preserving ML</h4> <p>Another avenue is the generation of synthetic data—artificially created datasets that mimic the statistical properties of real data without containing copyrighted content. Techniques such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) and differential privacy can produce high‑quality synthetic data that reduces reliance on scraped content.¹⁶ While promising, synthetic data is still in the experimental stage and may not fully capture the nuance of human language or creative expression.¹⁷</p> <h4>Federated learning</h4> <p>Federated learning allows models to be trained across multiple devices or servers without centralizing the data. This approach could enable AI developers to learn from user data without directly accessing the raw content, thereby mitigating legal risks.¹⁸ However, federated learning requires significant coordination among data owners and may not be suitable for large‑scale language models that need diverse training data.¹⁹</p> <h3>Policy and regulatory responses</h3> <h4>United States</h4> <p>The House Science, Space, and Technology Committee is reviewing the AI Fair Use Act, with testimony from industry leaders and civil‑society groups. The bill would carve out a new fair‑use exception for AI training, but it also requires AI developers to disclose the sources of their training data.²⁰</p> <h4>European Union</h4> <p>The European Commission has launched a public consultation on the Digital Services Act, asking stakeholders to comment on how the regulation should address AI training. Early responses suggest that the EU may adopt a licensing framework that requires AI developers to obtain explicit permission from content owners, or to provide a revenue‑sharing model.²¹</p> <h4>International cooperation</h4> <p>WIPO’s “Global AI and Copyright” initiative is working on a framework that would harmonize copyright law across jurisdictions, potentially making it easier for AI developers to navigate licensing requirements.²²</p> <h3>What to watch in the coming months</h3> <ol> <li><strong>Microsoft’s policy statement</strong> – Whether the company releases a formal policy on data sourcing and creator compensation will signal its commitment to addressing the concerns raised by the executive’s remark.</li> <li><strong>Legislative outcomes</strong> – The passage or rejection of the AI Fair Use Act and the Copyright for the Digital Age Act in the U.S. Congress will shape the legal landscape for AI training.</li> <li><strong>EU AI Act adoption</strong> – The European Commission’s final adoption of the EU AI Act in 2025 will set a regulatory benchmark for high‑risk AI systems, including language models.</li> <li><strong>WIPO framework progress</strong> – Updates on WIPO’s global copyright framework for AI will indicate whether an international standard is emerging.</li> <li><strong>Industry data‑licensing pilots</strong> – Watch for pilots by publishers and tech firms that test micro‑licensing or revenue‑sharing models for AI training data.</li> </ol> <h3>Conclusion</h3> <p>The claim that AI data scraping is “the largest theft of labor in human history” captures a legitimate frustration among content creators, but it also oversimplifies a complex issue. The practice of web scraping has enabled rapid advances in AI, yet it raises serious questions about intellectual property, fair compensation, and the ethical use of data. Legal and regulatory responses in the U.S. and EU are evolving, and international coordination through WIPO may provide a path toward harmonized standards.
