SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-09
SpaceX’s Starship prototype SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica on a clear Texas morning, reaching an altitude of roughly 150 km before an onboard propulsion anomaly triggered an automated abort. The vehicle re‑entered the atmosphere and splashed down in the Gulf of Mexico, ending the flight in just over two minutes. Although the ascent reached the altitude that signals the vehicle has achieved orbital velocity, the abort prevented a true orbital insertion, marking another setback in SpaceX’s rapid‑development program. The event has reignited questions about the timeline for lunar missions and the future of U.S. space dominance.

What happened

SN20’s launch on 18 March 2024 was the first time a Starship prototype climbed to the 150‑km threshold that indicates the vehicle has reached the speed needed to stay in orbit. However, a loss of thrust in the upper‑stage engine caused the vehicle to abort before it could complete the circularization burn required for a stable orbit. The flight lasted 2 min 14 s from liftoff to splashdown, a fraction of the planned 30‑minute orbital insertion sequence. SpaceX’s chief engineer, Elon Musk, clarified that the anomaly was isolated to a single engine and that the vehicle’s design would be updated for the next test [1].

The abort was the most recent in a series of high‑profile failures that have punctuated Starship’s development. Earlier orbital attempts, such as SN15 in 2023, also reached 150 km but were terminated early due to engine malfunctions [2]. Each failure has provided data that SpaceX uses to refine the vehicle’s heat‑shield, propulsion control software, and structural design.

Background and context

Starship is the centerpiece of SpaceX’s vision for a fully reusable, super‑heavy launch system capable of carrying humans to the Moon, Mars, and beyond. The vehicle’s development has been driven by an aggressive schedule set by CEO Elon Musk, who has repeatedly emphasized the need for rapid iteration to achieve cost advantages over existing heavy‑lift systems. Since the first suborbital test in 2019, SpaceX has flown 20 prototypes (SN1–SN20), each iteration pushing the envelope of size, thrust, and heat‑shield technology. The SN8–SN15 series suffered a series of high‑profile failures, but each failure provided data that refined the design [3].

The 2023 SN15 orbital test was the first to reach the 150‑km threshold, a critical altitude that indicates the vehicle has achieved orbital velocity. However, the test was cut short when an engine anomaly halted the ascent. That experience informed the design changes implemented for SN20, including a redesigned heat shield and updated propulsion control software.

SpaceX’s ambition has always been to outpace competitors such as United Launch Alliance (ULA) and Blue Origin. In the commercial launch market, ULA’s Atlas V and Delta IV remain the dominant heavy‑lift vehicles, while Blue Origin’s New Glenn is still in development. A successful Starship orbit would have positioned SpaceX as the sole provider of a fully reusable super‑heavy launch system, dramatically reducing launch costs and opening new markets for satellite operators and space‑tourism investors [4].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

NASA and the Artemis program

NASA’s Artemis program, which aims to return humans to the Moon by 2025, has partnered with SpaceX to launch the Space Launch System (SLS) and to develop the lunar lander. Starship’s success is seen as a potential game‑changer for Artemis, offering a cheaper, more flexible launch option for lunar payloads. The early abort, however, raises concerns about whether SpaceX can meet the Artemis schedule. NASA officials have expressed cautious optimism, noting that the company is still refining the vehicle and that the anomaly was isolated to a single engine [5].

FAA and regulatory oversight

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and NASA jointly oversee launch safety in the United States. The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation (AST) reviews each launch license, while NASA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation (OCST) provides additional oversight for missions that involve U.S. government payloads. The recent abort has prompted a review of the safety protocols and risk‑mitigation strategies employed by SpaceX. FAA officials have indicated that they will examine whether the rapid iteration cycle compromises safety standards and whether additional testing is warranted before the next orbital attempt [6].

Investor sentiment and the commercial market

Space tourism investors and satellite operators have watched SpaceX’s progress closely, betting on the company’s ability to deliver rapid, low‑cost access to orbit. The abort has introduced a degree of uncertainty. Some investors have paused new funding rounds, while others view the incident as a normal part of the learning curve for a groundbreaking technology. Analysts suggest that the commercial launch market will remain competitive, with ULA and Blue Origin poised to capture market share if SpaceX’s timeline stalls. Yet the potential cost advantage—estimated at roughly $55 million per launch for Starship according to SpaceX’s own cost model—remains a strong selling point for future customers [7].

Competitive advantage for ULA and Blue Origin

While SpaceX’s rapid development has kept it in the spotlight, the early abort underscores the risks of a high‑speed program. ULA’s Atlas V and Delta IV have a proven track record of reliability, and Blue Origin’s New Glenn, once launched, will offer a reusable heavy‑lift option. The market may see a shift in customer preference toward these more conservative options if SpaceX’s timeline extends. Conversely, if SpaceX resolves the anomaly and successfully completes an orbital flight, it could regain its competitive edge and potentially undercut the cost of ULA and Blue Origin launches by a significant margin [8].

What to watch (future implications)

Next flight schedule and technical fixes

SpaceX has announced that the next Starship prototype will incorporate a redesigned heat shield and updated propulsion control software. The company aims to conduct another orbital test within the next six months, pending FAA approval. The timeline will be closely monitored by NASA, the FAA, and the commercial launch community. A successful flight would validate the vehicle’s design and restore confidence in the company’s rapid‑development model.

Impact on Artemis and lunar exploration

If Starship can achieve a reliable orbital insertion, NASA could use it as an alternative launch vehicle for Artemis payloads, potentially accelerating the program’s schedule and reducing costs. However, any delay or additional anomalies could push back the Artemis timeline, forcing NASA to rely more heavily on the SLS and other heavy‑lift options. The outcome will influence international partners such as the European Space Agency (ESA) and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA), who are also planning lunar missions.

Commercial launch market dynamics

The abort has highlighted the importance of safety and reliability in the commercial launch market. Investors and customers will weigh the risk of a high‑speed program against the potential cost savings. If SpaceX can demonstrate a robust safety record, it may attract new satellite operators and space‑tourism ventures. Conversely, a prolonged delay could shift market share to ULA, Blue Origin, or emerging players such as Rocket Lab.

Regulatory implications

The FAA and NASA may revise their oversight procedures to accommodate the rapid iteration cycle of companies like SpaceX. This could involve more stringent pre‑flight testing, additional safety margins, or new certification processes for reusable launch vehicles. The balance between fostering innovation and ensuring safety will be a key policy discussion in the coming months.

In sum, SpaceX’s latest attempt at an orbital flight—though short‑lived—marks a significant milestone in the company’s quest to revolutionize space access. The propulsion anomaly that forced an early abort underscores the technical challenges that remain, but it also provides valuable data that will shape the next generation of Starship prototypes. The outcome of the upcoming flight will reverberate through NASA’s Artemis program, the commercial launch market, and the broader policy landscape that governs spaceflight in the United States.
