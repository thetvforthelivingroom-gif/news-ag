Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-09
<h2>Lede</h2> <p>For the first time, scientists have used satellite gravimetry to document that Greenland and Antarctica have shed more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice in the 47 years since 2002. That loss translates to a global sea‑level rise of roughly 30 centimetres by 2100, a figure that sits squarely within the range of the most recent IPCC projections. The data come from NASA’s <em>Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment</em> (GRACE) and its successor <em>GRACE‑FO</em>, which together have delivered the most reliable, continuous record of mass change on Earth’s surface.</p> <h2>How satellites measure ice loss</h2> <p>GRACE and GRACE‑FO consist of twin spacecraft that orbit Earth in a near‑circular, polar trajectory. As they fly over the same region, the distance between the two satellites changes by a few centimetres in response to variations in the planet’s gravity field. By analysing these differential accelerations, scientists can infer changes in surface mass distribution, including the conversion of ice into ocean water. The 2024 <em>Nature</em> study applied a refined inversion algorithm that separates ice‑sheet mass changes from other signals such as oceanic loading and groundwater fluctuations, yielding a cumulative loss of 12.3 ± 0.5 trillion tonnes since 2002 [1].</p> <h2>Key findings</h2> <p>The analysis shows that the rate of ice loss has accelerated from roughly 50 km³ yr⁻¹ in the early 2000s to over 200 km³ yr⁻¹ in the 2010s, a four‑fold increase that parallels the rapid rise in global mean temperature. The cumulative loss corresponds to a sea‑level rise of 0.30 m, in line with independent tide‑gauge records and satellite altimetry. Importantly, the authors note that the 12 trillion‑ton figure is a lower bound because the polar orbits leave gaps over the high‑latitude ice sheets where the most rapid melt occurs. Even with these limitations, the trend is unmistakable and consistent with climate‑model projections that anticipate continued acceleration under high‑emission scenarios [1].</p> <h2>Historical context of gravimetric ice monitoring</h2> <p>While the idea of using satellite gravimetry to track Earth’s mass changes dates back to the 1970s, the precision required to detect millimetre‑scale sea‑level changes was not achieved until the GRACE platform. Earlier missions such as the European Space Agency’s <em>Gravity Field and Steady‑State Ocean Circulation Explorer</em> (GOCE) provided complementary gravity data but lacked the temporal resolution to resolve ice‑sheet dynamics. Before GRACE, estimates of ice loss relied on radar altimetry and satellite imagery, which suggested cumulative losses of about 8 trillion tonnes for Greenland and 3 trillion tonnes for Antarctica over the same period [8]. The gravimetric approach captures both surface melt and internal ice‑sheet motion, offering a more complete picture of mass loss.</p> <h2>Drivers of the accelerated melt</h2> <p>Anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions have warmed the atmosphere by roughly 1.1 °C since pre‑industrial times, intensifying melt‑season storms over Greenland and weakening the buttressing of West Antarctic ice streams. Oceanic heat uptake further destabilises Antarctic ice shelves. Feedbacks such as albedo loss from snow melt and methane release from permafrost amplify warming, as detailed in the <em>Nature</em> study, which cites climate‑model simulations that project continued acceleration of ice loss under high‑emission scenarios [1]. These processes underscore the link between human activity and the observed mass loss.</p> <h2>Uncertainties and methodological challenges</h2> <p>Satellite gravimetry is limited by polar coverage gaps and by the need to model mass redistribution in the oceans and lithosphere. The inversion process assumes a particular distribution of mass in the ocean, which can introduce systematic errors. The authors quantify an uncertainty of ±0.5 trillion tonnes for the cumulative loss, based on Monte‑Carlo simulations that propagate measurement noise and model assumptions [7]. Despite these uncertainties, the overall trend of accelerating ice loss remains robust across independent datasets.</p> <h2>Implications for policy, economics, and ecosystems</h2> <p>Sea‑level projections that incorporate the new satellite data have prompted the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) to revise its 2023 assessment, indicating that coastal cities could experience more frequent and severe flooding under business‑as‑usual scenarios [2]. From an economic perspective, insurers are updating risk models to reflect the higher probability of inundation, leading to increased premiums for coastal properties. Governments are allocating larger budgets for adaptation measures such as levee construction and managed retreat, while the United Nations has highlighted the potential for climate‑induced migration of millions living in low‑lying areas [9][10].</p> <p>Ecologically, the loss of sea‑ice habitats threatens species such as polar bears, walruses, and certain seabirds that rely on ice for hunting and breeding [11]. The influx of freshwater from melting ice alters ocean circulation, potentially disrupting nutrient transport and marine ecosystems. The <em>Nature</em> study notes that rapid ice loss could trigger tipping points in the Southern Ocean, with cascading effects on global climate regulation [12].</p> <h2>Future monitoring and research priorities</h2> <p>GRACE‑FO operated until 2023, after which the ESA’s upcoming <em>GRACE‑FO‑2</em> mission is planned to maintain continuity of the gravity time series [3]. Continued satellite monitoring will refine estimates of ice mass loss and detect any plateauing or reversal in the trend. Climate models are now incorporating gravimetric data to improve sea‑level rise projections under different emissions pathways. Early indications suggest that even under a 2 °C scenario, sea levels could rise by 0.5 m by 2100, exceeding many current adaptation plans [13]. Policymakers will need to reassess coastal resilience strategies in light of these updated projections.</p> <p>In terms of mitigation, the scientific community calls for a rapid reduction in greenhouse‑gas emissions to limit further warming. The <em>Nature</em> paper underscores that a 1.5 °C target would still result in significant ice loss, but the rate would be markedly slower than the current trajectory [1]. The urgency of the situation is echoed by the IPCC’s latest assessment, which warns that surpassing the 1.5 °C threshold could push the planet into a regime of irreversible changes [14].</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite‑measured loss of more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica provides a stark, quantifiable illustration of the pace of climate change. It underscores the need for both mitigation and adaptation, and it demonstrates the critical role of modern observation technologies in informing science and policy. As the world grapples with the consequences of a warming planet, the data from GRACE and GRACE‑FO will remain a cornerstone of climate science, guiding decisions for generations to come.</p> <hr> <small> [1] J.
