Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-09
Over the past 47 years, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed roughly 6 trillion tonnes of ice, a loss that could raise global sea level by about 1.8 m. This figure, derived from a decade‑long series of satellite missions, confirms the accelerating pace of ice‑sheet decline and its potential to reshape coastlines worldwide. Earlier media reports that quoted a 12‑trillion‑tonne loss were a misinterpretation of a high‑emissions projection rather than a measurement; the consensus estimate from satellite data is 5.9 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes (Pritchard et al., 2021). The uncertainty band reflects remaining challenges in sensor calibration, atmospheric interference, and the treatment of glacial isostatic adjustment. The story of this loss begins in the early 1970s, when the first Earth‑observation satellites—such as Landsat and the Nimbus‑7 SMMR—started to map the planet’s surface and provide the first coarse estimates of ice‑sheet extent. Ground‑based mass‑balance studies in Greenland and Antarctica, using stakes, snow pits, and radar, began to reveal that the ice sheets were not static. By the 1990s, the launch of the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) in 2002 marked a watershed moment. GRACE measured subtle changes in Earth’s gravity field that correspond to mass loss from the polar ice sheets, providing the first direct, global, and continuous measurement of ice‑sheet mass change (Rignot et al., 2009). GRACE‑FO, its successor, extended the time series to 2020 and improved the spatial resolution of the gravity anomalies. Together, the two missions have been the backbone of gravimetric ice‑sheet monitoring, revealing a steady increase in mass loss that accelerated after 2000. In parallel, altimetric missions such as CryoSat‑2 (launched 2010) and ICESat‑2 (launched 2018) have measured the elevation of the ice sheets with centimeter‑level precision, allowing scientists to infer volume changes from surface height variations. Radiometric sensors, including the Advanced Microwave Scanning Radiometer (AMSR‑E) and the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS), have provided complementary data on surface temperature and meltwater distribution, helping to link surface processes to mass change. When researchers combined the gravimetric, altimetric, and radiometric datasets, they arrived at a consensus estimate of 5.9 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes of ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica between 1973 and 2020 (Pritchard et al., 2021). This figure is consistent with independent analyses that use different combinations of satellite data and ground‑based observations, such as the 5.5 ± 0.5 trillion‑ton estimate from Bamber et al. (2020) and the 5.8 ± 0.6 trillion‑ton estimate from Holland et al. (2020). The convergence of these studies underscores the robustness of the 6‑trillion‑ton figure, while the ± 0.4 trillion‑ton uncertainty band reflects the remaining challenges in sensor calibration, atmospheric interference, and the treatment of glacial isostatic adjustment. The loss is not uniform across the two ice sheets. Greenland’s mass loss has accelerated in the last decade, driven largely by surface melt and the accelerated calving of outlet glaciers such as Jakobshavn and Helheim (Mouginot et al., 2021). In Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet has thinned dramatically, and the Antarctic Peninsula’s glaciers have retreated at record rates, largely due to oceanic warming and atmospheric changes that enhance basal melt (Kopp et al., 2020). Together, these processes contribute to a net sea‑level rise that is now measurable in the millimeter‑per‑year range, a rate that has roughly doubled since the 1990s. The spatial heterogeneity of the loss also means that local sea‑level rise can differ by several centimeters from the global average, with low‑lying coastal communities facing disproportionate risks. The implications of a 6‑trillion‑ton loss extend far beyond the scientific community. A 1.8‑m rise in sea level would inundate millions of people, submerge critical infrastructure, and alter ocean circulation patterns that drive global climate. Coastal erosion, saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers, and increased storm surge frequency are already observable in places like Bangladesh, the Netherlands, and the Pacific Islands. Moreover, the loss of ice mass reduces the Earth’s albedo, amplifying the warming that drives further melt—a feedback loop that has become increasingly evident in recent decades. Looking ahead, the satellite record suggests that the rate of ice‑sheet loss will continue to accelerate unless global greenhouse‑gas emissions are curtailed. Climate models project that, under a high‑emissions scenario, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets could lose an additional 10–15 trillion tonnes by 2100, potentially raising sea level by 0.5–1.0 m (IPCC, 2023). Even under a moderate‑emissions pathway, the loss would be significant, underscoring the need for continued monitoring and robust mitigation strategies. New satellite missions, such as the upcoming GRACE‑FO‑Plus and the planned CryoSat‑3, will enhance spatial resolution and temporal coverage, allowing scientists to disentangle the relative contributions of surface melt, calving, and basal melt with greater precision. In conclusion, the 6‑trillion‑ton loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years is a stark reminder of the tangible impacts of climate change. The convergence of gravimetric, altimetric, and radiometric observations provides a compelling, data‑rich narrative of a world in transition. As the scientific community continues to refine its measurements and projections, policymakers and the public must translate these findings into concrete actions—reducing emissions, protecting vulnerable coastlines, and investing in resilient infrastructure—to safeguard the planet for future generations. The ice sheets are not merely distant, frozen reservoirs; they are dynamic, living indicators of our planet’s health, and their continued loss demands urgent, collective response.
