Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-09
<h1>Stocks Rise on Speculation of U.S.–China Trade Talks Ahead of Trump‑Xi Summit</h1> <p>On June 10, 2024, U.S. equity markets surged after Michael Bessent, Intel’s senior vice‑president of international business, told Reuters that preliminary talks with Chinese officials had been productive and that the parties had reached a “common understanding” on tariff relief for semiconductor components and potential joint research initiatives. The comment came just days before the scheduled Trump‑Xi summit in Washington, D.C., and sent a wave of optimism through the technology sector.</p> <p>In the early session, the Nasdaq Composite jumped 1.3 % to 13,450, while the S&P 500 gained 1.1 % to 4,210. The Russell 2000, a small‑cap benchmark, rose 1.4 % to 4,210. Semiconductor names led the rally: TSMC shares climbed 2.1 % to $105.60, Nvidia rose 1.9 % to $650.30, and AMD gained 1.7 % to $95.20. AI‑related stocks also benefited; Alphabet’s shares increased 1.5 % to $2,650, and Microsoft’s shares rose 1.4 % to $320. Biotech firms such as Moderna and BioNTech posted gains of 1.8 % and 1.6 % respectively, reflecting broader optimism about the high‑tech sector.</p> <p>Trading volume for the day hit $1.2 trillion, a 5 % increase over the previous week’s average of $1.14 trillion, according to Bloomberg data. The spike was driven largely by institutional investors and algorithmic trading systems that reacted to the headline about potential tariff relief.</p> <h2>What the Market Reaction Means</h2> <p>Investor sentiment has long been tied to the trajectory of U.S.–China trade policy. The sudden uptick in technology stocks suggests that market participants believe the U.S. may be moving toward a more open stance on semiconductor trade, a shift that could have ripple effects across the global supply chain. While the comment from Bessent is not a formal policy announcement, it is enough to influence expectations and drive short‑term price movements.</p> <h2>Context: The Evolution of U.S.–China Trade Tensions</h2> <p>Since 2018, the U.S. and China have been locked in a protracted trade dispute that began with the U.S. imposing 25 % tariffs on $34 billion of Chinese goods and 10 % tariffs on $16 billion of U.S. goods. China retaliated with counter‑tariffs on $28 billion of U.S. goods. The conflict escalated into a “technology war,” with the U.S. restricting exports of advanced semiconductor manufacturing equipment and AI hardware to China.</p> <p>In January 2020, the two sides signed the Phase One trade agreement, which reduced tariffs on $200 billion of U.S. goods and opened Chinese markets to U.S. agricultural products. The deal also required China to increase its purchases of U.S. goods by $200 billion over two years. However, the agreement did not address the technology restrictions imposed in 2020 and 2021, which remain in force.</p> <p>In 2021, the U.S. extended its export‑control restrictions to AI‑related hardware and software, citing national‑security concerns. These measures have reshaped the global semiconductor supply chain, forcing U.S. firms to seek domestic alternatives and prompting Chinese companies to look for new sources.</p> <p>More recently, in 2023, the U.S. announced additional tariffs on certain Chinese electronics and tightened enforcement of existing restrictions. The Biden administration has signaled a willingness to engage with China on trade issues, but the policy remains cautious.</p> <h2>Potential Tariff Relief</h2> <p>Bessent’s remarks imply that the U.S. might consider easing tariffs on semiconductor components that have been subject to duties since the 2018 trade war. The Phase One deal included a phased approach to tariff reduction, and a similar strategy could be applied to semiconductor imports. However, any tariff change would require formal negotiations, regulatory approvals, and compliance with the Trade Act of 1974.</p> <p>The U.S. Treasury’s Office of the Special Inspector General for Trade (SIGT) has indicated that it will scrutinize any changes to tariff policy to ensure they do not undermine national‑security interests. The Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security would also evaluate whether tariff adjustments could affect the U.S. export‑control regime.</p> <h2>Joint Research Ventures</h2> <p>In addition to tariff relief, Bessent hinted at potential joint research initiatives in semiconductor technology and artificial intelligence. While no specific projects have been announced, the technology sector has long sought collaboration across borders to accelerate innovation. A joint effort could involve shared research on next‑generation process nodes, advanced packaging, or AI algorithm development.</p> <p>Such collaboration would need to navigate U.S. export‑control regulations, which restrict the transfer of certain technologies to China. Chinese officials, meanwhile, have emphasized a desire for “mutual benefit” and “balanced technology trade.” The feasibility of joint research will depend on both sides’ willingness to reconcile security concerns with commercial interests.</p> <h2>Perspectives from Key Stakeholders</h2> <h3>U.S. Officials</h3> <p>On June 11, the U.S. Treasury’s Office of the Special Inspector General for Trade released a statement saying it was “monitoring developments in trade policy” and would “continue to enforce the Trade Act of 1974.” Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo tweeted that the U.S. “remains committed to fair and reciprocal trade practices” and that “any changes to tariff policy will be made in consultation with industry stakeholders.”</p> <h3>Chinese Officials</h3> <p>On June 12, a spokesperson for the Chinese Ministry of Commerce, Li Wei, issued a statement saying China “expects a constructive and mutually beneficial outcome” from the upcoming summit. Li emphasized that China “will continue to pursue a balanced approach to technology trade” and that it “remains open to cooperation in areas of shared interest.”</p> <h3>Industry Experts</h3> <p>Dr. Emily Chen, a semiconductor analyst at Gartner, noted that “the market’s reaction is consistent with a belief that the U.S. may be willing to ease some of the restrictions that have hampered the industry.” She added that “any tariff relief would need to be carefully calibrated to avoid undermining the national‑security rationale that underpins the current restrictions.”</p> <p>Professor David Liu, an international trade scholar at MIT, cautioned that “the U.S. and China have a long history of using trade policy as a tool of geopolitical strategy.” He argued that “while tariff relief could provide short‑term benefits to U.S. firms, it may also signal a shift in the broader U.S. trade policy that could have unintended consequences.”</p> <h3>Academic Voices</h3> <p>A recent paper published in the <em>Journal of International Economics</em> (June 2024) examined the impact of U.S. export‑control restrictions on the global semiconductor supply chain. The authors found that “the restrictions have led to a fragmentation of the supply chain, with U.S. firms increasingly relying on domestic suppliers and Chinese firms seeking alternative sources.” The paper suggests that any easing of restrictions could help restore some degree of integration, but also warns that it could expose U.S. firms to new security risks.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The June 10 rally in U.S. technology stocks reflects a broader market belief that the U.S. may be moving toward a more open stance on semiconductor trade with China. While Bessent’s comments are not a formal policy announcement, they carry weight because they come from a senior executive at one of the world’s largest semiconductor companies. The potential for tariff relief and joint research initiatives could reshape the competitive landscape, but any concrete policy shift will require careful navigation of national‑security concerns, regulatory approvals, and diplomatic negotiations.</p> <p>As the Trump‑Xi summit approaches, investors and policymakers alike will be watching closely for official statements that confirm or refute the market’s expectations. Whether the U.S. and China can find common ground on technology trade will have lasting implications for global supply chains, innovation, and the balance of economic power in the 21st century.</p>
