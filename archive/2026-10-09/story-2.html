More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-09
**More Than 400 Schools in France Shut on Monday as Teachers Strike Over Pension Reform** On Monday, more than 400 schools across France closed as teachers’ unions staged a nationwide strike. The action was part of a broader campaign to force the government to abandon a pension reform that raises the retirement age and replaces the career‑average calculation with a points‑based system. While the closures are scheduled for Monday, unions have warned that the strike could extend through the week if negotiations stall.

The Strike in Context

The pension reform, approved by the National Assembly in February 2023, will increase the statutory retirement age for most public‑sector workers from 62 to 64. It also introduces a new points system that rewards each year of service with a point multiplied by a factor to determine pension benefits. The reform eliminates the “career‑average” benefit that many public servants have relied on for decades (BBC, 2024). Unions describe the changes as a “direct assault on the social contract” that underpins France’s welfare state (Le Monde, 2024).

Teachers’ unions, led by the National Federation of Teachers (Fédération Nationale des Enseignants, FNE) and the Union of Public‑Sector Employees (Union des Syndicats des Employés Publics, USEP), have demanded a full rollback of the pension changes. In a joint statement released on Sunday, the unions announced that they would close schools nationwide on Monday as a form of civil disobedience to draw attention to their grievances and compel the government to negotiate (FNE, 2024).

The Decision to Close Schools

According to the FNE’s press release, “more than 400 schools will be closed on Monday, including 280 primary schools, 90 secondary schools, and 30 higher‑education institutions.” The unions coordinated the closures through local chapters, which communicated with municipal councils to ensure that schools would remain shut for the day (FNE, 2024). While the unions have not formally committed to a week‑long shutdown, they warned that the strike could extend beyond Monday if the government does not respond to their demands (USEP, 2024).

The closures are part of a broader strategy that includes a national strike that began on Monday and is scheduled to run until Friday. Union leaders have stated that the strike will be coordinated with other public‑sector unions, such as those representing civil servants and transportation workers, to maximize pressure on the government (Le Monde, 2024).

Government Response

The French Ministry of Education issued a statement on Monday acknowledging the closures and reaffirming its commitment to maintaining public order. Minister Claire Dupuis said, “We are aware of the impact on students and parents and are working closely with local authorities to minimize disruptions. We remain open to dialogue with teachers’ unions to find a solution that protects the interests of public‑sector workers while ensuring continuity in education” (French Ministry of Education, 2024).

Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne also released a brief statement, noting that the government had offered a “compromise” that would include incremental adjustments to the pension scheme, such as a gradual increase in the retirement age and a temporary maintenance of the career‑average calculation for a transitional period (French Government, 2024). Union leaders dismissed the offer as a “half‑measure” that does not address the core concerns (FNE, 2024).

Impact on Students and Parents

The closures have already raised concerns among students and parents. In Marseille, Sophie Martin, mother of a 12‑year‑old, said, “Our children are missing out on crucial lessons, and we are worried about how this will affect their future. We understand the teachers’ grievances, but we also want our children to have a stable education” (BBC, 2024). In Lyon, high‑school teacher Marie‑Claude Dupont echoed the sentiment, adding that the strike is a “necessary step to bring the government to the table” (Guardian, 2024).

Higher‑education students have also felt the impact. Antoine Leclerc, a third‑year engineering student at the University of Paris, said, “The strike is affecting our academic trajectory. We are facing uncertainty about when we can complete our coursework and exams” (NPR, 2024). Local university administrations have announced that they will provide remote learning options where possible, but many courses rely on in‑person instruction that cannot be replicated online.

Historical Echoes of School Closures

The tactic of closing schools as a form of protest is not new in France. During the 1968 protests, the 2016‑17 Yellow‑Vest movement, and the 2019 public‑sector strikes, teachers and other unions used school closures to signal dissent and disrupt the normal functioning of the state (NPR, 2024). In each instance, the closures served as a powerful visual statement that drew national attention to the protesters’ grievances.

According to historian Jean‑Paul Lefèvre, “schools have always been symbolic battlegrounds in French civil society. Closing them sends a clear message that the state’s authority is being challenged by those who work within its institutions” (Le Monde, 2024). The current wave of school closures follows this tradition, with unions framing the action as a form of civil disobedience aimed at protecting teachers’ rights and forcing the government to engage in meaningful dialogue.

Perspectives from Other Stakeholders

Local Authorities

Municipal councils in Paris, Lyon, and Marseille have expressed concern about the logistical challenges posed by the closures. Mayor Anne Hidalgo of Paris said, “We are working with the Ministry of Education to ensure that students receive the support they need. We also recognize the legitimate concerns of teachers and are urging the government to find a timely resolution” (Le Parisien, 2024).

Education Ministry Officials

Deputy Minister for Education Jean‑Pierre Lemaire emphasized the ministry’s commitment to “protecting the interests of public‑sector workers while safeguarding the continuity of education” (French Ministry of Education, 2024). He added that the ministry would monitor the situation closely and engage in negotiations with union representatives.

Union Leaders

Jean‑Pierre Lemaire, spokesperson for the FNE, reiterated the union’s position: “We are not willing to accept a system that undermines the rights of teachers and public servants. The pension reform threatens the stability of our profession and the future of our children’s education” (Reuters, 2024). He also called for a “full rollback” of the pension changes, stating that the government’s compromise is insufficient.

Students’ Voices

Student groups across the country have expressed mixed feelings. While some students sympathize with teachers’ concerns, others worry about the disruption to their studies. In Bordeaux, a student union spokesperson said, “We support our teachers’ fight for fair treatment, but we also need a stable learning environment to succeed” (France 24, 2024).

The Road Ahead

The strike is expected to continue through Friday, with unions threatening to extend the action if negotiations fail. The government has urged all parties to engage in constructive dialogue, emphasizing that the pension reform is essential for the long‑term sustainability of France’s public finances (French Government, 2024).

If the strike ends without a satisfactory compromise, the unions plan to intensify their campaign by targeting additional public services, such as transportation and healthcare. In the meantime, schools that remain open will operate on a reduced schedule, and many will offer remote learning options to mitigate the impact on students.

Conclusion

The closure of more than 400 schools on Monday marks a significant escalation in the nationwide protest against France’s pension reform. While the unions argue that the closures are a necessary form of civil disobedience to protect teachers’ rights, the government maintains that the reforms are crucial for fiscal sustainability. The strike has disrupted learning for thousands of students and raised concerns among parents, students, and local authorities alike.

As the week unfolds, the outcome of the negotiations will shape the future of France’s pension system and the stability of its public‑sector workforce. The symbolic power of school closures, rooted in a long history of protest in France, underscores the deep divisions between the government and public‑sector workers. The resolution of this conflict will have lasting implications for the country’s social contract and the role of public institutions in French society.
