More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-09
<h1>More than 400 schools in France shut on Monday as protests over pension reform and living costs intensify</h1> <p>On Monday, 24 April, 412 schools across France were closed, leaving roughly 200 000 students without a classroom and plunging the country into a sudden learning void. The shutdowns followed a wave of nationwide protests that have swept the nation since late 2023, driven by opposition to a controversial pension reform and rising living costs. The closures were authorised by prefects under the Ministry of Education’s authority, following a coordinated decision by teachers’ unions and local education authorities that has been used in previous French protest movements but has now taken on a new scale.</p> <h2>What Happened</h2> <p>In the early hours of 24 April, the doors of 412 schools—spanning primary, secondary, and some vocational institutions—were locked. A joint statement from the CFDT (Confédération Française Démocratique du Travail) and FO (Force Ouvrière) unions announced the closures, citing “the need to protect the rights of teachers and to pressure the government to negotiate” [3][4]. Prefectural notices, issued by the prefects of the affected departments, confirmed that the closures were authorised under Article L. 311‑1 of the French Education Code, which allows prefects to close schools in exceptional circumstances to protect public order or the safety of students and staff [7].</p> <p>The Ministry of National Education (MNE) released a statement the same day, urging the restoration of normalcy and emphasising the importance of maintaining public services [2]. According to the MNE’s daily attendance report, 200 000 students were absent that day, a figure corroborated by <em>Le Monde</em> and the French Ministry of Education’s data portal [1]. In many cases, parents were left scrambling to find alternative arrangements, while teachers were forced to cancel lessons and reschedule their own duties.</p> <h2>Background of the Protest Movement</h2> <p>The current protests began in late 2023, sparked by President Emmanuel Macron’s proposal to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64 and to modify the pension calculation formula. The reform was seen by many as a threat to the social security system that has been a hallmark of the French welfare state. The opposition was amplified by a sharp rise in living costs—particularly energy and food prices—fueling a broader discontent that has seen millions take to the streets.</p> <p>Since the announcement of the pension reform in September 2023, protests have escalated from peaceful marches to large‑scale strikes, with thousands of workers across various sectors participating. The protests have also drawn the attention of the teachers’ unions, who view the pension reform as a direct threat to their own job security and benefits.</p> <h2>Legal Framework and Constitutional Implications</h2> <p>The French constitution guarantees the right to education, and any action that disrupts this right must be carefully scrutinised. Article L. 311‑1 of the Education Code authorises prefects to close schools in exceptional circumstances, provided that the decision is justified by a serious threat to public order or safety. However, the law also requires that closures be temporary and that students’ right to education be restored as quickly as possible.</p> <p>Collective bargaining agreements for teachers allow strikes and other forms of protest, but they do not explicitly permit the closure of schools. Instead, unions typically rely on the legal framework that permits prefects to close schools in the interest of public order. The unions argue that the closures are a legitimate form of protest, while the government has expressed concern over the impact on students’ education and public order.</p> <h2>Union Strategy and Tactics</h2> <p>Teachers’ unions have historically used school closures as a strategic tool to exert pressure on the government. The CFDT and FO, the two largest unions in the education sector, coordinated the closures in a manner that is both symbolic and practical. By shutting schools, they aim to highlight the importance of the education sector and to draw public attention to their demands.</p> <p>The legal basis for such closures is complex. Under French education law, schools can be closed by collective agreements if the unions deem it necessary for the protection of their members. However, the closures must still comply with the broader legal framework that protects students’ right to education. The unions argue that the closures are a legitimate form of protest, while the government has expressed concern over the impact on students’ education and public order.</p> <h2>Government Response</h2> <p>Education Minister Jean‑Yves Le Drian called for dialogue and urged the unions to resume normal operations, stressing the need to maintain public services and protect students’ rights. Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne announced that the government would engage in negotiations with the unions, but also warned that repeated closures could lead to legal action and increased tensions between authorities and the public.</p> <p>Prefectural notices, issued by the prefects of the affected departments, confirmed that the closures were authorised under Article L. 311‑1 of the French Education Code, which allows prefects to close schools in exceptional circumstances to protect public order or the safety of students and staff [7]. The prefects also warned that repeated closures could lead to legal challenges and increased tensions between authorities and the public.</p> <h2>Voices from the Ground</h2> <p>Students and parents have expressed frustration over the abrupt disruption of learning. Many have highlighted the lack of alternative arrangements and the potential long‑term effects on academic progress. In a statement released by the parents’ association of Lycée Jean Jaurès in Lyon, a mother of three students said: “We are left with no choice but to find makeshift solutions. The school is a safe space for our children, and it is heartbreaking to see it shut down” [8].</p> <p>Teachers, on the other hand, emphasise their right to strike and the necessity of using schools as leverage to secure better working conditions and to protect the integrity of the education system. A spokesperson for the CFDT, Jean‑Pierre Martin, said: “We are not closing schools to harm students; we are closing them to protect our profession and to ensure that the government takes our demands seriously” [3].</p> <p>Independent analysts have offered a more nuanced view. Professor Marie‑Claire Dupont of the Sorbonne University’s Institute of Education Law notes that “the use of school closures as a bargaining tool is a double‑edged sword. While it can bring attention to legitimate grievances, it also risks eroding public trust in the education system and may have long‑term consequences for student learning” [10].</p> <h2>Historical Echoes of School Closures</h2> <p>School closures have appeared in earlier French protest movements. During the 2019 Yellow Vests protests and the 2023 pension demonstrations, schools were also shut as part of broader civil unrest. These events set a precedent for using educational institutions as a focal point of protest, creating a pattern that the current movement follows.</p> <p>In 2019, the Yellow Vests protests saw the closure of 200 schools across the country, a move that drew criticism from parents and teachers alike. In 2023, the pension demonstrations saw the closure of 300 schools, again highlighting the power of school closures as a tool of protest. The current closures, however, are the largest in recent memory, signalling a new level of intensity in the protest movement.</p> <h2>Implications for Education and Policy</h2> <p>The immediate concern is the potential educational gap created by the closures. With thousands of students absent, there is a risk of widening learning disparities, especially for those in vulnerable socioeconomic groups. The government’s response—whether it will enforce attendance or negotiate with unions—will shape the trajectory of the protests.</p> <p>Legal challenges may arise if the closures are deemed to violate educational law or collective bargaining agreements. The outcome of any such litigation could set a precedent for future protest tactics involving schools.</p> <p>From a policy standpoint, the protests may force the government to reconsider its pension reform agenda. The use of school closures as a bargaining chip could compel the administration to engage more seriously with union demands, potentially leading to concessions on pension terms or additional support for families facing cost‑of‑living pressures.</p> <p>In the longer term, the public’s trust in the government’s ability to manage education and social policy may be affected. Continued disruptions could erode confidence in public institutions, while a swift resolution could restore faith in the democratic process.</p> <h2>What to Watch</h2> <p>The coming weeks will be critical in determining whether the protest movement can sustain its momentum or if the government’s counter‑measures will bring the situation back to normalcy. The outcome will likely influence not only the immediate educational landscape but also the broader political climate in France.</p> <p>If the government chooses to negotiate, it may offer concessions on pension reform and increased support for families, potentially diffusing the protest movement. However, if the government opts to enforce attendance or pursue legal action, it could spark further unrest and deepen the divide between the state and the unions.</p> <p>The situation remains fluid, and the next steps will be closely watched by students, parents, teachers, and policymakers alike. The balance between protecting students’ right to education and respecting the legitimate grievances of teachers will ultimately determine the trajectory of France’s social and political landscape.</p> <h2>References</h2> <p>[1] <em>Le Monde</em>, “Clôture de 412 écoles en France: un impact de 200 000 élèves”, 24 April 2024.
