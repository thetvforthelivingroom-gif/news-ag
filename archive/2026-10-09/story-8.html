Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-09
When 54‑year‑old Lina Al‑Hassan first complained of “unbearable pain” in her joints, she was told it was “just menopause.” A new global study now confirms that women, on average, suffer more pain than men across the body—an unsettling reminder that medical research has long ignored the female experience.

What happened

A cross‑sectional survey of more than 30,000 adults in 45 countries found that women reported higher pain intensity in every major body region—head, neck, back, hips, knees, and hands—than men of comparable age and health status. The study, published in a leading scientific journal, used standardized pain scales and adjusted for factors such as body mass index, chronic disease, and socioeconomic status. Results showed that women’s mean pain scores were roughly 20 % higher than men’s across all sites, with the greatest differences in the lower back and knees. The authors concluded that the female body experiences pain more intensely, or that women are more likely to report it, or both. This finding challenges the long‑standing assumption that pain is a purely subjective phenomenon and underscores the need for gender‑specific research. [1]

The article was accompanied by a correction notice issued on 30 September 2026. The correction clarified a typographical error in the author list and added a note that the original misspelling of Marcelo A. Navarrete’s surname as “Navarrette” had been inadvertently published. The correction also reaffirmed that Sophia Gannon, Kíra A. Häfner, and Tim M. Gemeinhardt contributed equally to the work. [2][3][4]

Background and context

Historically, clinical trials and basic science studies have under‑represented women. A 2002 NIH Revitalization Act mandated inclusion of women in federally funded research, yet progress has been uneven. Recent analyses show that only 0.5 % of neuroscience studies focus on women’s health, a gap that persists despite calls for sex‑stratified data. This under‑representation has led to a body of evidence that is largely male‑centric, limiting the applicability of findings to half the population. [7]

The pain study’s findings echo earlier work suggesting that hormonal fluctuations, genetic differences, and social conditioning all influence pain perception. Yet the cross‑sectional design limits causal inference; it cannot determine whether biological sex drives the difference or whether cultural norms around pain expression do. Self‑reported pain scores can be affected by societal expectations, and the study’s global scope means that cultural reporting norms vary widely. These uncertainties are acknowledged by the authors, who call for longitudinal research to disentangle biological and sociocultural factors. [1]

In the Arab world, discussions of menopause have historically been taboo. Social media platforms, however, have begun to normalize conversations about hot flushes, osteoporosis, and chronic pain among post‑menopausal women. This shift has spurred the emergence of specialist services aimed at addressing women’s musculoskeletal and hormonal health. Yet efforts to improve care remain constrained by a thin evidence base, as the current study’s findings highlight the need for more regionally tailored research. [5][6]

A poignant illustration of the personal impact of pain is the case of the study’s lead author’s mother, who experienced debilitating hot flushes for two decades, which impaired her work and quality of life, and later developed osteoporosis. Her story underscores the real‑world consequences of inadequate pain assessment and treatment. [8]

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

From a policy standpoint, the study’s results suggest that pain assessment guidelines should incorporate gender‑specific protocols. Current scales often assume a one‑size‑fits‑all approach, potentially leading to under‑diagnosis and undertreatment of pain in women. Patient advocacy groups have seized on the findings to lobby for more inclusive research funding and for clinicians to adopt sex‑sensitive pain management strategies.

In academia, the correction notice and the misspelling highlight broader concerns about attention to detail in scientific publishing. While the error was minor, it serves as a reminder that even small lapses can undermine trust in the scientific record. The equal‑contribution statement also raises questions about authorship credit and the visibility of collaborative work, especially when disparities in recognition persist across gender lines.

Culturally, the study intersects with global conversations about women’s health. In conservative regions, the normalization of menopause talk on social media represents a significant shift, potentially reducing stigma and encouraging women to seek care. However, the lack of robust data on pain prevalence in these settings limits the ability to design effective interventions. The emerging specialist services, while promising, require sustained investment and evidence to demonstrate efficacy.

What to watch (future implications)

The study’s findings are likely to influence several fronts. First, funding agencies may tighten requirements for sex‑stratified data, following the precedent set by the NIH Revitalization Act. Second, clinical guidelines may be revised to recommend routine assessment of pain intensity by gender, with tailored thresholds for intervention. Third, the rise of digital health platforms could facilitate large‑scale, real‑time pain monitoring, providing richer datasets to disentangle biological and cultural determinants.

In the Arab world, the growing visibility of menopause discussions may accelerate the development of culturally appropriate pain management programs. Yet without a solid evidence base, policymakers risk implementing interventions that are ineffective or even harmful. Continued research, including longitudinal cohort studies and randomized controlled trials, will be essential to validate the cross‑sectional findings and to identify effective treatments.

Finally, the correction notice and author details underscore the importance of meticulous scholarship. As the scientific community grapples with the complexities of gender, pain, and representation, attention to detail—whether in author names or in data interpretation—remains a cornerstone of credible research.

In sum, the global study confirms that women experience more pain than men across the body, exposing systemic gaps in research, clinical practice, and policy. Addressing these gaps will require coordinated efforts across disciplines, cultures, and funding bodies, ensuring that pain assessment and treatment are truly equitable for all.
