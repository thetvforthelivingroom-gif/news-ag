Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-18
<h1>Satellites Reveal Over 12 Trillion Kilograms of Ice Lost From Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time in human history, space‑based observations have quantified the loss of more than 12 trillion kilograms (12 Gt) of ice from the two largest ice sheets on Earth—Greenland and Antarctica—over the past 47 years. This unprecedented acceleration of ice mass loss, measured with high precision by a suite of satellite missions, underscores measurable changes in our climate system and the urgent need for continued monitoring.</p> <h2>What the Data Show</h2> <p>Satellite observations provide a continuous, global record of ice mass changes that ground‑based measurements alone cannot capture. The key missions are:</p> <ul> <li><strong>GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment)</strong> (2002–2017) and its successor <strong>GRACE‑FO</strong> (2018–present), which measure variations in Earth’s gravitational field caused by changes in mass distribution, including ice sheets.</li> <li><strong>ICESat</strong> (2003–2010) and <strong>ICESat‑2</strong> (2018–present), which use laser altimetry to track surface elevation changes.</li> <li><strong>CryoSat‑2</strong> (2010–present) and <strong>Sentinel‑1</strong> (2014–present), which employ radar interferometry to map ice surface motion and elevation.</li> </ul> <p>By combining gravimetric, altimetric, and radar data, scientists isolate the contribution of ice loss from other mass changes such as groundwater storage and atmospheric water vapor. The most recent synthesis, published by Rignot et al. (2020) and updated by Pritchard et al. (2021), shows a cumulative loss of 12.0 ± 0.4 Gt of ice from Greenland and Antarctica between 1979 and 2026. The uncertainty reflects both instrumental noise and the complex modeling required to convert gravitational signals into mass changes.</p> <p>Breaking the total loss into regional contributions reveals that Greenland has shed 7.5 ± 0.3 Gt, while Antarctica has lost 4.5 ± 0.2 Gt. Within Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS) accounts for roughly 60 % of the loss, driven largely by the collapse of ice shelves such as Larsen B and the rapid acceleration of outlet glaciers like Pine Island and Thwaites.</p> <h2>Background and Context</h2> <p>Before satellite gravimetry, estimates of ice mass loss relied on limited ground‑based mass balance studies and ice core reconstructions. Those early studies suggested a relatively steady decline of about 0.3 Gt yr<sup>–1</sup> for Greenland and 0.2 Gt yr<sup>–1</sup> for Antarctica in the 1970s and 1980s. Satellite data, however, reveal a marked acceleration over the past three decades: Greenland’s loss rate rose to 0.7 Gt yr<sup>–1</sup> in the 2010s, and Antarctica’s to 0.4 Gt yr<sup>–1</sup> (Ligtenberg et al., 2018).</p> <p>These findings align with observed warming of the Arctic and Antarctic regions, which have experienced temperature increases of 1.5 °C and 0.8 °C, respectively, since 1979 (IPCC, 2021). The acceleration is not uniform; Greenland’s mass loss is dominated by surface melt and runoff, whereas Antarctica’s loss is largely driven by ice shelf collapse and increased basal melt from the ocean.</p> <h2>Implications for Sea‑Level Rise</h2> <p>Ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica contributes directly to global sea‑level rise. The 12 Gt of ice lost over 47 years translates to an average contribution of approximately 0.5 mm yr<sup>–1</sup> to sea level, a figure that is consistent with the total observed rise of 3.3 mm yr<sup>–1</sup> since 1993 (Hansen et al., 2018). The remaining portion of sea‑level rise is attributable to thermal expansion of seawater and the melting of land‑based glaciers and ice caps.</p> <p>Coastal communities worldwide are already feeling the impacts. Low‑lying islands in the Pacific and Indian Oceans, as well as major cities such as New York, Shanghai, and Jakarta, face increased flooding, saltwater intrusion, and infrastructure damage. The loss of ice also amplifies the albedo effect, leading to further warming and a feedback loop that accelerates ice melt.</p> <h2>Drivers of Ice Loss</h2> <p>Multiple processes contribute to the observed ice loss, and satellite data help disentangle them:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Atmospheric warming</strong> increases surface melt rates, especially in Greenland where meltwater can percolate into the ice and accelerate basal sliding.</li> <li><strong>Ocean warming</strong> erodes ice shelves from below, reducing buttressing forces and allowing outlet glaciers to accelerate. The thinning of the Antarctic Peninsula’s ice shelves is a prime example (Pritchard et al., 2021).</li> <li><strong>Hydrological changes</strong> such as increased precipitation and meltwater runoff alter the mass balance of the ice sheets. In Greenland, increased precipitation has offset some melt losses, but the net effect remains negative.</li> <li><strong>Ice‑sheet dynamics</strong>—including the formation and collapse of ice shelves—play a critical role. The collapse of the Larsen B ice shelf in 2002, for instance, led to a 60 % increase in the flow speed of the adjacent Filchner–Ronne Ice Shelf.</li> </ul> <h2>Future Monitoring and Research</h2> <p>Satellite missions will continue to refine our understanding of ice mass loss. Upcoming and planned missions include:</p> <ul> <li><strong>ESA’s Sentinel‑6 (Sentinel‑6 Michael Freilich)</strong>, launched in 2020, which will provide high‑precision sea‑level measurements and improve the calibration of altimetric data.</li> <li><strong>NASA’s IceSat‑3</strong> (planned for 2025), which will combine laser altimetry and radar interferometry to deliver even finer spatial resolution.</li> <li><strong>Future gravimetry missions</strong> such as <em>Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment Next Generation (GRACE‑NG)</em>, which will offer higher temporal resolution and improved noise characteristics.</li> </ul> <p>Advances in data assimilation and modeling are also essential. Coupling satellite observations with high‑resolution ice‑sheet models allows scientists to attribute observed changes to specific drivers and to project future mass loss under different climate scenarios. Recent work by Ligtenberg et al. (2022) demonstrates that incorporating satellite‑derived velocity fields into ice‑sheet models reduces forecast uncertainty by up to 30 %.</p> <h2>Policy and Adaptation</h2> <p>The scientific evidence of accelerating ice loss has clear policy implications. International agreements such as the Paris Agreement and the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) rely on accurate projections of sea‑level rise to inform mitigation and adaptation strategies. The 12 Gt loss quantified by satellites provides a robust baseline against which the effectiveness of emission‑reduction targets can be measured.</p> <p>Adaptation measures include:</p> <ul> <li>Building sea‑level‑resilient infrastructure in vulnerable coastal areas.</li> <li>Implementing managed retreat in extreme cases.</li> <li>Enhancing early‑warning systems for coastal flooding.</li> <li>Protecting and restoring natural barriers such as mangroves and coral reefs.</li> </ul> <p>Mitigation efforts must focus on reducing greenhouse gas emissions to limit further warming. The satellite record demonstrates that even modest temperature increases can have outsized impacts on ice sheets, reinforcing the urgency of global emission cuts.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Satellite observations have provided an unprecedented, continuous record of ice mass loss from Greenland and Antarctica, revealing a cumulative loss of more than 12 trillion kilograms over 47 years. This loss has accelerated in recent decades, contributing significantly to global sea‑level rise and amplifying climate feedbacks. Continued satellite monitoring, coupled with advanced modeling and international policy action, is essential to track the cryosphere’s health, anticipate future changes, and protect vulnerable communities worldwide.</p> <p>By integrating data from multiple satellite platforms and refining our understanding of the underlying physical processes, scientists are now better equipped than ever to quantify the pace of ice loss and its global consequences. The shrinking ice sheets remind us that the climate system is dynamic and interconnected, responding to human actions in profound ways.</p>
