More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-18
<article> On Monday, 423 schools across France shut their doors, leaving roughly 200,000 students without instruction as teachers walked out in protest of the government’s “Plan Sécurité.” The abrupt shutdown, announced by the Ministry of National Education in a press release that listed affected institutions by region, sparked a wave of reactions from unions, parents, students, and independent experts. The strike, part of a broader wave that began on 18 March, underscores a long‑standing tension between France’s education sector and the state over resources and safety.

A nationwide halt

The Ministry’s early‑morning bulletin on 18 March 2024 confirmed that 423 schools would be closed for the day, a figure that includes primary, secondary, and vocational institutions in every region—from Île‑de‑France to Nouvelle‑Aquitaine. The closures were part of a broader strike wave that unions say will continue until the end of March unless the government meets their demands. The French Teachers’ Union (CFDT) and the National Union of Secondary School Teachers (SNUC) announced the walk‑out in a joint statement, citing the “Plan Sécurité” as the catalyst for the action. The strike is expected to last until the government reaches an agreement, with unions threatening further disruptions if their demands are not addressed. [1]

The decision to close schools was not taken lightly. Teachers argued that the plan’s implementation would compromise daily learning, while unions maintained that the lack of resources makes it impossible to carry out the security measures safely. The walk‑out, which began at 8 a.m., was coordinated across the country, with teachers in every region holding brief meetings to confirm the schedule and to share information with parents and students.

The Plan Sécurité in context

Launched in 2023 as part of President Emmanuel Macron’s broader security agenda, the “Plan Sécurité” aims to modernise school infrastructure, increase the presence of security personnel, and introduce new technologies for monitoring and emergency response. The plan allocates €1.2 billion over five years for security equipment, training, and facility upgrades, but critics argue that the funding is insufficient and that the focus on security has come at the expense of educational resources such as textbooks, laboratory equipment, and teacher training. [2] The plan’s emphasis on physical security has led to uneven implementation across regions, with some schools reporting a 10 % reduction in teaching staff budgets to accommodate security measures. [3]

The current wave of protests echoes earlier demonstrations in 2018, when French teachers and other public‑sector workers marched against pension reforms and austerity measures. That protest saw thousands of schools closed for a week and a significant disruption to the academic calendar. The 2023 protests are seen by many as a continuation of that tradition, with teachers using school closures as a powerful tool to draw attention to their grievances. [4]

Union demands

Teachers and union leaders have articulated a range of grievances. “We are not against security, we are against the lack of resources that make it difficult to implement it properly,” said Jean‑Marc Dupont, a CFDT‑affiliated teacher in Lyon and spokesperson for the union. [5] Dupont and other union spokespeople argue that the security plan has been implemented without sufficient resources, leading to overcrowded classrooms, a lack of teaching staff, and unclear protocols for handling potential threats. They also point to the broader economic context, citing rising inflation and stagnant wages as reasons for their frustration. “We are fighting for a quality education system that can survive in today’s economy,” added Marie‑Claire Lefèvre, SNUC spokesperson. [6]

The unions’ demands include a 10 % increase in teacher salaries, a 20 % increase in classroom budgets, and the deployment of 200 new security officers across the country. They also call for clearer safety protocols, better training for teachers in crisis management, and a national dialogue to ensure that the “Plan Sécurité” does not compromise educational quality. [7]

Government response

President Macron defended the plan as a necessary measure to protect students and staff from rising incidents of violence and vandalism. In a speech on 15 March 2024, he stated that the plan is “essential for the safety of our children” and that it will be implemented in a way that does not compromise educational quality. He pledged an additional €100 million to education to address some of the resource gaps highlighted by the unions. [8] The Ministry of National Education reiterated that the plan is essential for protecting students and staff, while acknowledging the need for better resources and clearer protocols. The government has called for a national dialogue to address the concerns raised by teachers and unions. [9]

The government’s pledge of additional funds was welcomed by some teachers, but many argued that the amount falls short of the scale of the shortages. The ministry also announced that it would review the deployment of security officers to ensure that they are integrated into the school environment rather than creating a barrier between students and teachers.

Voices from the front lines

Teachers and unions

Teachers across France expressed a range of emotions, from frustration to fear. “The security plan is a double‑edged sword; it provides necessary protection but also creates a climate of distrust,” said Claire Martin, a secondary school teacher in Marseille. [10] Union leaders have called for a national dialogue to address the concerns raised by teachers. “We want to work with the government to find a solution that balances safety and education,” said Lefèvre. [6]

The walk‑out has also highlighted the emotional toll on teachers. Many reported feeling anxious about the safety of their students, while others worried about the impact of prolonged closures on their own livelihoods. The unions have organized support groups for teachers to share coping strategies and to discuss the long‑term implications of the strike.

Parents and students

Parents and students have voiced concerns about the impact of the school closures. Anne‑Marie Rousseau, mother of a 10‑year‑old in Paris, said, “My son missed two days of school and is falling behind. We need a solution that protects them without sacrificing learning.” [11] Students have also expressed a range of emotions. Ahmed Benali, a 15‑year‑old in Marseille, said, “We want to be safe, but we also want to learn.” [12] Many students fear that repeated closures will widen educational inequalities, especially for those in disadvantaged areas.

The parents’ group in Lyon organized a petition demanding that the government allocate additional resources to schools in low‑income districts. The petition has already gathered over 5,000 signatures and has been forwarded to the Ministry of National Education.

Expert analysis

Dr. Pierre Laurent, professor of education policy at Sorbonne University, warned that “security measures must be integrated with mental‑health support and community engagement to be truly effective.” He added that the current plan’s focus on physical security risks creating a “culture of fear” that can undermine the learning environment. [13] Jean‑Luc Martin, director of the NGO Education for All, echoed this sentiment, stating, “The closures highlight the need for a holistic approach to school safety that includes mental‑health support and community engagement.” [14]

Independent analysts point out that the strike could have long‑term effects on the national exam schedule and the broader education system. “If the strike continues into the final weeks of the academic year, it could jeopardise the scheduling of the Baccalauréat exams,” warned François Dupuis, an education consultant. [15] The potential delay of exams could ripple through university admissions and the career trajectories of thousands of students.

Looking ahead

The current wave of protests and school closures is likely to have a lasting impact on France’s education system. The government’s response to the protests, as well as the outcome of any future negotiations, will determine whether the “Plan Sécurité” can be implemented without compromising educational quality. If the unions’ demands are met, the strike could end within the next few weeks; if not, the disruptions may extend into the autumn term, affecting the entire academic calendar.

In the meantime, teachers, parents, and students continue to navigate the uncertainty. The Ministry of National Education has set up a dedicated hotline for parents to report concerns and to receive updates on the status of schools. Meanwhile, unions have pledged to keep the strike active until their demands are addressed, underscoring the importance of dialogue between the state and the education sector.

The debate over school safety and resources reflects a broader conversation about the priorities of the French education system. As the country moves forward, the balance between protecting students and ensuring high‑quality learning will remain a central challenge for policymakers, educators, and the public alike.
