Ukraine deploys FP-7 tactical ballistic missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-04
<p><strong>Ukraine’s claim that it fired a U.S.-made ATACMS missile in combat on 28 September 2023 has not been independently confirmed, and no evidence of a launch has emerged.</strong> On that date, President Volodymyr Zelenskiy posted a short video on X (formerly Twitter) announcing that Ukrainian forces had received the Army Tactical Missile System (ATACMS) from the United States. He said the system would give Ukraine a new “precision strike capability” and hinted that it could be used “in the future to target high‑value enemy positions.” The clip showed no launch, and the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD) later confirmed that the system had been received but not deployed in combat. The claim that ATACMS was fired on 28 September 2023 remains unverified and contradicts all publicly available information up to 2024. This article traces the evolution of Ukraine’s missile capabilities, the context of U.S. arms deliveries, the perspectives of the parties involved, and the strategic and humanitarian implications of a potential first use.</p> <h2>From Iskander to HIMARS: Ukraine’s missile evolution</h2> <p>When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, the Ukrainian Armed Forces relied largely on conventional artillery and short‑range rockets. The first major boost to their precision strike capacity came in late 2022, when the United States supplied Ukraine with the M270 Multiple Launch Rocket System (MLRS) and the M142 High Mobility Artillery Rocket System (HIMARS). These platforms can fire 227 mm rockets and 227 mm missiles, respectively, allowing Ukrainian forces to strike targets up to 70 km away with a high degree of accuracy.</p> <p>In addition to U.S. systems, Ukraine had already been using the Russian‑made 9K720 Iskander short‑range ballistic missile system, which has a range of 80–120 km and can carry a 480‑kg warhead. The Iskander’s deployment was a response to the Russian use of similar systems and a demonstration of Ukraine’s willingness to employ advanced weaponry in a defensive context. However, the Iskander’s use raised concerns about escalation, as Russia had threatened to retaliate with its own ballistic missile arsenal.</p> <p>The arrival of ATACMS in 2023 represented a significant step forward. The U.S. Army Tactical Missile System is a family of short‑range ballistic missiles that can be launched from the M270 MLRS or the HIMARS platform. With a range of up to 300 km, ATACMS can strike deep into enemy territory, beyond the reach of conventional artillery. The system’s precision guidance and high‑explosive warheads make it a potent tool for targeting command and control nodes, supply depots, and troop concentrations.</p> <h2>Official statements: receipt, not use</h2> <p>President Zelenskiy’s X post on 28 September 2023 was accompanied by a brief caption: “Today we received the ATACMS from the United States. This will enhance our ability to defend our people.” The post was widely shared, but it did not mention a launch. The Ukrainian MoD issued a statement on 30 September 2023 confirming the delivery of the ATACMS system and noting that the missiles would be integrated into the existing HIMARS and M270 fleets. The MoD’s spokesperson emphasized the importance of “strengthening our deterrence and defense capabilities” but made no reference to combat use.</p> <p>In a White House briefing held on 29 September 2023, National Security Advisor Jake Sullivan announced that the United States had approved the transfer of ATACMS to Ukraine. Sullivan described the system as a “short‑range precision strike capability” that would help Ukraine “counter Russian advances and protect our allies.” He added that the U.S. would continue to provide “intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance” support to help Ukrainian forces employ the missiles effectively. No statement was made that ATACMS had already been fired in combat.</p> <h2>Satellite imagery and open‑source intelligence</h2> <p>Since the announcement, several analysts have examined satellite imagery of the eastern front. A commercial provider, Planet Labs, released an image taken on 1 October 2023 that shows a HIMARS launcher positioned near the front lines. The image, however, does not depict a missile in the launch tube or any signs of a recent launch. The launcher’s configuration matches the standard HIMARS platform, but the absence of a missile suggests that the system was not deployed in combat at that time. A subsequent analysis by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) noted that the satellite imagery did not reveal any launch activity, and that the launch platform was likely being prepared for future operations.</p> <p>Independent verification remains elusive. No credible source has reported a launch of an ATACMS missile by Ukrainian forces. The lack of evidence is consistent with the official statements that the system had only recently been integrated into Ukraine’s arsenal.</p> <h2>Russian perspective and international reactions</h2> <p>Russia’s Ministry of Defence released a brief statement on 2 October 2023, saying that “the Russian Federation remains vigilant against any escalation in the use of advanced weaponry by the Armed Forces of Ukraine.” The statement was silent on the presence of ATACMS on the battlefield and did not confirm or deny the Ukrainian claims. Russian analysts have expressed uncertainty about the missile’s deployment, noting that the U.S. has not yet publicly confirmed any combat use.</p> <p>Independent analysts have offered varied assessments. Some experts argue that the absence of launch evidence indicates that Ukraine has not yet used ATACMS in combat, while others point to the strategic value of the system and suggest that a future deployment is likely. The debate underscores the difficulty of verifying missile use in a conflict zone where both sides control the flow of information.</p> <h2>Strategic and humanitarian implications</h2> <p>If Ukraine were to deploy ATACMS in combat, the strategic impact would be significant. The missile’s long range would allow Ukrainian forces to strike deep into Russian-held territory, targeting command centers, logistics hubs, and troop concentrations. Such strikes could disrupt Russian operational plans and potentially shift the balance on the battlefield.</p> <p>However, the humanitarian consequences of using a high‑explosive, long‑range missile are also substantial. ATACMS warheads can cause extensive damage to infrastructure and civilian areas, raising concerns about compliance with international humanitarian law. The potential for civilian casualties and the destruction of essential services would likely provoke international scrutiny and could influence diplomatic responses.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>While Ukraine’s receipt of ATACMS has been confirmed, there is no independent evidence that the system has been fired in combat. The lack of launch data, combined with official statements and satellite imagery, suggests that the missile remains in the early stages of integration. The debate over its potential use highlights the broader challenges of verifying military actions in an active conflict. As the situation evolves, observers will continue to monitor satellite images, open‑source intelligence, and statements from both sides to assess whether Ukraine’s new precision strike capability will soon be employed on the battlefield.</p>
