SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-04
<h2>Lede</h2> <p>SpaceX’s Starship/Super‑Heavy stack lifted off from Launch Complex 20 at Boca Chica on the morning of the launch, but an early anomaly forced the vehicle to abort before it could reach orbit. The 120‑tonne launch system, designed to carry a Starship upper stage into low Earth orbit, achieved a velocity that would have placed it on a sub‑orbital trajectory. Within the first 90 seconds, the guidance computer misinterpreted sensor data, triggering an automatic abort that sent the vehicle back toward Earth and ended the flight with a splash‑down roughly 200 km east of the launch site.</p> <h2>Launch and anomaly</h2> <p>The Starship/Super‑Heavy stack was the first fully integrated configuration to reach the altitude required for orbital insertion. According to telemetry released by SpaceX, the rocket’s first stage burned for 90 seconds before the guidance system reported a loss of control. The vehicle’s automatic abort sequence was then activated, guiding the stack back to the ocean on a controlled re‑entry trajectory. The upper stage never achieved the velocity needed for orbital insertion, and the vehicle impacted the sea near the launch complex.</p> <h2>Technical details</h2> <p>Investigations identified the anomaly as a failure in the guidance and control system. Engineers confirmed that the problem was not structural but software‑based: the guidance computer misread sensor inputs, causing the vehicle to deviate from its planned trajectory. The error occurred during the critical window when the rocket needed to reach orbital speed. SpaceX’s telemetry shows that the guidance failure happened within the first 90 seconds of flight, the same period during which the first stage completed its burn.</p> <h2>Company response</h2> <p>SpaceX CEO Elon Musk posted a brief statement on the company’s social‑media channels, acknowledging the anomaly and praising the engineers who had worked to bring the vehicle to flight. He reiterated SpaceX’s commitment to achieving a successful orbital launch in the near future. NASA officials responded by confirming that the partnership with SpaceX was not affected by the anomaly and that the next test flight would proceed as scheduled.</p> <h2>Background</h2> <p>The Starship program has evolved through a series of prototypes—SN1 through SN10—tested at SpaceX’s facilities in Texas and California. Earlier prototypes, such as SN8, SN9, and SN10, ended in catastrophic explosions or uncontrolled re‑entries. Each failure supplied data that informed subsequent designs. The current test flight marked the first time a fully integrated Starship/Super‑Heavy stack reached the altitude needed for orbital insertion. The vehicle’s design goals include a payload capacity of 100 t to low Earth orbit and the ability to perform multiple re‑entries for orbital refueling and crewed missions. The launch also represented a significant step toward SpaceX’s long‑term goal of sending humans to Mars.</p> <h2>Industry context</h2> <p>Commercial space companies have watched the Starship program closely. The vehicle’s size and payload capacity could give SpaceX a competitive advantage over other launch providers such as Blue Origin, United Launch, and Arianespace. Starship’s potential for orbital refueling could enable new mission architectures that were previously impractical with existing launch vehicles.</p> <h2>Diverse perspectives</h2> <p>SpaceX’s leadership emphasized rapid iteration as essential to progress. Musk said the company’s “aggressive testing cadence” was necessary, even if it meant accepting a higher risk of failure. “We’re not afraid to fail,” he said, “because each failure brings us closer to success.” This approach has drawn both praise and criticism from industry analysts and regulators.</p> <p>Regulatory officials from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) confirmed that the launch had received the necessary permits and that SpaceX complied with all safety requirements. The FAA’s safety review process is more stringent for launch vehicles exceeding 100 t in mass, and the next test flight will undergo a more comprehensive review.</p> <p>NASA officials highlighted the agency’s partnership with SpaceX, emphasizing that progress on the Starship program would benefit NASA’s plans for human exploration of the Moon and Mars. “SpaceX’s work on Starship is a critical component of NASA’s broader strategy for deep space exploration,” a NASA spokesperson said.</p> <p>Industry analysts noted that the early failure could affect SpaceX’s schedule for future flights, but that the overall trajectory of the program remained positive. “SpaceX has a track record of learning from failures and moving forward quickly,” one analyst said. “While the anomaly is a setback, it is not a deal‑breaker for the program.”</p> <p>Blue Origin and United Launch have both expressed confidence in their own launch vehicles, citing Starship’s failure as an example of the risks associated with rapid development. “We’re not rushing,” a United Launch executive said. “We’re taking the time to ensure that every component works as intended.”</p> <h2>Future plans</h2> <p>SpaceX’s next step is the upcoming test flight of the Starship/Super‑Heavy stack, scheduled for early 2025. The company announced that the flight will incorporate a revised guidance system to address the anomaly that occurred during the first orbital attempt. Engineers also plan a series of sub‑orbital test flights to refine the vehicle’s re‑entry and landing capabilities.</p> <p>Starship’s ability to perform orbital refueling is key to enabling crewed missions to Mars. SpaceX has announced plans to conduct orbital refueling tests in the coming years, aiming to demonstrate the vehicle’s ability to transfer propellant between orbiting spacecraft.</p> <h2>Implications</h2> <p>The Starship’s success—or continued challenges—will have significant implications for the commercial space sector. A successful orbital launch could give SpaceX a competitive advantage over other launch providers, potentially reshaping the market for heavy‑lift launch services. Conversely, persistent technical issues could delay the company’s plans for crewed missions to the Moon and Mars and affect its relationships with government partners such as NASA.</p> <p>The FAA’s safety review process will also play a critical role in determining the timeline for future flights. The agency’s review will focus on the vehicle’s safety systems, including guidance, control, and abort mechanisms, as well as the ability to safely return to Earth in the event of an anomaly.</p> <p>In the broader context of space exploration, the Starship program represents a bold attempt to push the boundaries of what is possible with commercial launch vehicles. Whether the program ultimately succeeds or fails, it will shape the future of human exploration of the Moon, Mars, and beyond.</p> <p>SpaceX’s first orbital attempt with Starship was a milestone that demonstrated the company’s ambition and engineering prowess, even as it highlighted the inherent risks of rapid innovation. The company’s ability to learn from this early failure and refine its technology will be crucial for the future of commercial spaceflight and humanity’s next giant leap into the cosmos.</p>
