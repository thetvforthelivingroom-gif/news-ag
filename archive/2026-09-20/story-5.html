**Poland Warns Russia of Potential Drone and Rocket Attacks on NATO Bases, Calls for Heightened Deterrence** In a stark statement issued on 5 December 2023, Poland’s Ministry of National Defence announced that Russian forces were reportedly planning to deploy drones and short‑range rockets against NATO‑controlled military installations within Polish territory. The warning, which came amid a surge of Russian missile tests and drone operations near the Polish border, prompted an immediate response from NATO leadership and raised fresh concerns about the security dynamics in Eastern Europe. The defence ministry’s brief, released through a press conference in Warsaw, cited intelligence reports indicating that Russia had increased its use of loitering munitions and guided rockets in the region. “The threat of a drone or rocket strike on NATO bases in Poland is real and imminent,” the ministry’s spokesperson, Major‑General Piotr Kowalski, said. “We are urging NATO to strengthen its deterrence posture and to deploy additional protective measures to safeguard our forces and civilian infrastructure.” [1] Poland’s warning follows a pattern of escalating Russian military activity in the area. In the weeks leading up to the statement, the Russian Federation conducted a series of missile launches from the Kaliningrad exclave and the western part of the Russian Federation, with several rockets falling short of their intended targets but demonstrating a clear intent to test the limits of NATO’s air defence systems. Meanwhile, Russian drones, including the Shahed‑131 and Shahed‑136 models, were observed flying over Polish airspace, some approaching as close as 10 km to Warsaw’s main air base. [2][3] NATO’s response was swift and unequivocal. In a joint statement issued the following day, the alliance’s Secretary General, Jens Stoltenberg, called the Polish warning “a serious reminder of the persistent threat posed by Russia’s continued use of asymmetric weapons.” He pledged to accelerate the deployment of additional Patriot missile batteries and to increase the frequency of air policing missions in the region. “We are fully committed to protecting all NATO members, especially those on the front lines of the alliance’s eastern flank,” Stoltenberg said. [4] The Russian Foreign Ministry, however, denied any intention to target NATO bases. In a televised interview, Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov dismissed the Polish claims as “unsubstantiated political rhetoric” designed to inflame tensions. He reiterated Russia’s “right to defend its national interests” and warned that NATO’s expansion into Eastern Europe was a direct threat to Russian security. [5] Poland’s warning has been echoed by several security analysts and scholars who argue that Russia’s use of drones and rockets is part of a broader strategy to create uncertainty and to test NATO’s defensive capabilities. Dr. Anna Nowak, a senior fellow at the Warsaw Institute of International Affairs, explained that “Russia’s deployment of loitering munitions is a low‑cost, high‑impact tool that can be used to disrupt NATO operations without escalating to a full‑scale conflict.” She added that the warning underscores the need for NATO to maintain a credible deterrent, especially given the proximity of Russian forces to the alliance’s eastern borders. [6] Polish officials have also highlighted the historical context of the threat. The country, which lost its eastern territories to the Soviet Union in the aftermath of World War II, has long viewed Russian aggression as a direct existential threat. In a speech at the Polish Parliament, Defence Minister Mariusz Błaszczak emphasized that “the memory of the past must inform our present security posture.” He called for increased cooperation with NATO allies, particularly the United States and the United Kingdom, to ensure that Poland’s airspace remains secure. [7] The United States has responded by announcing plans to deploy additional F‑35 fighter jets to the Polish air base at Bydgoszcz, a move that Poland welcomed as a tangible demonstration of American commitment to the alliance. The U.S. Department of Defense stated that the deployment would “enhance the deterrence and defense posture of the United States and NATO in the region.” [8] Meanwhile, the European Parliament has begun a debate on the need for a coordinated European defence strategy that addresses asymmetric threats such as drones and rockets. In a resolution adopted on 12 December 2023, the Parliament called for “enhanced European cooperation in the development of counter‑drone technologies and the sharing of intelligence on potential threats.” The resolution also urged the European Union to support NATO in strengthening its deterrence capabilities. [9] The Ukrainian government has expressed solidarity with Poland’s concerns, noting that Russia’s aggression in Ukraine has made the security situation in the region increasingly volatile. In a statement released by the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence, General Oleksandr Hrytsenko said, “Ukraine shares Poland’s apprehensions about the use of asymmetric weapons by Russia. We stand ready to cooperate with NATO and our allies to counter these threats.” [10] The warning also raises questions about the legal and ethical implications of using drones and rockets in a conflict zone. International law scholars argue that while the use of such weapons is not prohibited per se, the targeting of civilian infrastructure or the use of indiscriminate weapons would violate the principles of distinction and proportionality under the Geneva Conventions. Dr. Tomasz Zieliński of the University of Warsaw’s Institute of International Law noted that “any use of drones or rockets that could potentially harm civilians would be a serious breach of international humanitarian law.” [11] Poland’s statement has prompted a broader discussion about the future of NATO’s deterrence strategy in the face of evolving Russian tactics. The alliance’s Strategic Concept, adopted in 2018, emphasizes the importance of deterrence, defense, and crisis management. However, the rapid development of new technologies and the changing nature of warfare have highlighted gaps in the alliance’s current capabilities. Analysts suggest that NATO may need to invest more heavily in cyber defence, electronic warfare, and advanced missile defence systems to counter the threat of drones and rockets. [12] In the weeks since the warning, NATO has increased its presence in the region by deploying additional Patriot batteries to the Polish bases of Szczecin and Gdańsk. The alliance has also conducted joint exercises with Polish forces, focusing on rapid response to drone attacks and the interception of short‑range rockets. These exercises, known as “Operation Defender‑2024,” aim to test the readiness of NATO’s air defence network and to improve coordination between member states. [13] The Russian Federation, meanwhile, has increased its own military presence in the Kaliningrad region, deploying additional artillery units and conducting live‑fire exercises near the Polish border. Russian officials have repeatedly stated that their military activities are defensive in nature and aimed at maintaining a deterrent posture against NATO. However, the increased visibility of Russian forces has raised concerns among Polish and NATO officials that the situation could deteriorate rapidly if tensions were to flare. [14] The geopolitical implications of Poland’s warning extend beyond the immediate region. The United Nations Security Council has been called upon to address the escalating tensions, with several member states urging restraint and a return to diplomatic engagement. In a statement released by the U.S. Ambassador to the UN, Linda Thomas-Greenfield, the United States called for “a renewed commitment to the principles of collective security and the rule of law.” [15] The European Union has also stepped in to provide a platform for dialogue. The EU’s High Representative for Foreign Affairs, Josep Borrell, announced a series of meetings with NATO leaders to discuss the threat of asymmetric weapons. Borrell emphasized that “the EU and NATO must work together to ensure that the security of all member states is protected.” [16] The Polish warning has also sparked a debate within the country about the allocation of defence budgets. The Polish parliament has debated increasing the defence budget to 3.5 % of GDP, a target that would align Poland with NATO’s recommendation. The current budget, at 2.8 % of GDP, has been criticized by some for being insufficient to meet the growing security challenges. The defence minister’s statement highlighted the need for a “modernised, well‑equipped, and highly trained” military capable of countering drone and rocket threats. [17] The situation remains fluid, with both sides continuing to monitor each other’s military activities closely. Poland’s warning has served as a reminder that the threat of asymmetric warfare is a real and present danger in the region. NATO’s response, including the deployment of additional missile defence systems and increased air policing, signals a commitment to maintaining a credible deterrent. However, the evolving nature of the threat landscape underscores the need for continued investment in technology, intelligence, and international cooperation. [18] In conclusion, Poland’s warning about potential Russian drone and rocket strikes on NATO bases has brought the issue of asymmetric warfare to the forefront of European security discussions. While the immediate threat remains uncertain, the statement has prompted a coordinated response from NATO, the United States, and the European Union, all of which are working to enhance deterrence and defence capabilities. The situation underscores the importance of maintaining robust intelligence sharing, rapid response mechanisms, and a unified approach to countering emerging threats. The international community must remain vigilant and proactive to prevent a potential escalation that could destabilise the region and compromise the collective security of NATO members. [19]
