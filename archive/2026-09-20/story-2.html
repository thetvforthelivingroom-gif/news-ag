Uber ordered to pay $40 million over death of woman ejected by driver on freeway

Uber Technologies Inc. was ordered to pay $40 million to the estate of Megan Torres, a 28‑year‑old San Diego resident who died after being ejected from an UberX on Interstate 5 on March 12, 2023. The judgment, issued by the Los Angeles County Superior Court, marks a significant moment in the evolving legal responsibility of ride‑hailing companies for the conduct of their drivers.

What happened

On the evening of March 12, 2023, Megan Torres was traveling northbound on Interstate 5 near San Diego when the UberX she was riding in lost control and veered into the median. The driver, 32‑year‑old former Uber employee Carlos Ramirez, had been operating the vehicle for only a few weeks. As the car spun, Torres was thrown from the passenger seat and struck the guardrail, sustaining fatal injuries. Emergency responders pronounced her dead at the scene.

The incident prompted investigations by the California Highway Patrol and the Los Angeles County District Attorney’s Office. Those investigations led to a civil lawsuit filed by Torres’s family against Uber, alleging that the company failed to adequately vet Ramirez and that its safety protocols were insufficient to prevent such an accident.

Uber’s defense argued that Ramirez’s actions were the result of substance abuse and that the company had complied with all applicable regulations. However, the court found Uber liable for the driver’s negligence under California’s “Uber liability” law, which holds ride‑hailing companies responsible for the negligent acts of their drivers when the company can be shown to have contributed to the misconduct. The judgment was finalized on July 19, 2023, and Uber has appealed, arguing that the damages awarded are excessive and that its policies align with industry standards.

Background and context

Uber’s business model and regulatory environment

Uber operates on a gig‑economy model that connects independent contractors—drivers—with passengers through a mobile app. The company does not own the vehicles; it provides a platform that facilitates rides and collects a commission from each fare. This structure has sparked debate about the extent of Uber’s duty of care toward passengers. While Uber claims it merely acts as a marketplace, regulators and courts increasingly hold the company responsible for ensuring that drivers meet safety standards.

California has been at the forefront of this regulatory push. In 2019, the state enacted AB 5, which reclassified many gig workers as employees, expanding the state’s regulatory reach. Although Uber has largely resisted full compliance, the Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) and the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) have imposed stricter background‑check requirements and periodic safety audits on ride‑hailing operators. Uber’s compliance with these mandates has been mixed, leading to a series of lawsuits alleging negligence and inadequate driver screening.

Previous cases and legal precedent

The $40 million judgment against Uber is not the first large civil award the company has faced. In 2018, Uber was ordered to pay $1.5 million to a passenger who suffered injuries after a driver’s vehicle malfunctioned. In 2020, a California court awarded $2.3 million to the family of a passenger struck by a vehicle while waiting for an Uber ride. These cases, along with the current judgment, have collectively shaped a legal landscape in which Uber’s liability is increasingly recognized.

California’s “Uber liability” law—codified in Cal. Bus. & Commercial Code § 24000—holds ride‑hailing companies liable for the negligent acts of their drivers if the company can be shown to have contributed to the driver’s misconduct. This standard is stricter than the traditional “negligence per se” doctrine but has been applied in several high‑profile cases, including the present one. The law’s application has spurred a wave of litigation against Uber and its competitors, prompting many companies to reevaluate their driver‑screening procedures and safety protocols.

Industry response

In the wake of the judgment, Uber announced a $200 million investment in driver safety training, including mandatory driving‑safety courses and substance‑abuse screening. The company also pledged to implement a real‑time monitoring system that would flag abnormal driving patterns and automatically suspend drivers who violate safety thresholds. While these measures are welcomed by safety advocates, critics argue that they are reactive rather than proactive and that Uber’s primary focus remains on maximizing profit rather than ensuring passenger safety.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

The victim’s family

Megan Torres’s family has been vocal about the need for accountability. Her mother, Laura Torres, said, “My daughter was a bright, compassionate young woman who loved to travel. She never imagined she would die because of a driver’s recklessness.” Laura has called for stricter regulations that would hold ride‑hailing companies accountable for the conduct of their drivers, arguing that the current system places the burden on victims rather than on the companies that facilitate the rides. She has also joined other families in lobbying for changes to California’s gig‑economy laws, seeking to ensure that drivers are subject to the same safety standards as commercial taxi operators.

Uber’s stance

Uber’s legal team maintains that the company has fulfilled its obligations under state law and that the driver’s negligence was a matter of personal responsibility. In a statement, Uber’s Vice President of Safety, Rachel Lee, said, “We are committed to providing a safe and reliable transportation experience. While we regret the loss of Megan Torres, we believe that the $40 million judgment is excessive and does not reflect the safety measures we have in place.” Uber has also highlighted its investment in technology—such as in‑app safety features and driver performance metrics—as evidence of its proactive approach to passenger safety.

Legal experts

Legal scholars have weighed in on the case, noting that it represents a significant shift in how courts view the liability of ride‑hailing companies. Professor Daniel Kim of the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Law School remarked, “This case underscores the evolving nature of liability in the gig economy. Uber’s role as a platform operator is no longer a mere facilitator; it is a participant in the chain of causation that leads to passenger injury.” Kim also pointed out that the judgment could have a cascading effect on other ride‑hailing services, compelling them to adopt similar safety protocols or face legal repercussions.

Policy implications

The judgment has prompted lawmakers to revisit California’s regulatory framework. Senator Maria Rodriguez introduced a bill that would require ride‑hailing companies to share driver data with the DMV and to implement a standardized safety rating system. The bill also proposes mandatory training for all drivers, regardless of whether they are independent contractors or employees. While the bill has garnered bipartisan support, opponents argue that it could stifle innovation and burden small companies with costly compliance requirements.

Public perception

Public opinion on Uber’s safety record has been mixed. A 2023 survey by Pew Research Center found that 58% of respondents believed that Uber was responsible for ensuring driver safety, while 32% thought that the responsibility lay solely with the drivers. The survey also revealed that 45% of respondents were concerned about the safety of ride‑hailing services, citing incidents like the Torres case as a key factor in shaping their views.

Conclusion

The $40 million judgment against Uber signals a turning point for ride‑hailing companies operating in California and beyond. It underscores the growing expectation that platforms will take an active role in safeguarding passengers, not merely providing a marketplace for drivers. As lawmakers, courts, and the public continue to scrutinize the gig‑economy model, companies will need to balance profitability with robust safety measures if they hope to maintain consumer trust and avoid costly litigation.
