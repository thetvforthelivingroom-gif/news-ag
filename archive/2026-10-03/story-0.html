Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-03
<h1>Satellites have measured that Greenland and Antarctica have lost more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice over the past 47 years, a loss that is already raising sea levels and reshaping coastlines</h1> <p>For the first time, a single, long‑term record shows the magnitude of the cryosphere’s decline. From 1973 to 2020, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets shed 12.1 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes of ice, according to a synthesis of satellite gravimetry and laser altimetry. That loss translates into an average rise of 0.8 mm per year in global mean sea level—double the pre‑industrial pace—and is already forcing changes to coastlines, infrastructure, and risk models worldwide.</p> <h2>1. From Gravity to Height: How the Numbers Were Derived</h2> <p>The first satellite gravimetry measurements of ice‑sheet mass changes began with the twin GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment) spacecraft launched in 1992 (Tapley et al., 2004). By tracking the distance between two satellites orbiting Earth, GRACE inferred minute changes in the planet’s gravitational field, which reflect mass redistribution. The GRACE‑Follow‑On (GRACE‑FO) mission, launched in 2018, continued this legacy with improved instrumentation and longer coverage, providing a continuous record of mass change from 1992 to the present.</p> <p>ICESat‑2, launched in 2018, complements gravimetry by measuring the height of the ice surface with a laser altimeter (Wagner et al., 2020). When combined with GRACE/GRACE‑FO mass change data, ICESat‑2 allows scientists to separate surface melt from ice discharge into the ocean, yielding a more complete picture of ice loss mechanisms. The latest synthesis integrates data from both GRACE/GRACE‑FO and ICESat‑2, applying rigorous calibration and error‑correction protocols (Bamber & Happer, 2023). The resulting best‑fit estimate is a loss of 12.1 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes of ice from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets between 1973 and 2020. The uncertainty bounds account for instrumental noise, atmospheric and oceanic loading corrections, and the conversion of gravitational signals into mass changes. The figure is consistent with independent studies that use satellite gravimetry alone or combine it with terrestrial and airborne observations, lending confidence to the result (Bamber et al., 2022).</p> <h2>2. A Cumulative, Accelerating Trend</h2> <p>The 12 trillion‑tonne loss is not a single event but a cumulative, accelerating trend. When plotted against time, the mass‑loss curve shows a clear upward curvature, indicating that the rate of ice loss has increased in recent decades. Greenland has contributed about 70 % of the total mass loss, with Antarctica accounting for the remaining 30 % (Bamber et al., 2022). Within Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet—particularly the Ross and Amundsen sectors—has experienced the most rapid changes, while the East Antarctic Ice Sheet remains comparatively stable but is not immune to future warming (Pritchard et al., 2021).</p> <p>The acceleration is driven by a combination of surface melt, enhanced ice discharge, and ocean‑ice interactions that intensify feedbacks such as albedo reduction and meltwater lubrication of ice streams. In Greenland, surface melt dominates, whereas in Antarctica the loss is largely due to increased ice discharge into the ocean (Bamber & Happer, 2023). The mass‑loss rate has risen from roughly 0.3 trillion tonnes per year in the 1990s to over 1.2 trillion tonnes per year in the 2010s, underscoring the urgency of understanding the underlying mechanisms.</p> <h2>3. Historical Context: From IPCC Projections to Satellite Reality</h2> <p>Early IPCC assessments in 2007 and 2013 projected ice‑sheet loss at rates that were later found to be conservative. The 2007 IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (AR5) estimated that Greenland would lose 0.5–1.0 trillion tonnes per year by 2100 under a high‑emissions scenario, while the 2013 Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) projected 1.0–1.5 trillion tonnes per year for Greenland and 0.5–1.0 trillion tonnes per year for Antarctica (IPCC, 2013). These estimates were based largely on climate model simulations and limited observations. The satellite record has revealed a steeper decline, indicating that the ice sheets are responding more quickly to climate forcing than previously modeled. The acceleration is consistent with the increasing frequency and intensity of heat waves, melt seasons, and ocean‑temperature anomalies documented over the same period (Hansen et al., 2020).</p> <p>The 12 trillion‑tonne loss over 47 years represents a significant acceleration relative to those earlier projections, underscoring the urgency of both mitigation and adaptation. The ice‑sheet inertia means that even if emissions were cut dramatically, the loss will continue for decades, as the ice sheets will continue to respond to the legacy of past warming (Bamber et al., 2022). Climate scientists emphasize that the ice‑sheet loss is a key driver of long‑term sea‑level rise and that the inertia of the cryosphere amplifies the lag between emissions and impacts.</p> <h2>4. Sea‑Level Rise Implications</h2> <p>The quantified loss feeds directly into sea‑level rise projections. The 12 trillion‑tonne figure translates to an average rise of about 0.8 mm per year, adding to the 0.5 mm per year already attributed to thermal expansion of seawater (Llovel et al., 2021). When combined, the total global mean sea‑level rise is now projected to reach 1.1–1.3 mm per year by 2030 under a moderate emissions scenario (RCP4.5) and 2.0–2.5 mm per year under a high‑emissions scenario (RCP8.5) (IPCC, 2022). These rates are already reshaping coastal communities worldwide.</p> <p>Coastal planners in the United States and Europe are revising building codes and flood‑plain maps in light of the new data. In the United Kingdom, the Environment Agency has updated its flood risk maps to incorporate the latest sea‑level rise projections, which now include the 12 trillion‑tonne loss as a baseline (Environment Agency, 2023). Insurers are adjusting premiums for properties in vulnerable zones, and some municipalities are accelerating the relocation of critical infrastructure. Indigenous communities in Greenland, whose livelihoods depend on stable ice conditions, are expressing growing concern about the long‑term viability of their traditional practices (Kang et al., 2022). Antarctica has no permanent indigenous population, but scientific and logistical communities operating on the continent are also affected by the changing ice dynamics (Fretwell et al., 2021).</p> <h2>5. Policy Relevance and the Next IPCC Cycle</h2> <p>The timing of this satellite assessment aligns with the next IPCC assessment cycle and the upcoming COP negotiations, making the data highly relevant for international climate policy. Policymakers will need to incorporate the updated ice‑sheet loss into national greenhouse‑gas reduction targets and adaptation strategies. The persistence of ice‑sheet loss, driven by feedbacks such as albedo reduction, ocean warming, and meltwater lubrication, suggests that sea‑level rise will continue to accelerate unless global emissions are curtailed to near‑zero levels (IPCC, 2022). The scientific community calls for independent verification and cross‑validation with other platforms, such as airborne and ground‑based measurements, to refine the uncertainty bounds and improve the reliability of future projections (Bamber et al., 2023).</p> <h2>6. The Human Dimension</h2> <p>The story of the 12 trillion‑tonne loss is not just a scientific milestone; it is a call to action that bridges hard data, policy urgency, and human vulnerability. As the planet’s ice sheets continue to respond to the warming world, the stakes for coastal communities, national economies, and global climate governance have never been higher. The satellites have given us a clear, quantifiable measure of change; now it is up to the international community to translate that knowledge into decisive, sustained action.</p> <p>The 12 trillion‑tonne figure reminds us that the cryosphere is not a passive backdrop but an active participant in the climate system. It stores and releases water, reflects solar radiation, and signals the pace of warming. The satellites have captured its loss in unprecedented detail, and the data now demand that we rethink how we plan, build, and govern in a world where sea levels are rising at an accelerating pace. The next decade will test our willingness to act on the evidence, to reduce emissions, and to protect the most vulnerable among us. The satellites have spoken; it is time for humanity to listen.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ul> <li>Bamber, J. L., & Happer, R.
