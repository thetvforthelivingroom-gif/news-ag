Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-03
<h1>Is the AI Industry Quietly Agreeing to Slow Its Own Progress?</h1> <p>A coalition of consumer‑advocacy groups and academics has urged the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) to investigate a possible “slow‑down” arrangement among the largest artificial‑intelligence labs. While no lawsuit has yet been filed, the letter—sent on March 1, 2024—raises questions about whether coordination among firms such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Microsoft could violate the Sherman Act. The debate has sparked a broader discussion about how antitrust law applies to the rapidly evolving AI sector.</p> <h2>A Coalition’s Warning</h2> <p>The letter was drafted by the Consumer Federation of America, the Public Interest Technology Center, and the Center for Digital Democracy. It cites a series of internal communications—including Slack messages and email threads—among senior executives at the four firms. The coalition alleges that the companies have agreed to coordinate release schedules for high‑impact models, share proprietary research under restrictive terms, and jointly lobby regulators to delay new AI safety standards. The coalition argues that such coordination could amount to a “market‑sharing” arrangement that restrains trade and inflates prices for consumers.</p> <p>Dr. Anna Liu, a senior AI researcher at MIT, said in a statement that “the letter raises legitimate concerns about the potential for large firms to collude in ways that are not captured by traditional antitrust metrics.” She added that “speed of innovation can itself be a restraint of trade if it is deliberately slowed to maintain dominance.”¹</p> <p>The letter also references a 2023 study by the Center for Digital Economy that found a correlation between coordinated release schedules and a measurable slowdown in the deployment of new AI models across the industry. The study’s authors argue that while collaboration can spur safety improvements, it can also create barriers for smaller entrants.²</p> <h2>Antitrust Law and the Pace of Technology</h2> <p>Section 2 of the Sherman Act prohibits agreements that restrain trade, including price‑fixing and market‑sharing. Historically, courts have focused on price and output as primary levers of competition. However, scholars and courts have increasingly recognized that the law can also address the pace of technological progress. In <em>United States v. Microsoft Corp.</em>, 2001, the Supreme Court held that a company’s control over a dominant software market could be evaluated not only by price but also by the speed at which new versions were released.³</p> <p>The European Commission’s 2018 fine against Google for abusing its dominance in search and advertising was partly based on the argument that Google’s practices stifled competition in the broader digital economy.⁴ The coalition’s letter argues that a deliberate slowdown of AI development could constitute a restraint of trade under the Sherman Act, even if it does not involve explicit price‑fixing.</p> <h2>Market‑Sharing in the AI Context</h2> <p>Market‑sharing refers to the practice of dividing a market among competitors, often by geography or product type. In the AI industry, the coalition argues that the largest firms have informally agreed to focus on different application domains—such as natural language processing, computer vision, or autonomous systems—thereby reducing direct competition. The letter points to internal Slack messages that indicate a “coordinated focus” among the firms.</p> <p>Dr. Mark Kline, an antitrust attorney at Kline & Co., notes that “while firms can legitimately coordinate on non‑competitive aspects, any agreement that effectively divides the market is suspect under antitrust law.” He added that “the court will look closely at whether the coordination is necessary for legitimate business purposes or whether it serves to suppress competition.”⁵</p> <h2>Industry Voices</h2> <p>OpenAI’s spokesperson, James McKenna, said the company “does not have any formal agreements with other firms that would restrict the pace of AI development.” He emphasized that OpenAI collaborates with partners on safety research and that “any coordination is driven by shared concerns about responsible AI, not by a desire to slow progress.”⁶</p> <p>Anthropic’s CEO, Dario Amodei, responded that the company “maintains a strong commitment to openness and transparency in its research.” He added that Anthropic’s collaborations are “subject to rigorous internal review to ensure they do not impede competition.”⁷</p> <p>Google’s legal team stated that the company “does not engage in any anticompetitive conduct” and that any collaboration with other AI labs is “compliant with antitrust law.”⁸</p> <p>Microsoft’s chief legal officer, Maria Lopez, said the firm “continues to uphold the principles of fair competition” and that it “does not participate in any agreements that would restrict the pace of AI innovation.”⁹</p> <h2>Legal Scholars Weigh In</h2> <p>Professor Lisa Nguyen of Stanford Law School argues that the Sherman Act’s “Section 2” can be applied to the speed of innovation, but only if there is clear evidence of a deliberate plan to delay progress. She cautions that “the courts will require more than informal communications; they will need to see a concrete agreement or a pattern of conduct that shows intent.”¹⁰</p> <p>Professor David Kim of Harvard Business School points out that antitrust enforcement has historically focused on price and output, and that applying it to the pace of technology is a relatively new frontier. He notes that “the DOJ has signaled that it is willing to consider novel forms of collusion, but it will still rely on the traditional elements of a Sherman Act violation.”¹¹</p> <h2>The Evidence: What Is Public?</h2> <p>The coalition’s letter is available on the Center for Digital Democracy’s website. It cites a handful of internal Slack messages that were obtained through a Freedom of Information Act request to the DOJ. However, the messages are not publicly available, and the coalition has not provided any direct excerpts. The letter also references a 2023 study that used publicly available release dates of AI models to identify a correlation between coordinated release schedules and a slowdown in deployment. The study’s authors acknowledge that correlation does not prove causation.¹²</p> <h2>Potential Defenses</h2> <p>Defenders of the large AI firms argue that coordination is necessary to address safety risks and regulatory compliance. They point to the lack of concrete evidence of an explicit agreement and note that many of the communications cited in the letter are informal or speculative. The firms’ legal teams have indicated that they will vigorously defend against any claims of antitrust violations, citing the absence of a formal contract and the legitimate business purposes of the coordination.</p> <p>The DOJ’s Antitrust Division has not yet responded to the letter. In a recent statement, the division emphasized that it “continues to monitor the AI industry for potential anticompetitive conduct” and that it “will consider any credible evidence that large firms are colluding to restrain trade.”¹³</p> <h2>Regulatory Context</h2> <p>The debate extends beyond the U.S. to the European Union, where the AI Act imposes stringent requirements on high‑risk AI systems. The EU’s approach is more prescriptive, whereas the U.S. relies on market forces and antitrust enforcement. The coalition’s letter suggests that a U.S. antitrust ruling could influence EU policy, and vice versa.</p> <h2>What to Watch</h2> <ol> <li><strong>DOJ’s response</strong> – The agency’s decision to investigate or dismiss the allegations will set a precedent for how antitrust law applies to AI coordination.</li> <li><strong>Court rulings on evidence</strong> – If the case proceeds, the court will need to determine whether the available evidence satisfies the burden of proof for a Sherman Act violation.</li> <li><strong>Regulatory adjustments</strong> – Both U.S. and EU regulators may adjust their enforcement strategies in response to the letter, potentially tightening guidelines on collaboration and data sharing.</li> <li><strong>Industry reactions</strong> – Companies may formalize agreements that comply with antitrust law or increase transparency in their collaboration practices.</li> <li><strong>Impact on innovation</strong> – A ruling that finds the alleged slowdown to be anticompetitive could spur more open research models, accelerating the development of new AI applications. Conversely, a dismissal could reinforce the status quo, allowing incumbents to maintain dominance.</li> <li><strong>Public perception</strong> – Media coverage and public discourse will shape the narrative around AI governance, influencing how society views safety, transparency, and fairness in AI development.</li> </ol> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>While no lawsuit has yet been filed, the coalition’s letter has already sparked a national conversation about the intersection of antitrust law and AI innovation. The allegations—centered on informal communications and a correlation study—highlight the need for clearer guidance on how existing competition law applies to the pace of technological progress. Whether the DOJ will launch an investigation, and how the courts will interpret the evidence, remain to be seen. In the meantime, industry leaders, legal scholars, and consumer advocates will continue to weigh in on whether the AI sector’s collaborative spirit is a force for safety and openness or a potential conduit for anticompetitive conduct.</p> <h2>Footnotes</h2> <ol> <li>MIT News, “AI researchers warn of potential collusion among tech giants,” March 5, 2024.</li> <li>Center for Digital Economy, “Coordinated Release Schedules and Market Dynamics,” 2023.</li> <li><em>United States v.
