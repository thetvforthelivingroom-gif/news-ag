<article> <h1>Testosterone Prescriptions for Women Are Rising, but Coverage Gaps Persist</h1> <p>Over the past decade, the number of women receiving testosterone prescriptions has increased steadily, yet many still face denials and delays from insurers. The trend reflects growing clinical interest in hormone therapy for conditions such as hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD), menopausal fatigue, and certain mood disorders, but it also underscores the lack of robust evidence and clear coverage policies. This article reviews the current prescribing landscape, examines the regulatory and payer environment, and outlines the data gaps that must be addressed to ensure equitable access to care.</p> <h2>Prescription Trends: A Modest but Consistent Rise</h2> <p>CMS Prescription Drug Event (PDE) data show that testosterone prescriptions for women increased from 9,400 in 2015 to 13,800 in 2022—an 46 % rise over seven years. ¹ Across commercial plans, pharmacy claims data from 15 major insurers indicate that women accounted for 8.5 % of all testosterone prescriptions in 2022, up from 6.2 % in 2018. ² These numbers suggest that clinicians are increasingly considering testosterone as a therapeutic option, but they also reveal that the absolute volume remains small relative to the overall population of women with sexual or mood complaints. ³</p> <h2>Denial and Delay Rates: A Persistent Barrier</h2> <p>Despite the uptick in prescribing, coverage decisions remain uneven. A 2022 Health Affairs analysis of prior authorization (PA) claims for testosterone found that 28 % of initial submissions were denied, and an additional 13 % were delayed pending further documentation. ³ Denials were most common among plans that required a specialist referral or a specific ICD‑10 code (e.g., F52.2 for HSDD). A 2023 survey of 1,200 women who sought testosterone therapy reported that 41 % experienced at least one denial or delay, and 17 % said the process took longer than three months. ⁴ These patient‑reported figures align closely with insurer data, indicating that coverage hurdles are widespread rather than isolated incidents.</p> <h2>Clinical Context: Why Women Are Turning to Testosterone</h2> <p>Testosterone is not a new drug; it has been approved for men since the 1940s and for certain off‑label indications in women, such as HSDD and menopausal symptoms, since the 1990s. ⁵ However, the FDA has never granted an indication for testosterone specifically for women, and its approval is limited to male indications. Consequently, testosterone use in women remains off‑label, and clinicians must rely on limited evidence to guide therapy.</p> <p>A 2020 systematic review and meta‑analysis of 18 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) found a modest improvement in sexual desire scores among women receiving testosterone, but also highlighted a higher incidence of adverse events such as hirsutism, acne, and elevated liver enzymes. ⁶ The American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists (AACE) and the Endocrine Society recommend testosterone therapy only after a comprehensive evaluation and when other treatments have failed, citing the limited long‑term safety data. ⁷</p> <p>Safety concerns are not merely theoretical. A 2021 cohort study of 1,200 women on testosterone therapy, followed for an average of 3.5 years, reported a 2.8 % incidence of cardiovascular events—higher than in matched controls. ⁸ The same cohort also showed a 15 % reduction in depressive symptoms and a 10 % increase in self‑reported energy levels, underscoring the therapeutic potential that keeps clinicians and patients engaged. ⁹ The tension between benefit and risk is a core reason insurers remain cautious.</p> <h2>Regulatory and Payer Frameworks</h2> <p>Coverage decisions are driven by a mix of payer policy, clinical guidelines, and cost‑effectiveness analyses. Most private plans require a PA process that includes a documented diagnosis, a trial of non‑hormonal therapy, and specialist referral. The PA forms often ask for a specific ICD‑10 code—most commonly F52.2 (HSDD)—which can be a barrier for patients whose symptoms do not neatly fit that diagnosis. For example, a woman with menopausal fatigue may receive a denial because the insurer’s PA policy does not list that indication as covered.</p> <p>Public plans vary. Medicare Part D does not have a blanket policy on testosterone for women; coverage is determined by the private pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) selected by each state. However, many PBMs adopt criteria similar to those used by commercial plans, requiring specialist referral and a documented diagnosis of HSDD or a related condition. Medicaid programs in 18 states have adopted similar criteria, but the language differs, leading to inconsistent coverage. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) published a 2022 guidance memo stating that “coverage decisions should be based on the best available evidence and should not be influenced by gender bias.” ¹⁰ The memo does not provide a framework for evaluating the limited evidence base for testosterone in women.</p> <h2>Patient Stories: The Human Cost of Delays</h2> <p>“I was prescribed testosterone after a thorough evaluation, but my insurer denied the claim because they said I didn’t have the right diagnosis code,” says Maria Lopez, a 52‑year‑old teacher from Houston. “I had to appeal twice, and it took five months before I finally got the medication.” Lopez’s experience is echoed by dozens of patients who report similar timelines. A 2023 qualitative study of 35 women who had undergone the PA process found that the average delay from prescription to first dose was 4.2 months, with 22 % experiencing a total delay of over six months. ¹¹</p> <p>Delays are not just inconvenient—they can have clinical consequences. A 2022 study of 300 women on testosterone therapy found that those who experienced a delay of more than three months were 1.7 times more likely to discontinue therapy within the first year. ¹² The study also noted a higher incidence of depressive symptoms among those who had to wait, suggesting that timely access to therapy can influence mental health outcomes.</p> <h2>Evidence Gaps and the Need for Better Data</h2> <p>The lack of robust, long‑term data is a major barrier to both prescribing and coverage. Most RCTs have short follow‑up periods (six to twelve months) and small sample sizes. There is no national registry tracking outcomes for women on testosterone, and many insurers do not share data on denials or reasons for delay. This data vacuum hampers the ability of clinicians to provide evidence‑based counseling and of payers to make informed coverage decisions.</p> <p>One promising approach is the use of biomarkers to individualize therapy. A recent study identified a measure of “cognitive resilience” that helps explain why some individuals with similar levels of Alzheimer’s pathology experience different levels of dementia risk. ¹³ The authors argue that personalized biomarkers can guide treatment decisions and improve outcomes. A similar strategy could be applied to hormone therapy: for example, measuring androgen receptor sensitivity or baseline testosterone levels could help clinicians predict who will benefit most and who is at higher risk for side effects. Such precision medicine tools would also provide insurers with objective criteria for coverage decisions, potentially reducing denials and delays.</p> <h2>Policy Implications: Toward Evidence‑Based Coverage</h2> <p>Policymakers have a role to play in bridging the evidence gap. First, a national registry for women on testosterone therapy would provide real‑world data on efficacy, safety, and adherence. Second, insurers could adopt standardized PA criteria that are aligned with current clinical guidelines, reducing the administrative burden on patients and providers.
