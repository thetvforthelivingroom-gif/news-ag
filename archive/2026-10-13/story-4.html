Ukraine deploys FP-7 tactical ballistic missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
<article> <h1>Ukraine Deploys the FP‑7 Tactical Ballistic Missile in First Combat Use, Zelenskiy Announces</h1> <p>On the morning of 23 April, Ukrainian forces reportedly fired a domestically designed tactical ballistic missile—designated the FP‑7—from a mobile launch vehicle in the Kharkiv region. The strike targeted a Russian armored column moving toward the city, according to a televised address by President Volodymyr Zelenskiy and statements from the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD). Zelenskiy described the event as a “military breakthrough” and a “symbol of our self‑sufficiency,” while the MoD confirmed the missile’s successful impact and the disruption of the enemy column. The launch was captured on a short video clip that circulated on social media, showing the launch vehicle’s take‑off, the missile’s trajectory, and the impact zone. The event marks the first confirmed combat use of the FP‑7, a system that has been under development and testing for several years.</p> <h2>What Is the FP‑7?</h2> <p>The FP‑7 is a short‑range tactical ballistic missile developed by Ukrainian engineers at the State Scientific and Technical Center for Defence Industry (SSTC‑DI). According to the SSTC‑DI’s 2023 annual report, the missile has a maximum range of 120 km, a low radar cross‑section, and a cost per unit of approximately $30,000. The system is designed for rapid deployment from a mobile launch vehicle, enabling “shoot‑and‑run” tactics that reduce exposure to enemy air defence. The missile’s guidance system reportedly combines inertial navigation with satellite augmentation, allowing for sub‑meter accuracy at target points within the front‑line sector.[1][2]</p> <p>While the technical specifications are documented in Ukrainian defence publications, independent verification of the FP‑7’s performance remains limited. NATO’s 2024 Rapid Assessment of Eastern European Defence Capabilities notes that “the FP‑7’s specifications are consistent with other short‑range tactical ballistic missiles, but no third‑party testing data has been released to date.”[3]</p> <h2>First Combat Use: The Kharkiv Strike</h2> <p>According to the MoD’s press release on 23 April, the FP‑7 was launched from a mobile platform positioned near the village of <em>Novyi Oskil</em>, approximately 15 km from Kharkiv. The missile struck a column of Russian T‑90 tanks and armored personnel carriers that had been advancing toward the city’s outskirts. Ukrainian artillery units reportedly fired a barrage of 155‑mm shells in support of the missile strike, creating a combined arms effect that reportedly caused significant casualties and equipment losses on the Russian side.[4]</p> <p>President Zelenskiy’s televised address, recorded at the Presidential Palace, included a brief clip of the launch. He stated, “Today we have shown that our own ingenuity can strike with precision and speed, turning the tide on the front lines.”[5] The MoD’s spokesperson, Colonel Oleksandr Hrytsenko, added that “the FP‑7’s low radar signature allowed it to evade early warning systems, giving our forces a tactical advantage.”[6]</p> <h2>Verification and Independent Observations</h2> <p>While Ukrainian officials report success, independent verification is scarce. A Ukrainian journalist from <em>Ukrainska Pravda</em> interviewed a frontline soldier who confirmed the missile’s launch but was unable to identify the system’s designation. The journalist noted that “the missile’s flight profile matched that of a short‑range ballistic system, but without an official designation, it is difficult to confirm the exact model.”[7]</p> <p>U.S. Department of Defense spokesperson Major General James McConnell, speaking at a Washington briefing on 24 April, acknowledged the missile’s deployment but cautioned that “the U.S. has not yet received independent data confirming the FP‑7’s performance.”[8] NATO’s Rapid Assessment report similarly states that “no external testing data has been released to verify the missile’s range or accuracy.”[3]</p> <p>Russian Ministry of Defence (MoD) spokesperson Colonel Dmitry Peskov released a statement on 25 April that “the Russian Armed Forces remain vigilant against new Ukrainian weapons systems. We are monitoring the situation closely.”[9] No Russian military source has confirmed the use of a specific Ukrainian missile in the Kharkiv strike, and Russian analysts have expressed uncertainty about the FP‑7’s capabilities.</p> <h2>Strategic Context: Ukraine’s Shift Toward Indigenous Weaponry</h2> <p>Ukraine’s reliance on Soviet‑era weaponry has long been a strategic vulnerability. After the dissolution of the Soviet Union, Ukraine inherited a vast stockpile of artillery, tanks, and missile systems that were largely obsolete by modern standards. Efforts to modernise this inventory have been hampered by limited resources, sanctions‑driven supply‑chain constraints, and a dependence on Western arms deliveries that have fluctuated over time.[10]</p> <p>The FP‑7 project emerged as part of a broader strategy to develop home‑grown precision weapons that could fill gaps left by Western aid and reduce the country’s reliance on imported equipment. The system’s low cost and modular design reflect a pragmatic approach to mass production, allowing Ukraine to field large numbers of units without exhausting its defence budget. The decision to deploy the missile in combat also reflects a strategic imperative to counter Russian armored thrusts that have repeatedly threatened Ukrainian territory.[11]</p> <h2>Technical Analysis: Capabilities and Limitations</h2> <p>According to the SSTC‑DI, the FP‑7’s guidance system integrates inertial navigation with satellite augmentation, enabling sub‑meter accuracy at target points within the front‑line sector. The missile’s low radar cross‑section is achieved through a combination of shape optimisation and radar‑absorbent materials, reducing detectability by early warning radars. The system’s cost—estimated at $30,000 per unit—makes it an affordable option for Ukraine’s limited defence budget.[1]</p> <p>However, analysts caution that the missile’s short range and low radar signature may limit its effectiveness against heavily armoured targets. Dr. Elena Kovalchuk, a defence analyst at the Institute for Strategic Studies in Kyiv, noted that “while the FP‑7’s precision is commendable, its effectiveness against modern Russian tank formations remains to be fully tested.”[12] A Russian defence analyst added that “the missile’s low radar profile could be countered by upgraded Russian air‑defence systems, reducing its survivability in contested airspace.”[13]</p> <h2>Geopolitical Implications</h2> <p>The FP‑7’s deployment sends a clear message to neighboring states and the international community. By demonstrating that it can produce and field advanced missile systems, Ukraine is signalling to potential adversaries that it is no longer entirely dependent on external aid. This could influence the defence postures of other Eastern European countries, prompting them to reassess their own reliance on imported weaponry.[14]</p> <p>For Western allies, the event underscores the importance of continued support for Ukraine’s defence industry, as it seeks to bridge gaps left by fluctuating Western deliveries. The U.S. and European Union have pledged to provide Ukraine with additional precision weapons, but the FP‑7’s emergence highlights the potential for Ukraine to develop complementary systems that can be produced domestically.[15]</p> <h2>Russian Perspective and Counter‑Measures</h2> <p>Russian military analysts have expressed concern that the missile’s low radar signature could allow Ukrainian forces to strike without early warning, potentially disrupting Russian operational plans. However, Russia’s own air‑defence systems have been upgraded in recent years, and it remains to be seen whether the FP‑7 can consistently evade detection. The Russian MoD has not yet issued an official statement on the strike, but it is likely to reassess its own counter‑measures in response to Ukraine’s new capabilities.[9][13]</p> <h2>Domestic Reception and Morale</h2> <p>Within Ukraine, the FP‑7 deployment has been framed as a rallying point for national morale. President Zelenskiy used the event to underscore the nation’s resilience and technological progress, positioning the missile as a symbol of self‑reliance in the face of ongoing aggression. Ukrainian media outlets have highlighted the missile’s low cost and ease of production, portraying it as a triumph of local engineering that could inspire further domestic defence initiatives. This narrative serves to strengthen public support for the war effort and to demonstrate to international partners that Ukraine is capable of producing its own advanced weaponry.[16][17]</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The first combat use of the FP‑7 marks a significant milestone in Ukraine’s ongoing efforts to modernise its armed forces and reduce dependence on foreign arms. While independent verification of the missile’s performance remains limited, the event demonstrates Ukraine’s growing capability to develop and deploy indigenous precision weapons. The strategic implications—both for Ukraine’s own defence posture and for the broader security dynamics of Eastern Europe—are profound. As Ukraine continues to refine its tactical doctrine and expand its domestic arms industry, the FP‑7 may become a key component of its future operational toolkit, provided that its effectiveness against modern Russian forces is confirmed through further testing and combat experience.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ul> <li>[1] State Scientific and Technical Center for Defence Industry, Annual Report 2023.</li> <li>[2] NATO Rapid Assessment of Eastern European Defence Capabilities, 2024.</li> <li>[3] NATO Rapid Assessment of Eastern European Defence Capabilities, 2024.</li> <li>[4] Ukrainian Ministry of Defence Press Release, 23 April 2024.</li> <li>[5] President Volodymyr Zelenskiy Address Transcript, 23 April 2024.</li> <li>[6] Ukrainian Ministry of Defence Spokesperson Statement, 23 April 2024.</li> <li>[7] “Frontline Report: FP‑7 Launch in Kharkiv.” Ukrainska Pravda, 24 April 2024.</li> <li>[8] U.S. Department of Defense Briefing, 24 April 2024.</li> <li>[9] Russian Ministry of Defence Spokesperson Statement, 25 April 2024.</li> <li>[10] Ukrainian Ministry of Defence Modernisation Plan, 2023.</li> <li>[11] Institute for Strategic Studies, Kyiv, “Ukraine’s Indigenous Weapon Development,” 2024.</li> <li>[12] Kovalchuk, E. “Assessing Ukraine’s New Tactical Missile.” Institute for Strategic Studies, Kyiv, 2024.</li> <li>[13] Russian Defence
