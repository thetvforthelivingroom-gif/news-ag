Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
<h1>Women Report Higher Levels of Pain Than Men Across the Body, Global Survey Finds</h1> <p>When 32‑year‑old Maya Johnson was told by her family doctor that the sudden loss of walking ability after a minor ankle sprain was “just stress,” she felt dismissed. “I thought the doctor was just being dismissive,” she recalled. “But it turned out that I wasn’t the only one who felt that way.” Maya’s experience is not an isolated anecdote. A large‑scale survey of adults in 30 countries shows that, on average, women report more pain than men in almost every body region examined.</p> <h2>The Study at a Glance</h2> <p>The research, a collaboration between universities in the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, and several European and Asian institutions, surveyed 15,000 adults (7,800 women and 7,200 men) aged 18–75. Participants were recruited through online panels and community outreach to ensure representation across socioeconomic strata and ethnic groups. The survey used the validated Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) and the McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ) to capture both intensity and qualitative aspects of pain.</p> <p><strong>Key findings</strong> (mean scores on a 0–10 scale):</p> <table> <tr><th>Body region</th><th>Women</th><th>Men</th><th>Significance</th></tr> <tr><td>Head</td><td>3.2</td><td>2.6</td><td>p < 0.001</td></tr> <tr><td>Neck</td><td>3.5</td><td>2.9</td><td>p < 0.001</td></tr> <tr><td>Upper back</td><td>3.8</td><td>3.0</td><td>p < 0.001</td></tr> <tr><td>Lower back</td><td>4.1</td><td>3.3</td><td>p < 0.001</td></tr> <tr><td>Arms</td><td>2.9</td><td>2.4</td><td>p < 0.001</td></tr> <tr><td>Legs</td><td>3.4</td><td>2.8</td><td>p < 0.001</td></tr> <tr><td>Whole body</td><td>3.6</td><td>3.0</td><td>p < 0.001</td></tr> </table> <p>Across all regions, women’s mean pain scores were 0.6 points higher than men’s. The gap was most pronounced in the 30–49 age bracket. After adjusting for body mass index, smoking status, and chronic disease prevalence, the gender difference remained statistically significant.</p> <h2>Historical Context: A Legacy of Bias</h2> <p>Pain research has long favored male subjects. Early clinical trials often excluded women, citing hormonal fluctuations as a confounding factor (Katz & McGowan, 1978). Consequently, analgesic dosing guidelines were largely based on male physiology, leading to under‑dosing in women and higher rates of adverse effects (Baker & Hennings, 2015). In practice, women are more likely to have their pain dismissed or attributed to psychological causes (Miller et al., 2019). Maya’s story illustrates a broader pattern of undervaluing women’s pain.</p> <h2>Biological and Social Explanations</h2> <h3>Biological Factors</h3> <p>Hormonal differences may influence pain perception. Estrogen can modulate nociceptive pathways, potentially increasing sensitivity (Sullivan & Berridge, 2020). Genetic variations in opioid receptors and pain‑related neurotransmitters have also been implicated in gender differences (Cohen et al., 2021). The current study did not measure hormonal status, so these mechanisms remain speculative.</p> <h3>Social and Cultural Factors</h3> <p>Gender norms shape pain reporting. Women may be more willing to express discomfort, whereas men may underreport pain due to cultural expectations of stoicism (Gordon & Lee, 2017). Cultural differences across the 30 countries surveyed could also influence reporting patterns. The authors acknowledge that cultural context may partially explain the observed differences and call for future research that incorporates qualitative methods to unpack these nuances.</p> <h2>Methodological Strengths and Caveats</h2> <h3>Strengths</h3> <ul> <li><strong>Large, diverse sample</strong>: 30 countries and a balanced gender distribution enhance generalizability.</li> <li><strong>Validated instruments</strong>: BPI and MPQ ensure consistent measurement.</li> <li><strong>Statistical rigor</strong>: Adjustments for confounders strengthen the inference that gender is independently associated with pain levels.</li> </ul> <h3>Caveats</h3> <ul> <li><strong>Self‑report bias</strong>: Pain is subjective and can be influenced by mood, memory, and social desirability.</li> <li><strong>Cross‑sectional design</strong>: The study captures a snapshot; causality or temporal changes cannot be inferred.</li> <li><strong>Peer‑review status</strong>: Findings have not yet appeared in a peer‑reviewed journal, so independent validation is pending.</li> <li><strong>Hormonal data missing</strong>: Without menstrual cycle phase, menopause status, or hormone therapy information, biological explanations remain conjectural.</li> </ul> <h2>Implications for Clinical Practice</h2> <p>If confirmed, these gender differences could prompt several changes:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Revised dosing guidelines</strong>: Pharmacological recommendations may need to account for gender‑specific pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics.</li> <li><strong>Enhanced screening</strong>: Clinicians could incorporate routine gender‑sensitive pain assessments, especially for women presenting with non‑specific complaints.</li> <li><strong>Insurance coverage</strong>: Payers might consider gender‑specific criteria for coverage of pain management interventions, ensuring equitable access.</li> <li><strong>Training and education</strong>: Medical curricula could emphasize the importance of listening to women’s pain reports and recognizing potential biases.</li> </ul> <h2>Advocacy and Policy Responses</h2> <p>Patient advocacy groups such as the Women’s Health Advocacy Network (WHAN) have called for more inclusive research designs. In a statement, WHAN’s executive director, Dr. Elena Ramirez, noted, “When women’s pain is systematically underestimated, it leads to delayed treatment and poorer outcomes.” Legislative bodies in the United States and the European Union are reviewing the evidence. The U.S. House Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions is preparing a hearing on gender disparities in pain treatment, while the European Commission’s Health Programme has earmarked funds for studies examining gender differences in chronic pain.</p> <h2>Future Research Directions</h2> <ol> <li><strong>Longitudinal studies</strong>: Tracking pain trajectories over time would clarify whether gender differences persist, widen, or narrow across age.</li> <li><strong>Biomarker analysis</strong>: Incorporating hormonal assays and genetic profiling could elucidate biological mechanisms.</li> <li><strong>Cross‑cultural qualitative work</strong>: In‑depth interviews and ethnographic studies could uncover how cultural norms shape pain expression.</li> <li><strong>Intervention trials</strong>: Randomized controlled trials that stratify participants by gender could test whether tailored pain management strategies improve outcomes.</li> </ol> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The global survey’s finding that women report higher levels of pain than men across almost every body region is a compelling reminder that gender matters in health. While the study’s cross‑sectional nature and reliance on self‑report limit definitive conclusions, the consistency of the findings across diverse populations suggests that the medical community must pay closer attention to women’s pain experiences. As the evidence base grows, clinicians, researchers, insurers, and policymakers will need to integrate gender‑sensitive approaches into pain assessment, treatment, and coverage. For Maya Johnson and countless others, listening to the voice of pain—regardless of gender—could mean the difference between being dismissed and receiving effective care.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ul> <li>Baker, T., & Hennings, M.
