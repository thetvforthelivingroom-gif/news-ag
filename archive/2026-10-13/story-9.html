Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
<h1>Women Report More Pain Than Men Across the Body, Global Study Finds</h1> <p>A landmark analysis that pooled data from more than 70 countries has shown that women consistently report higher levels of pain throughout the body than men. Published in the <em>Journal of Pain Research</em>, the study challenges long‑standing assumptions that pain experience is largely gender‑neutral and calls for a re‑examination of clinical guidelines, insurance coverage, and research priorities. The authors caution that the findings stem from self‑reported measures and cross‑sectional data, yet the magnitude of the gender gap—spanning headaches, musculoskeletal discomfort, and visceral pain—suggests that biological, psychological, and cultural factors converge to shape pain perception and expression.</p> <p>The study drew on the International Pain Outcomes Survey (IPOS), a standardized questionnaire administered to 20,000 adults aged 18–65 who were recruited through primary‑care practices, community health centers, and online platforms. Participants answered 12 items that assessed pain intensity, frequency, and interference with daily activities over the past month. The authors then compared mean pain scores between men and women, adjusting for age, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, and country‑level health indicators. The analysis revealed that women’s average pain score was 1.8 points higher on a 0–10 scale, a difference that persisted across all age groups and geographic regions (p < 0.001). This result is reported in the study’s abstract and forms the basis of the article’s headline.</p> <p>Beyond the overall mean difference, the authors explored pain across specific body regions. Women reported significantly higher pain in the lower back (mean difference = 1.2), neck (0.9), and joints (1.1), while men’s pain was slightly higher only in the abdomen (0.4). These regional patterns mirror prior laboratory studies that have documented sex differences in nociceptive thresholds and inflammatory responses [2]. The authors also noted that women’s pain was more likely to interfere with sleep, mood, and work productivity, underscoring the broader functional impact of gendered pain experiences.</p> <p>While the study’s design strengthens the generalizability of the findings, the authors acknowledge several limitations. First, pain was measured through self‑report, which can be influenced by individual pain thresholds, mood states, and social desirability. Second, the cross‑sectional nature of the data precludes causal inference; it remains unclear whether biological factors, cultural norms, or a combination of both drive the observed gender gap. Finally, the sample, though large, was not fully representative of all populations, with under‑representation of older adults and rural communities in some regions. These caveats are explicitly stated in the discussion section and serve to temper the enthusiasm for immediate policy changes.</p> <p>Historically, pain research and clinical trials have been skewed toward male participants, a bias that has shaped analgesic development, dosing guidelines, and reimbursement policies. A 2015 review highlighted that 70 % of preclinical studies used male animals, and that many early‑phase trials excluded women or failed to analyze data by sex [3]. This legacy has contributed to a knowledge gap regarding how women metabolize opioids, respond to non‑opioid analgesics, and experience chronic pain syndromes. The new global study adds weight to calls for sex‑specific research designs and for the inclusion of women in all phases of clinical investigation.</p> <p>Biologically, several mechanisms may underlie the heightened pain sensitivity reported by women. Hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle can modulate pain thresholds, while estrogen has been shown to influence the expression of pain‑related genes in the spinal cord and brain [4]. Additionally, women are more likely to develop conditions such as fibromyalgia and temporomandibular disorders, which are characterized by widespread pain and central sensitization. Pharmacologically, women often metabolize opioids more slowly, leading to higher plasma concentrations and potentially greater side‑effects, whereas some non‑opioid analgesics (e.g., NSAIDs) may be less effective in women due to differences in prostaglandin synthesis pathways [5]. These biological insights dovetail with the study’s observation that women’s pain interferes more with functional outcomes.</p> <p>Cultural norms also shape pain reporting. In many societies, stoicism is valorized in men, leading to under‑reporting of discomfort, whereas women may be socialized to express pain more openly. A qualitative study from Japan found that men were less likely to seek medical care for back pain, whereas women reported higher levels of pain but also higher rates of help‑seeking behavior [6]. The IPOS data corroborate this pattern, showing that women were more likely to report pain that interfered with sleep and work, and that they were also more likely to seek medical attention. These findings suggest that cultural expectations can amplify or dampen the visibility of pain across genders.</p> <p>From a clinical perspective, the gender gap in pain reporting has practical implications. Physicians may need to adopt gender‑sensitive screening tools that account for differential pain expression. For instance, the Brief Pain Inventory could be supplemented with questions that assess pain interference specific to women’s common pain sites. Moreover, treatment algorithms should incorporate sex‑specific pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, ensuring that dosing regimens are tailored to women’s metabolic profiles. In the realm of policy, insurers could use the data to justify coverage of gender‑specific pain management programs, such as multidisciplinary rehabilitation for women with chronic musculoskeletal pain.</p> <p>Patient advocacy groups have seized on the study to push for more inclusive research and equitable care. The Women’s Health Advocacy Coalition released a statement calling for the integration of gender as a biological variable in all pain research, citing the IPOS findings as evidence of systemic neglect. Meanwhile, the International Association for the Study of Pain has announced a new task force to develop guidelines that explicitly address sex differences in pain assessment and treatment. These movements reflect a broader shift toward precision medicine, where individual characteristics—including sex—inform clinical decisions.</p> <p>Looking ahead, the authors call for longitudinal studies that track pain trajectories over time, as well as mechanistic research that identifies biomarkers of pain sensitivity in women. Advances in neuroimaging and genomics may uncover sex‑specific neural circuits that mediate pain perception, offering targets for novel therapeutics. Additionally, cross‑cultural studies that integrate qualitative data can illuminate how societal norms shape pain expression, thereby informing culturally competent care. Ultimately, the study underscores that pain is not a neutral experience; it is filtered through a complex interplay of biology, culture, and individual history.</p> <p>In sum, the global analysis confirms that women report more pain than men across the body, a finding that has significant ramifications for clinical practice, health policy, and research priorities. While self‑report and cross‑sectional design limit causal claims, the consistency of the gender gap across regions and age groups signals a need for systemic change. By integrating sex‑specific considerations into research, guidelines, and insurance coverage, the medical community can move toward more equitable and effective pain management for all patients.</p>
