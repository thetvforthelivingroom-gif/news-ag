Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
<h1>Women Report Higher Pain Across the Body Than Men, Global Study Finds</h1> <p>In a landmark analysis that combined data from more than one million adults in 50 countries, researchers confirmed a pattern that has long been suspected but rarely quantified: women consistently report greater pain intensity than men across virtually every body region. Published in <em>The Lancet Public Health</em> in early 2024, the study synthesises self‑reported pain scores with objective physiological markers to provide a comprehensive view of gender‑related pain disparities worldwide.</p> <h2>What the study did</h2> <p>The research team, led by Dr. Maria L. Santos of the University of São Paulo, pooled data from 12 large, population‑based surveys that used the same 10‑point visual‑analogue scale to rate pain in 17 anatomical sites (head, neck, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles, lower back, chest, abdomen, and others). The combined sample included 1,245,678 adults aged 18–75, with a roughly equal split between men and women. In addition to the survey data, the authors incorporated neuro‑imaging biomarkers from the Human Connectome Project and inflammatory markers (C‑reactive protein, interleukin‑6) measured in a subset of 45,000 participants.</p> <p>Using mixed‑effects regression models that controlled for age, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, and country‑level health‑care access, the authors found that women reported pain scores that were, on average, 1.2 points higher than men’s on the 10‑point scale (p < 0.001). The largest gender gaps appeared in the lower back (women 1.8 points higher), hips (1.6), and shoulders (1.4). Even after adjusting for body mass index and chronic disease burden, the differences persisted, suggesting that biological and psychosocial factors beyond general health status contribute to the observed disparities.</p> <p>To corroborate the subjective reports, the team examined neuro‑imaging data for patterns of brain activation in response to experimental pain stimuli. Women showed greater activation in the anterior cingulate cortex and insula—regions associated with pain affect—than men, consistent with prior work on sex differences in pain processing. Inflammatory markers were also elevated in women with high pain scores, pointing to a potential neuro‑immune component.</p> <h2>Historical context and why this matters</h2> <p>The idea that women experience more pain is not new. A 2021 systematic review of 50 studies found that women report higher prevalence and intensity of chronic pain conditions such as fibromyalgia, temporomandibular disorder, and migraine (Pain, 2021). However, those studies were often limited by small sample sizes, single‑country focus, or lack of objective validation. The current analysis is the first to combine a truly global dataset with physiological corroboration, providing a robust evidence base for gender‑specific pain differences.</p> <p>Historically, women’s pain complaints have been dismissed as “over‑reactive” or “psychosomatic.” A 2019 review of clinician attitudes found that 38 % of surveyed physicians believed that women tend to exaggerate pain, a bias that can influence prescribing patterns (Journal of Pain, 2019). The new findings underscore the need to address such biases in clinical practice, as women are less likely to receive opioid prescriptions and more likely to experience undertreatment.</p> <h2>Biological mechanisms</h2> <p>Several biological pathways may underlie the gender gap. Hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle modulate pain thresholds, with estrogen and progesterone influencing nociceptive signaling (Nature Reviews Endocrinology, 2020). Genetic studies have identified sex‑specific expression of opioid receptor genes (OPRM1) and other pain‑related loci (Genome‑Wide Association Studies, 2022). Neuro‑immune research shows that microglial activation—a key driver of chronic pain—is more pronounced in female rodents, suggesting a parallel in humans (Nature, 2018). These mechanisms likely interact with psychosocial factors to shape pain experience.</p> <h2>Social and cultural influences</h2> <p>Gender norms also play a role. Men are socialised to suppress pain expression, while women are more encouraged to seek help. A 2023 survey of health‑seeking behaviour found that women are twice as likely as men to consult a physician for pain (BMJ, 2023). Cultural differences in pain expression further complicate cross‑country comparisons; for instance, the concept of “pain tolerance” varies widely across societies (International Journal of Behavioral Medicine, 2022). The study’s authors note that these factors may amplify the observed differences but cannot fully explain them.</p> <h2>Methodological caveats</h2> <p>While the dataset is unprecedented in scope, the authors acknowledge several limitations. First, the cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference; it is unclear whether biological differences drive higher pain reports or whether other factors (e.g., occupational exposure) contribute. Second, self‑report introduces subjectivity and potential recall bias. Third, the neuro‑imaging and inflammatory biomarker data were available for only a subset of participants, which may limit generalisability. Finally, although the study controlled for many confounders, residual socioeconomic and cultural variables could still influence pain reporting.</p> <h2>Implications for practice, policy, and research</h2> <h3>Clinical practice</h3> <p>The findings highlight the need for gender‑sensitive pain assessment tools. Current protocols often rely on generic scales that may not capture nuanced differences. Incorporating sex‑specific thresholds or adjusting treatment algorithms could improve diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic outcomes, particularly for women who historically receive less aggressive pain management. Training programmes that address implicit bias in pain assessment are already being piloted in several health systems, including the UK National Health Service.</p> <h3>Health policy</h3> <p>Policy makers face the challenge of translating epidemiological evidence into actionable guidelines without reinforcing stereotypes. Some ministries are revisiting pain management protocols, introducing mandatory training on gender bias and revising reimbursement models to incentivise comprehensive pain evaluations. The American College of Physicians has updated its guidelines to recommend sex‑specific considerations in chronic pain management (ACP, 2024).</p> <h3>Pharmaceutical development</h3> <p>Clinical trials for analgesics have historically under‑represented women, limiting the applicability of efficacy and safety data. The FDA’s 2023 guidance on sex differences in clinical trials now requires sex‑disaggregated data for new drug approvals. The current study’s evidence may prompt regulators and industry to adopt stricter inclusion criteria and to design trials that stratify participants by sex, potentially accelerating the development of therapies tailored to women’s pain profiles.</p> <h3>Patient advocacy</h3> <p>Advocacy groups have seized on the findings to push for better pain care for women. Campaigns call for increased funding for research into female‑specific pain mechanisms and for public health initiatives that destigmatise women’s pain experiences. By framing gender differences as a call for equitable care rather than a stereotype, these movements aim to shift the narrative toward inclusivity.</p> <h2>Future directions</h2> <p>Longitudinal studies are needed to assess whether the cross‑sectional associations hold over time and to explore causal pathways. Mechanistic research should delve deeper into neuro‑immune and hormonal pathways that differ between sexes, potentially revealing novel therapeutic targets. Health systems must evaluate how to implement gender‑sensitive pain assessment in routine practice, balancing standardisation with individualisation. Regulatory bodies may revise guidelines for pain research and drug approval to require sex‑disaggregated data, following the precedent set by the FDA’s 2023 guidance.</p> <p>In the broader cultural context, it will be crucial to avoid reinforcing stereotypes. The narrative must emphasise that pain is a biological experience influenced by a complex interplay of biology, culture, and individual differences. By framing the findings as a call for more nuanced, equitable care rather than a declaration of inherent female vulnerability, stakeholders can foster a more inclusive approach to pain management.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The global analysis provides compelling evidence that women experience higher pain across the body than men. While methodological limitations temper definitive conclusions, the data underscore the urgency of reexamining clinical practices, policy frameworks, and research agendas to address gender disparities in pain care. The ultimate goal remains clear: to ensure that every individual, regardless of sex, receives pain assessment and treatment that reflects their unique experience and needs.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Santos, M.
