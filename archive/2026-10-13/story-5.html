Stocks advance after Bessent says talks with China were successful before Trump

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
Stocks surged on Tuesday after U.S. Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen announced that the United States and China had made “significant progress” in their trade talks. The headline lifted markets, but investors questioned whether the optimism would translate into concrete policy changes. The rally, driven by a diplomatic signal rather than a definitive policy shift, illustrates how high‑stakes diplomacy can temporarily sway investor sentiment amid the ongoing U.S.–China trade dispute. While the announcement injected optimism, analysts warned that the statement is vague and may serve more as a diplomatic cue than evidence of a breakthrough agreement.

What happened

The day after Yellen’s remarks, the S&P 500, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, and the Nasdaq Composite all posted gains, and trading volumes spiked across the board. Technology and manufacturing stocks—sectors highly sensitive to U.S.–China trade dynamics—were among the most active. Investors seemed enthusiastic about the possibility of easing tariff pressures. However, the rally was short‑lived. As the market digested the lack of concrete details in Yellen’s statement, the gains largely reversed in subsequent sessions. The episode underscores how a single diplomatic comment can trigger a temporary shift in sentiment when the broader policy environment remains uncertain.[1]

Yellen, who has served as Treasury Secretary since 2021, described the discussions with Chinese officials as “significant progress” in a brief statement released early on the morning of the rally. The statement came just days before a highly anticipated meeting between the U.S. and China to address a range of bilateral issues, including trade, technology, and geopolitical tensions. While Yellen’s comments did not specify the content of any agreements, the phrase “significant progress” was interpreted by market participants as a positive signal that the two sides were moving toward a resolution of lingering disputes.[2]

The market reaction was amplified by the fact that the U.S. and China had been engaged in a protracted trade war since 2018. During that period, the U.S. imposed tariffs on hundreds of billions of dollars of Chinese goods, and China retaliated with tariffs on U.S. products. The Biden administration’s approach to China has combined hard‑line rhetoric with behind‑the‑scenes negotiations, creating a volatile environment for investors who closely monitor any sign of policy change. Yellen’s remarks were therefore seen as a potential turning point, even though the statement did not confirm any specific tariff adjustments or policy concessions.[3]

Background and context

The U.S.–China trade conflict began in 2018 when President Trump announced a series of tariffs on Chinese goods, citing unfair trade practices and intellectual property theft. China responded with its own tariffs, leading to a tit‑for‑tat escalation that disrupted global supply chains and raised concerns about a broader economic slowdown. In 2020, the two sides signed a “Phase One” trade deal. The agreement included commitments by China to increase purchases of U.S. goods and services and to improve intellectual property protections. It also removed some tariffs, but many of the most contentious issues remained unresolved.[4]

Since the Phase One deal, the U.S. has rolled back some tariffs, but the overall trade relationship remains fraught. The Biden administration has continued to pursue a hard‑line stance on China, especially in areas such as technology transfer, national security, and geopolitical influence. Meanwhile, the Chinese government has maintained a cautious approach, balancing the need to protect its domestic industries with the desire to avoid a full‑blown trade war that could harm its export‑driven economy. The upcoming U.S.–China trade talks are therefore seen as a critical juncture where both sides may attempt to solidify gains or address lingering disputes.[5]

Yellen’s statement must also be viewed against the backdrop of U.S. domestic politics. President Biden faces pressure from business leaders who want a stable trade environment and from constituents who criticize China’s economic practices. The announcement of “significant progress” could be interpreted as a strategic move to appease both domestic and international audiences, signaling progress without committing to specific policy changes that could be politically costly. In this sense, the statement functions as a diplomatic cue designed to shape market expectations while preserving flexibility for future negotiations.[6]

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

From the perspective of U.S. investors, the rally was a welcome sign that the trade war might be winding down. Many analysts noted that the market’s reaction was largely driven by expectations that the U.S. could secure better terms in the upcoming talks, particularly regarding tariff reductions on high‑tech goods and intellectual property protections. However, some market participants warned that the lack of concrete details in Yellen’s statement meant that the rally could be temporary, and that investors should remain cautious until official agreements are announced.[7]

Chinese observers were more skeptical. While the Chinese government has not publicly commented on Yellen’s remarks, analysts in China noted that the U.S. has historically used diplomatic signals to influence market sentiment. They cautioned that the U.S. might be using the “significant progress” label to manage expectations while continuing to pursue its hard‑line stance on technology and national security issues. This perspective suggests that the market rally may reflect short‑term optimism rather than a substantive shift in policy.[8]

From a policy standpoint, the statement highlights the complexity of U.S.–China negotiations. The Biden administration has repeatedly emphasized the importance of a “fair” trade relationship, but the lack of specificity in Yellen’s remarks underscores the difficulty of translating diplomatic language into concrete policy outcomes. Analysts noted that the U.S. has historically used a combination of tariff adjustments, trade agreements, and diplomatic pressure to influence China’s economic behavior, and that the upcoming talks will likely involve a mix of these tools.[9]

Financial commentators also noted that the rally could have broader implications for global markets. The U.S. and Chinese economies are deeply intertwined, and any sign of a resolution to trade tensions can affect commodity prices, currency markets, and investment flows worldwide. The rally prompted a reassessment of supply‑chain strategies among multinational corporations, many of which have diversified production to mitigate the risk of tariff shocks. The potential easing of trade friction could shift the cost structure of global manufacturing, with ripple effects across the world economy.[10]

Conclusion

The market’s reaction to Yellen’s statement illustrates the power of diplomatic signals in shaping investor sentiment, even when the underlying policy details remain vague. While the rally lifted major indices and boosted sectors most exposed to U.S.–China trade dynamics, the subsequent pullback underscored the market’s appetite for concrete policy confirmation. As the U.S. and China continue to negotiate, investors will be watching closely for any tangible policy moves—such as tariff adjustments, intellectual property commitments, or technology‑transfer agreements—that could solidify the optimism sparked by Yellen’s remarks. Until such details emerge, the market will likely remain sensitive to every diplomatic cue, reflecting the enduring uncertainty that characterizes the U.S.–China trade relationship.
