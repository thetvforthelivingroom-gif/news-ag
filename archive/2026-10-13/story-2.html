More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will close, not because of a pandemic, but because students and teachers are caught in the crossfire of a national protest over pension reforms. The closures, announced by the Ministry of Education, come amid a wave of demonstrations that began last month and have spread from Paris to the provinces, turning classrooms into a flashpoint for a broader debate about the future of the French welfare state.

What happened

The Ministry of Education released a statement on Sunday evening saying that, for safety reasons, 406 schools would be closed for the day, affecting roughly 80,000 students and their families [1]. The decision followed a surge of protests that began on the 20th of last month, when thousands of workers, students and citizens marched in cities such as Lyon, Marseille and Lille to oppose the government’s pension reform plan. Police were deployed in several towns, raising concerns about the safety of students who would otherwise be attending school that day [3].

The closures were not limited to a single region; they spanned the entire country, from Île‑de‑France to the overseas departments. Local authorities coordinated with the national ministry to shut down schools in the most active protest zones, while schools in quieter areas remained open. The Ministry said that the presence of large crowds and the risk of clashes could jeopardise the safety of pupils and staff, and that it was preferable to close schools rather than risk injury or property damage [1].

The protests are part of a larger campaign against the pension reform bill that President Emmanuel Macron’s administration has been pushing for since early 2024. The bill proposes to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64, a change that has met fierce opposition from unions, students and many citizens who see it as a threat to the social contract that has defined French society for decades [2].

Background and context

The pension reform, officially titled the “Pension Reform Bill,” was introduced by the government in March 2024. It seeks to unify France’s 42 different pension schemes into a single, points‑based system, with the retirement age rising to 64 by 2035. The reform also proposes to reduce the number of years a person can contribute to the pension system, thereby lowering the overall pension benefits for many workers [2].

The government justifies the reform as a necessary step to ensure the long‑term sustainability of the French pension system, which faces a projected deficit of €1.5 trillion by 2030. President Macron has repeatedly described the reform as “the only way to protect the pension system for future generations” [5].

However, the proposal has sparked a wave of protests that echo earlier social movements in France. In 2018, the yellow‑vest movement erupted over fuel taxes and was followed by the 2020 labour‑reform demonstrations that challenged a range of reforms from the “parliamentary reform” to the “security law.” In both cases, the government’s attempts to push through reforms were met with widespread civil disobedience, culminating in strikes, roadblocks and, in some instances, the temporary closure of public institutions [4].

The use of school closures as a protest tactic is not new in France. During the 2018 yellow‑vest protests, several schools were shut down to allow students to join the demonstrations, a move that highlighted the role of education as a battleground for social change. The current closures therefore fit into a broader historical pattern of using schools as a lever in political disputes [4].

The bigger picture

Government and safety

The Ministry of Education’s statement emphasised safety as the primary concern. “The safety of our students and staff is paramount,” the ministry said, noting that the presence of large crowds and the potential for clashes made it unsafe for children to attend school that day [1]. The ministry also mentioned that local police had been deployed in several towns to manage the protests, and that the decision to close schools was taken in consultation with local authorities to minimise risk.

Union and protestor perspectives

Union leaders, however, have framed the closures as a form of coercion. “The government is silencing the voices of students and teachers by shutting down schools,” said a spokesperson for the National Federation of Teachers. “We are not asking for the closure of schools; we are demanding a fair and humane pension system.” The protests have been organised by a coalition of unions, student associations and civic groups, all of whom argue that the pension reform disproportionately affects lower‑income workers and threatens the solidarity that underpins the French welfare state.

Student groups have also expressed frustration. “We are being forced to choose between our education and our right to protest,” said a student leader from Lyon. “The closures are a reminder that the government is willing to disrupt our lives to push its agenda.” The protests have been largely peaceful, but the presence of police and the risk of clashes have led to concerns about the safety of students who might otherwise be attending school.

Teachers and families

For teachers, the closures have added to an already heavy workload. “We have to reschedule lessons, prepare alternative assignments and manage the fallout from the absence of students,” said a teacher from Marseille. The closures also disrupt exam schedules, particularly for students preparing for the baccalauréat, and force families to scramble for childcare or to miss work. Many parents have expressed anxiety about the impact on their children’s learning and the financial strain of arranging alternative care.

The closures have also highlighted the economic dimension of the protests. In a country where the average household spends a significant portion of its income on childcare, the sudden need for alternative arrangements can create a financial burden for families already stretched by the cost of living.

Broader social implications

The protests have attracted widespread attention, with thousands of demonstrators taking to the streets in cities across France. The government’s decision to close schools has been criticised by some as a sign of the administration’s willingness to use state power to silence dissent. Others argue that the closures were a necessary precaution to protect children from potential violence. The debate has brought the issue of the pension reform to the forefront of public discourse, with many citizens weighing in on the merits of the proposed changes.

What to watch

Potential for further closures

The current closures signal that the government is prepared to take drastic measures to ensure safety during the protests. Analysts suggest that further school closures could occur if the protests intensify or if clashes between demonstrators and police become more frequent. The Ministry of Education has indicated that it will continue to monitor the situation closely and will make decisions on a case‑by‑case basis [1].

Impact on upcoming elections

The pension reform bill has already become a key issue in the lead‑up to the next parliamentary elections. The protests have intensified public scrutiny of the government’s handling of the reform, and the closures may influence voter sentiment, particularly among younger voters and families. Political analysts predict that opposition parties will use the disruptions as evidence of the government’s failure to address public concerns.

Policy adjustments

While the government has defended the reform as essential for the pension system’s sustainability, the protests have forced it to reconsider its strategy. Some officials have indicated that the government is open to dialogue with unions and that it may consider adjustments to the bill’s implementation timeline. However, no concrete changes have been announced, and the debate is likely to continue in the coming months.

Long‑term effects on education

The closures have raised questions about the resilience of the French education system in times of social unrest. The Ministry of Education has pledged to provide additional resources to schools that have been affected, including temporary teachers and online learning platforms. The experience may prompt a reevaluation of how schools respond to future protests, balancing the need for safety with the continuity of education.

In the coming weeks, the French public will watch closely as the government, unions, teachers and families navigate the complex terrain of protest, policy and education. The decision to close more than 400 schools on a single day has underscored the deep divisions over pension reform and has highlighted the enduring role of schools as a focal point in France’s social and political life.
