More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
<article> On Monday, more than 400 schools across France shuttered their doors, a stark reminder that the country’s latest protest wave is not merely a political statement but a direct blow to the nation’s education system. The closures, announced by local prefectures amid escalating demonstrations, cut across urban and rural districts alike, leaving thousands of students without a classroom and teachers scrambling to adapt to a sudden halt in routine instruction. This interruption is part of a broader pattern in which French protests have historically leveraged public services—especially schools—to amplify dissent and pressure the government on contentious reforms.

What happened

The Ministry of Education issued a notice on Sunday that schools in over 400 schools would be closed on Monday, citing safety concerns linked to the ongoing protests. Prefectures across the country, from Paris to Brittany, issued precautionary orders to prevent potential clashes between demonstrators and school staff, as well as to avoid disruptions on major transit routes that had already been blocked by protestors. The closures were announced in the early hours of Sunday, giving families a narrow window to rearrange their schedules. According to the Ministry, the decision was taken after consultations with local authorities who reported increased risk of violence in several key regions, including Île-de-France and the Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes area [T2].

The impact on students was immediate: thousands of pupils were left without a classroom for the day, and many teachers had to cancel or postpone lessons. In some districts, teachers reported that they would need to conduct remote lessons or provide alternative assignments, a logistical challenge given the short notice. The Ministry has urged parents to keep their children at home and to check local news for updates on school reopenings. The closures also affected students preparing for national examinations, as some schools were scheduled to hold final assessments that day.

Background and context

The closures are part of a broader wave of protests that began in late 2023 over the government’s pension reform, which seeks to raise the retirement age to 64 and adjust pension benefits. The reform has been met with fierce opposition from workers’ unions, including the teachers’ unions SNES and SNESF, who argue that it would undermine the stability of the public sector and erode workers’ rights. The protests, which have escalated into nationwide demonstrations, have seen thousands of demonstrators block major highways and occupy city centers, prompting the government to issue safety orders in several regions [T2].

Historically, French protests have often targeted public institutions to maximize visibility and pressure. During the 2018‑2019 pension reform protests, schools and workplaces were shut down across the country, leading to a nationwide strike that disrupted the economy for weeks. The 2019 “gilets jaunes” movement also saw widespread school closures as protestors marched through schoolyards and occupied school buses, further illustrating how education has become a battleground for social dissent in France [T2].

The current protests are fueled by broader economic concerns, including high inflation, rising unemployment, and a public sense that the government’s policies are favoring the wealthy at the expense of ordinary citizens. The Ministry of Finance has reported that inflation remains above the European Central Bank’s target, while unemployment rates have hovered around 7.5% in recent months, contributing to the public’s frustration with the reform agenda [T2].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

From the perspective of the Ministry of Education, the decision to close schools was a precautionary measure aimed at safeguarding students and staff. The Ministry’s spokesperson emphasized that the safety of the school community was the primary concern and that the closures would be lifted as soon as the situation stabilized. The Ministry also highlighted its willingness to work with local authorities to ensure that students could continue their education through alternative means, such as online platforms, where feasible [T2].

Teachers’ unions, however, have expressed concerns that the closures disrupt the learning trajectory of students, especially those in the final years of secondary school who are preparing for the baccalauréat. SNES officials argue that the government’s approach to the protests, which includes the use of road blockages and police presence near schools, is a direct threat to the continuity of education. They have called for a more balanced strategy that protects both the rights of protestors and the educational needs of students [T2].

Protest organizers and student groups have adopted a more nuanced stance. While some student associations have expressed solidarity with the protestors, arguing that the reforms threaten their future job prospects, others have called for a temporary suspension of demonstrations near schools to avoid further disruptions. The student union “Étudiants pour la République” has urged the government to consider alternative venues for protest that do not interfere with educational institutions, emphasizing the importance of maintaining academic stability during times of social unrest [T2].

Local prefectures, tasked with maintaining public order, have issued orders to close schools in areas where protest activity is intense. Prefect François Dubois of the Paris region explained that the decision was made after a thorough assessment of the risk of violence and the potential for clashes between protestors and school staff. He added that the prefecture would monitor the situation closely and reopen schools as soon as it was safe to do so [T2].

The media’s portrayal of the closures has varied. Domestic outlets such as Le Monde and France 24 have focused on the human impact, highlighting stories of students left without a classroom and teachers scrambling to find alternatives. International outlets, including The Guardian and Reuters, have framed the closures as part of a broader pattern of civil disobedience in France, noting the historical precedent of using schools as a platform for protest. Meanwhile, NPR has highlighted the economic context, pointing out that the protests are driven by high inflation and unemployment, which have eroded public confidence in the government’s reform agenda [T2].

What to watch (future implications)

The immediate future will likely see a continuation of school closures in regions where protests remain intense. The Ministry of Education has indicated that it will monitor the situation closely and will reopen schools as soon as it is safe to do so, but it has also warned that further disruptions are possible if the protests intensify. The government’s willingness to negotiate with teachers’ unions and protest organizers will be a key factor in determining whether the closures will continue or be lifted.

The broader implications for the education sector are significant. A prolonged period of school closures could disrupt the academic calendar, delay the delivery of essential learning content, and increase the burden on teachers to provide remote or alternative instruction. The Ministry has stated that it will support schools in developing contingency plans, including the use of online platforms and distributed learning materials, to mitigate the impact on students. However, the effectiveness of these measures will depend on the availability of resources and the ability of schools to adapt quickly to changing circumstances.

On a political level, the protests and the resulting school closures underscore the tension between the government’s reform agenda and the public’s demand for social justice. The government’s approach to the protests—particularly its use of police presence near schools and road blockages—has drawn criticism from both teachers’ unions and civil society groups, who argue that it undermines the right to education and the safety of students. The outcome of these tensions will shape the future of France’s education policy and the broader social contract between the state and its citizens.

In the long term, the pattern of using public services as a platform for protest may continue, especially if the government’s reform agenda remains unpopular. The experience of the 2018‑2019 pension reform protests and the 2019 “gilets jaunes” movement suggests that school closures can be a powerful tool for mobilizing public support and drawing attention to policy grievances. As such, policymakers will need to balance the need for public order with the imperative to preserve educational continuity, ensuring that the right to education remains protected even amid social unrest.

The next few weeks will be critical in determining whether the government can find a compromise that satisfies both the protestors’ demands and the educational needs of France’s students. The outcome will not only shape the immediate future of the country’s schools but also set a precedent for how public institutions are treated in times of political dissent.
