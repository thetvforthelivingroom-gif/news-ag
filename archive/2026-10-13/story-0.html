Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
<article> In the last 47 years, satellites have measured a staggering 12 trillion tons of ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica—an amount that could raise global sea levels by more than a meter, putting millions of coastal residents at risk.

What happened

From the early 1990s to the present, a fleet of space‑borne instruments has been quietly watching the planet’s frozen reservoirs. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) satellites, launched in 1997, mapped variations in Earth’s gravity field that translate directly into mass changes on the surface. GRACE data, combined with observations from CryoSat‑1 (2010‑present) and ICESat‑2 (2018‑present), reveal that the combined ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica have shed over 12 trillion tons of ice since 1973 [1].

This loss is not a sudden event but a cumulative process. GRACE first detected a measurable decline in 1997, and the trend has accelerated in the 2000s and 2010s. The three missions together provide a continuous record that bridges gaps in ground‑based measurements, offering a more complete picture of ice mass balance than any single technique could achieve alone.

The figure translates into a sea‑level rise of roughly 1 m if the current rate persists for another 100 years, a scenario that would inundate low‑lying islands, coastal cities, and entire maritime trade routes.

Background and context

The satellite record is a cornerstone of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s (IPCC) latest assessment reports. The IPCC has used these data to quantify the contribution of ice loss to global sea‑level rise and to validate climate models that project future changes. In particular, the 2023 IPCC report cites the GRACE, CryoSat, and ICESat‑2 measurements as the most robust evidence of accelerating ice melt in the cryosphere [3].

Attribution of the ice loss is complex. While anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions have increased the average temperature of the planet, natural variability—El Niño events, volcanic eruptions, and solar cycles—also modulates ice mass on decadal scales. Studies that combine satellite data with atmospheric and oceanic observations suggest that the dominant driver of the recent acceleration is human‑induced warming, but the relative influence of atmospheric versus oceanic heat transport remains debated among scientists [4].

The precision of the satellite measurements is limited by several factors. Gravity‑field inversion techniques can introduce errors of a few percent, and the presence of clouds can bias laser altimetry measurements from ICESat‑2. Moreover, there is a lack of in‑situ ground confirmation for many remote regions of the ice sheets, which introduces additional uncertainty into the mass‑balance estimates [5].

The bigger picture

The 12 trillion‑ton loss is a stark reminder of the tangible consequences of climate change, but it also highlights the tension between mitigation and adaptation. On the one hand, policy makers face the urgent task of reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions to keep warming below 1.5 °C, the threshold set by the Paris Agreement. On the other hand, the irreversible nature of the ice loss forces coastal communities and insurers to prepare for higher sea levels, even if emissions are curtailed.

In the lead‑up to the 2025–26 climate negotiations, the satellite record has become a key evidence base for both sides. Pro‑mitigation advocates point to the data to argue that every degree of warming already translates into measurable ice loss and sea‑level rise. Adaptation groups, meanwhile, use the same numbers to lobby for stronger coastal defenses, relocation plans, and insurance reforms that account for the new baseline of sea level.

The human impact is already visible. Indigenous peoples in Greenland and the Arctic have reported changes in sea ice extent that affect hunting and travel. Coastal cities such as Miami, Shanghai, and Jakarta are experiencing more frequent flooding and are investing billions in infrastructure upgrades. Shipping lanes in the Arctic are opening, promising new trade routes but also raising concerns about environmental protection and navigation safety. Insurance markets are recalibrating premiums in light of the higher probability of extreme flooding events, a shift that could ripple through global finance [7].

What to watch

Satellite missions will continue to refine our understanding of ice dynamics. The upcoming GRACE‑FO mission, launched in 2018, is already delivering higher‑resolution gravity data that could reduce uncertainties in mass‑balance estimates. CryoSat‑2’s extended mission will provide longer time series for the polar oceans, helping to disentangle the roles of atmospheric and oceanic warming.

In the next decade, the IPCC is expected to release a new assessment that will incorporate the latest satellite data and updated climate models. The projections for sea‑level rise are likely to become more precise, but the range of uncertainty will still reflect the limits of current measurement techniques and the inherent variability of the climate system.

Policy makers will need to balance the science with economic and social realities. The 12 trillion‑ton figure, while alarming, is not a fixed endpoint; it is a dynamic metric that will evolve as the planet warms and as human actions alter the trajectory of emissions. The challenge will be to translate this metric into concrete policy—whether through stricter emissions standards, investment in resilient infrastructure, or international cooperation on climate finance.

In the coming years, the satellite record will remain a vital tool for monitoring the planet’s cryosphere, informing both the scientific community and the global policy arena. As the numbers continue to climb, the urgency of addressing climate change will only grow, demanding that governments, businesses, and communities act decisively on the evidence that the Earth’s satellites have been quietly, relentlessly, and precisely recording for nearly half a century.
