Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-13
A recent satellite study published in *Nature Climate Change* has quantified the loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica with unprecedented detail, revealing that the two polar ice sheets have shed more than 12 trillion metric tonnes of ice since 1975. If the current trajectory continues, the resulting sea‑level rise could reach roughly two metres, putting millions of people in low‑lying coastal zones at risk. The paper, led by researchers from the University of Oxford and the University of Cambridge, synthesises data from a decade‑long network of gravimetric and radar missions to produce the most comprehensive record of ice‑mass change to date.¹

How the numbers were derived

The study builds on the legacy of the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE Follow‑On, which measure variations in Earth’s gravity field to infer changes in mass distribution. By combining these gravimetric observations with high‑resolution radar interferometry from the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑2 and Sentinel‑1 missions, the authors were able to separate surface mass changes—primarily meltwater and snowfall—from basal mass changes, which represent ice loss to the ocean.² The analysis covers the period 1975–2022, a span of 47 years, and shows a cumulative loss of 12.1 ± 0.3 trillion tonnes—8.2 ± 0.2 trillion tonnes from Greenland and 3.9 ± 0.1 trillion tonnes from Antarctica. The average loss over the entire period is therefore about 255 million tonnes per year, but the rate has accelerated dramatically in the past two decades.³

The acceleration is evident when the record is split into two equal halves. From 1975 to 2000, the loss averaged 0.5 trillion tonnes per decade. From 2001 to 2022, the average rose to 1.5 trillion tonnes per decade—three times the earlier pace. The authors attribute this trend to a combination of increased surface melt driven by rising atmospheric temperatures and enhanced basal erosion caused by warmer ocean waters, especially along the West Antarctic Ice Sheet and the Antarctic Peninsula.⁴

From ice loss to sea‑level rise

Ice mass loss translates into sea‑level rise through two primary pathways. First, the direct addition of meltwater to the ocean increases the volume of water. Second, the loss of ice from the grounded ice sheets reduces the gravitational attraction that pulls ocean water toward the poles, causing a redistribution of water that raises sea level globally. The study estimates that the 12‑trillion‑ton loss has contributed about 1.9 mm of global mean sea‑level rise since 1975, a figure that aligns with independent estimates from tide‑gauge networks and satellite altimetry.⁵

Scientific context and uncertainties

The 2023 paper builds on earlier estimates from the IPCC’s Fifth Assessment Report (2013) and the Sixth Assessment Report (2021), which projected 7–10 trillion tonnes of ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica over the 21st century under a range of emissions scenarios. The new satellite record confirms that the actual loss has already surpassed the lower end of those projections and is accelerating faster than many climate models had anticipated.⁶

The authors acknowledge several sources of uncertainty. Gravimetric measurements are limited by the spatial resolution of the GRACE satellites and by the need to separate ice mass changes from other mass redistribution processes such as groundwater extraction and atmospheric pressure variations. Radar interferometry can be affected by surface roughness and snow cover, especially in the high‑altitude regions of Greenland. The combined uncertainty is quoted as ± 0.3 trillion tonnes, which translates to ± 0.05 mm of global mean sea‑level rise per year.⁷

Policy implications

The findings reinforce the urgency of the Paris Agreement’s goal to limit global warming to 1.5 °C. The study’s authors argue that even a modest increase in atmospheric temperatures beyond 1.5 °C would further accelerate ice loss, potentially doubling the projected sea‑level rise by 2100. They call for a rapid reduction in greenhouse‑gas emissions, stronger national commitments under the UNFCCC, and increased investment in adaptation measures such as coastal defenses and resilient infrastructure.⁸

Several national adaptation plans have already begun to incorporate the new data. For example, the United Kingdom’s National Adaptation Programme now includes a scenario that assumes a 1.5 m sea‑level rise by 2100, while the United States’ National Climate Assessment has updated its projections to reflect the accelerated loss of ice. These policy shifts illustrate how scientific findings can directly influence governance and planning.⁹

Looking ahead

Future satellite missions—such as the European Space Agency’s upcoming CryoSat‑3 and NASA’s Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission—promise higher spatial and temporal resolution, which will help to reduce uncertainties in ice‑mass estimates. Coupling these observations with advanced ice‑sheet models that incorporate ocean–ice interactions and sub‑glacial hydrology will improve projections of future sea‑level rise.¹⁰

The 12‑trillion‑ton loss over 47 years is a stark reminder that the cryosphere is not a static backdrop to climate change but a dynamic system that responds rapidly to warming. Continued monitoring, coupled with decisive policy action, will be essential to protect vulnerable coastal communities and to preserve the integrity of the planet’s climate system.
