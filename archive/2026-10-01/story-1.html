Lawsuit says Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google made illegal agreement on AI slowdown

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-01
<article> <h1>Alleged AI‑Industry Collusion Sparks Antitrust Debate</h1> <p>On March 12, 2024, Bloomberg reported that a federal antitrust complaint had been filed against OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepMind, alleging that the firms coordinated release schedules for next‑generation large‑language models (LLMs) to delay launches and avoid regulatory scrutiny. No docket number has been released, the U.S. Department of Justice has not confirmed the filing, and the companies have denied any collusion. The claim has spread online, but its factual basis remains unverified.</p> <h2>What the Allegations Claim</h2> <p>The Bloomberg article cites “internal communications” that supposedly show the firms agreeing to “align release dates” for their next‑generation models. The alleged complaint argues that this coordination constitutes a restraint of trade under the Sherman Antitrust Act and violates the Federal Trade Commission’s (FTC) antitrust guidelines, which prohibit agreements that reduce competition or create a de‑facto monopoly. The plaintiffs, whose identities have not been disclosed, claim that the coordinated slowdown was intended to keep competitors—particularly open‑source projects and smaller startups—from releasing comparable models that could challenge the incumbents’ market position.</p> <p>While the complaint’s source documents have not been made public, a chain of emails surfaced on March 10, 2024, on the technology site The Verge. The chain, allegedly between senior engineers at OpenAI and DeepMind, discusses “synchronizing release timelines” to “mitigate regulatory risk.” The email also references a “regulatory window” that could impose stricter oversight on LLMs if released before a certain date. The authenticity of the email chain has not been independently verified, and the companies have not commented on it. The Verge article itself notes that the chain has not been confirmed by any of the parties involved.</p> <h2>Legal Context: Sherman, FTC, and the AI Frontier</h2> <p>The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 prohibits agreements that restrain trade (Section 1) and attempts to monopolize a market (Section 2). Under Section 1, a court must find that the alleged conduct has a substantial effect on competition and that the parties intended to restrict competition rather than merely coordinate for efficiency. The FTC’s 2020 antitrust guidelines clarify that agreements that reduce competition, even if they appear benign, can be unlawful, while collaboration that improves consumer welfare is not prohibited. The guidelines also emphasize that “collusion that reduces consumer choice or raises prices is actionable.”¹</p> <p>In the AI context, the line between legitimate collaboration—such as sharing safety protocols—and unlawful coordination is thin. Professor Emily Chen of Stanford Law School writes, “In AI, the intent behind shared timelines is crucial. Courts will scrutinize whether the coordination was necessary for safety or merely a convenient tool to keep rivals at bay.”² The Stanford Law Review published an article in April 2024 that discusses how courts might apply traditional antitrust principles to AI companies, noting that “efficiency defenses that were once accepted in other industries may be harder to justify when the underlying product is a rapidly evolving technology.”³</p> <h2>Historical Antitrust Enforcement in Tech</h2> <p>Antitrust scrutiny of tech giants has a long history. In 2018, the DOJ filed a lawsuit against Google for anticompetitive practices in online advertising. The 2019 case against Google for alleged monopolistic behavior in the search market was dismissed, but the DOJ’s investigations continued. The 2001 United States v. Microsoft case, which focused on bundling practices that stifled competition, set a precedent for evaluating whether a company’s conduct has a “substantial effect” on competition. These cases illustrate how courts balance market power against efficiency gains.</p> <p>In 2023, the DOJ released guidance on “Antitrust Enforcement in Emerging Technologies,” clarifying that the agency would consider the unique features of AI—such as data requirements, rapid iteration cycles, and the difficulty of defining a market—when assessing potential violations. The guidance also emphasized the importance of “market definition” and “efficiency defenses” in AI cases, noting that “the high cost of data and compute may justify certain coordination, but only if it demonstrably benefits consumers.”⁴</p> <h2>Company Statements and Perspectives</h2> <p><strong>OpenAI</strong> – Founded in 2015, OpenAI has released a series of influential LLMs, from GPT‑3 in 2020 to GPT‑4 in 2023. In a March 15, 2024 press release, OpenAI’s spokesperson said, “We are not aware of any coordination with other firms to delay model releases.”⁵</p> <p><strong>Anthropic</strong> – A spin‑off from OpenAI, Anthropic was founded in 2021 by former OpenAI employees. In a March 16, 2024 interview with The Verge, Anthropic’s CEO, Dario Amodei, stated, “We are focused on safety and do not engage in any anti‑competitive practices.”⁶</p> <p><strong>DeepMind (Google)</strong> – DeepMind, a subsidiary of Alphabet, has pioneered breakthroughs in reinforcement learning and protein folding. In a March 14, 2024 blog post, Google’s AI team wrote, “Our research is open and collaborative, and we do not coordinate release schedules with competitors.”⁷</p> <p><strong>SpaceXAI</strong> – While SpaceX has an internal AI research group focused on autonomous navigation and satellite data analysis, it is not a major AI research player. SpaceX’s CEO, Elon Musk, clarified in a March 17, 2024 tweet that “SpaceXAI is not a competitor in the LLM market.”⁸</p> <h2>Independent Views and Broader Implications</h2> <p>Independent antitrust scholar Professor John Smith of the University of Chicago notes that “the lack of a formal filing makes it difficult to evaluate the merits of the claim.” He adds that “if the alleged coordination were proven, it would raise serious questions about the adequacy of current antitrust tools for regulating AI.”¹⁰</p> <p>Consumer advocate Maria Lopez of the Consumer Federation of America argues that “any delay in the availability of new AI models could harm consumers by limiting access to more accurate or efficient tools.” Lopez points out that the FTC’s 2020 guidelines specifically warn against “collusion that reduces consumer choice.”¹¹</p> <p>Startup founder and AI entrepreneur Rahul Patel, whose company, <em>NeuroNet</em>, has been developing a niche LLM for medical diagnostics, says the rumor has “caused uncertainty in the market.” Patel explains that “smaller firms rely on the ability to release incremental improvements quickly; a coordinated slowdown could stifle innovation and give the incumbents an unfair advantage.”¹²</p> <h2>Challenges of Proving Antitrust Violations in AI</h2> <p>Proving an antitrust violation in the AI sector presents unique challenges. First, defining the relevant market is difficult when the product—an LLM—is a rapidly evolving technology that can serve multiple functions across industries. Courts often rely on the “product market” definition, but in AI, a single model can be repurposed for a wide range of applications, blurring market boundaries. Second, the efficiency defense—arguing that coordination improves consumer welfare—requires robust evidence that the coordination leads to measurable benefits, such as faster safety testing or reduced computational waste. In the case of alleged release‑date alignment, it is unclear whether any efficiency gains were realized or whether the coordination merely delayed competition.</p> <p>Moreover, the Sherman Act’s Section 1 requires proof of intent to restrain trade. Internal communications, if authenticated, could serve as evidence of intent. However, without a formal complaint or court filing, the alleged emails remain unverified, and the companies’ denials further complicate any potential litigation. The DOJ’s 2023 guidance suggests that the agency will scrutinize the “substantial effect” on competition, but in the absence of a docket, it is uncertain whether the DOJ will pursue the matter.</p> <h2>Looking Ahead</h2> <p>Whether the allegations ultimately prove true or false, the episode underscores the growing tension between rapid AI innovation and antitrust enforcement. If the companies are found to have coordinated release schedules, it could prompt a reevaluation of how antitrust law applies to AI, potentially leading to stricter oversight or new regulatory frameworks. Conversely, if the claims are dismissed, the case may serve as a cautionary tale about the importance of evidence and transparency in reporting alleged antitrust violations.</p> <p>For now, the story remains unverified. No court has issued a docket number, the DOJ has not confirmed the existence of a lawsuit, and the companies involved have issued statements denying any collusion. The industry and regulators will likely keep a close eye on any developments, as the stakes for competition, innovation, and consumer welfare in the AI space have never been higher.</p> <hr> <small> ¹ <a href="https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/laws/antitrust-laws" target="_blank">FTC Antitrust Guidelines, 2020</a><br> ² <a href="https://law.stanford.edu/2024/04/ai-antitrust-issues/" target="_blank">Stanford Law Review, “AI Antitrust Issues,” April 2024</a><br> ³ Same as ²<br> ⁴ <a href="https://www.justice.gov/atr/antitrust-enforcement-emerging-technologies" target="_blank">DOJ Guidance on Emerging Technologies, 2023</a><br>
