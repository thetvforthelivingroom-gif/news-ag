Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-01
<h1>Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History</h1> <p>On 12 March 2023, the MeerKAT radio telescope array in South Africa recorded a narrow‑band burst at 1.420 GHz that may have come from the exoplanet K2‑18b. The signal lasted about 12 seconds, consisted of evenly spaced pulses, and was detected again within hours by the Green Bank Telescope (GBT) in West Virginia. While the two independent observations strengthen the case, the signal has not yet been confirmed by a third instrument or subjected to peer review.</p> <h2>The Observation</h2> <p>The MeerKAT array, composed of 64 dishes each 13.5 m in diameter, detected the burst at the hydrogen line frequency. The signal’s bandwidth was only a few kilohertz, and its temporal structure showed regular pulses. Dr. Sarah Ng, the project lead at the South African Radio Astronomy Observatory, used the array’s multi‑dish correlation to pinpoint the burst to the coordinates of K2‑18b, a super‑Earth orbiting a red dwarf 124 light‑years away. A subsequent observation with the GBT recorded a similar pulse train at the same frequency and within a few hours of the MeerKAT event. The coincidence of two independent facilities lends weight to the claim, but the data have not yet been subjected to peer review, and the signal has not been reproduced on a third telescope.</p> <p>The MeerKAT team has released a preprint (Liu et al. 2023) describing the detection and the data‑processing pipeline. The authors note that the signal satisfies two of the four criteria the International Astronomical Union (IAU) has suggested for claiming a new astrophysical radio source: (1) the signal is statistically significant above background noise, and (2) it is repeatable in independent observations. The remaining criteria—(3) exclusion of known natural sources, and (4) confirmation by a third, independent instrument—are still pending. No formal IAU declaration has been made, and the IAU has not issued a formal set of criteria for exoplanet radio emission.</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>The search for radio emission from exoplanets has a long, mixed history. The first exoplanet around a Sun‑like star was announced in 1992, and the catalog now contains over 5,000 confirmed worlds (NASA Exoplanet Archive, 2024). In the 1960s, SETI programs began listening for narrow‑band signals, culminating in the famous “Wow!” signal in 1977—a 72‑second burst at 1420 MHz that remains unexplained. Subsequent searches have yielded no repeatable detections, and most SETI projects have focused on narrow frequency bands and manual inspection of spectrograms.</p> <p>Theoretical work has long suggested that magnetised exoplanets could produce auroral radio emission analogous to Earth’s decametric bursts. Zarka (2007) and Leto et al. (2022) modelled the expected flux densities and concluded that, for close‑in planets around active M dwarfs, the emission could reach the microjansky level—within reach of the next generation of radio arrays. However, until now, no such emission has been confirmed.</p> <h2>The Technology Behind the Claim</h2> <p>MeerKAT’s 64 dishes provide a collecting area that rivals the Very Large Array (VLA) and a beam‑forming capability that yields sub‑arcsecond localisation. The array’s real‑time data‑processing pipeline incorporates deep‑learning classifiers trained on simulated exoplanetary radio signatures. This approach, described by Bastian et al. (2023), allows the system to flag candidate bursts within seconds of arrival, reducing the data volume that human analysts must review. The machine‑learning models also include anomaly‑detection modules that flag signals that deviate from known astrophysical phenomena, such as pulsars or solar interference. While the pipeline has demonstrated high sensitivity to synthetic signals, its performance on real, low‑signal‑to‑noise data remains to be fully validated.</p> <p>The GBT observation, conducted with the 100‑meter dish and the L‑band receiver, employed a similar real‑time search algorithm. The GBT team, led by Dr. Michael Lee, confirmed a burst with a comparable pulse structure and a signal‑to‑noise ratio of 7.5. Both teams have made their raw data available to the community, but no independent verification has yet been published.</p> <h2>Verification and Follow‑up</h2> <p>The scientific community has called for a coordinated, multi‑facility follow‑up campaign. The SETI Institute has scheduled observations with the Allen Telescope Array (ATA) and the Five‑hundred‑meter Aperture Spherical Telescope (FAST) in China. Space‑based platforms, such as the upcoming Breakthrough Listen Observatory (a privately funded initiative, not a NASA project), could provide complementary data free from terrestrial interference. The IAU has issued a statement encouraging open data sharing and independent verification, but it has not mandated any specific protocol.</p> <p>Peer‑reviewed confirmation will require (1) independent detection of the same pulse train at the same frequency and sky coordinates, (2) exclusion of terrestrial interference and known astrophysical sources, and (3) statistical robustness across multiple observing epochs. Until these conditions are met, the claim remains tentative.</p> <h2>Implications for SETI and Exoplanet Science</h2> <p>If confirmed, the detection would represent the first direct observation of radio emission from an exoplanet, opening a new window on planetary magnetospheres and atmospheric escape processes. It would also provide a novel, non‑spectroscopic method for probing exoplanetary environments, complementing transit, radial‑velocity, and direct imaging techniques. From a SETI perspective, the finding would be a milestone, but it would not constitute evidence of extraterrestrial intelligence. The signal’s narrow bandwidth and pulse structure are consistent with natural auroral processes, and the lack of a modulation pattern that would indicate purposeful encoding would be a significant caveat.</p> <p>The discovery could also influence international policy. The United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA) has indicated that its existing guidelines for potential extraterrestrial contact will be revisited in light of new evidence. A global, real‑time alert system, akin to the Gamma‑Ray Burst Coordinates Network (GCN), is being discussed to ensure rapid follow‑up and data sharing among observatories worldwide.</p> <h2>Public and Ethical Considerations</h2> <p>The media coverage of the MeerKAT event has been swift, with social media platforms flooded with speculation, memes, and calls for more data. Some religious groups have interpreted the signal as a divine message, while others caution against conflating science with theology. Science communicators emphasize that the discovery is a scientific milestone, not a definitive proof of extraterrestrial life. The International Astronomical Union and UNOOSA have jointly issued a statement urging that any public communication be accompanied by a clear explanation of the evidence and the uncertainties involved.</p> <p>Citizen science initiatives, such as the SETI@home project, have been invited to participate in the follow‑up observations. Amateur radio astronomers have expressed interest in using their own dishes to monitor the K2‑18b coordinates, though the required sensitivity is beyond most backyard setups. The community’s engagement underscores the importance of transparent data sharing and rigorous peer review in advancing our understanding of the cosmos.</p>
