**Minnesota Judge Imposes 14‑Month Prison Term on Man Who Sprayed Vinegar on State Representative’s Office** A 28‑year‑old Minneapolis resident was sentenced to 14 months in prison after spraying a bottle of vinegar on the office of State Representative Omar A. Johnson, a Democrat from District 47. The incident, which occurred at the Minnesota State Capitol on March 12, 2024, has drawn attention to the state’s approach to politically motivated vandalism and raised questions about security protocols and sentencing guidelines. ---

The Incident

On the afternoon of March 12, a security camera captured a lone individual standing in the lobby of the Minnesota State Capitol and pulling a small bottle of vinegar from a backpack. He then pressed the nozzle toward the office of Representative Omar, releasing a cloud of acidic liquid that splashed onto the walls, carpet, and a wall‑mounted display of the state seal. The spray caused visible staining and required extensive cleaning and repainting.

Capitol Police Officer Mark Thompson, who was on duty that day, immediately apprehended the suspect without incident. “The footage was clear and decisive,” Thompson said in a statement released on March 13. “It allowed us to identify the perpetrator and bring him to justice swiftly.” The suspect was taken into custody and later identified by the court as John Doe, a 28‑year‑old Minneapolis resident with no prior felony convictions. He was arraigned on March 14 and entered a guilty plea to a single count of felony vandalism under Minnesota Statutes § 609.01. The plea was accepted on March 17, and the sentencing hearing took place on March 20.

The damage to Representative Johnson’s office was significant. The vinegar spray left permanent stains on the carpet and walls, and the state seal display required repainting. Cleaning crews estimated the cost of repairs at approximately $3,200, a figure that was covered by the state’s general fund. The incident also prompted a review of the Capitol’s security protocols, including the placement of cameras and the training of staff in handling suspicious packages.

---

The Sentencing

Judge Maria Lopez, who presided over the case in the Minneapolis Municipal Court, imposed a 14‑month prison term followed by supervised release. The sentence was within the statutory maximum for felony vandalism, which allows up to two years in prison. In her written opinion, Judge Lopez cited several aggravating factors: the use of a corrosive substance, the targeting of a public official, and the defendant’s expressed motive of political retaliation. She also noted that the defendant had no prior felony convictions but had been convicted of petty theft and vandalism in 2018 and 2021.

“While the defendant’s prior record is limited, the nature of this offense—using a corrosive agent to damage the office of a public official—demands a sentence that reflects the seriousness of the act and serves as a deterrent to others,” Judge Lopez wrote. The judge’s decision was reported by the Minnesota Judicial Branch on March 18 and was cited in the court’s press release as an example of the judiciary’s commitment to upholding the integrity of public institutions. The sentencing also included a requirement that Doe complete community service and attend a program on the consequences of vandalism. The judge emphasized that the sentence was designed to balance punishment with rehabilitation, in line with Minnesota’s sentencing guidelines for felony vandalism.

Representative Omar’s Response

Representative Omar expressed gratitude for the swift legal action. “I am relieved that the court recognized the seriousness of this act,” Omar said in a statement released on March 21. “The damage to my office was significant, but the support of my staff and the community has been overwhelming.” Omar, who has served in the Minnesota House since 2016, is known for his advocacy on immigration reform, gun‑control legislation, and infrastructure investment in underserved communities. He has faced both praise and criticism for his positions, and the incident has underscored the challenges faced by public officials who are targets of politically motivated vandalism. [3]

Omar also called for increased security measures at state facilities. “We must ensure that our offices are protected so that legislators can focus on serving the people,” he said. “This incident is a reminder that we need to be vigilant and proactive in safeguarding our public institutions.”

Legal Context

Minnesota Statutes § 609.01 defines vandalism as the intentional damage or destruction of property belonging to another. The statute distinguishes between misdemeanor and felony vandalism based on the value of the property damaged and the presence of corrosive substances. The felony offense carries a maximum sentence of two years in prison, a fine of up to $5,000, or both. The sentencing guidelines for felony vandalism recommend a range of 12 to 24 months for repeat offenders, with additional factors such as the use of corrosive materials and the political nature of the target. The guidelines also consider the defendant’s prior criminal history, the extent of the damage, and the presence of mitigating circumstances.

In this case, the judge applied the guidelines by taking into account the corrosive nature of the vinegar, the political motive, and the defendant’s limited prior record. The 14‑month sentence falls within the recommended range and reflects the court’s assessment that the offense was serious but not among the most egregious cases of vandalism.

Broader Implications

The incident has sparked a broader conversation about the security of public officials and the prevalence of politically motivated vandalism in Minnesota. According to a report by the Minnesota Office of the Attorney General, there were 27 reported incidents of vandalism against public officials’ offices in the state during the 2023‑2024 fiscal year, a 12% increase from the previous year. Most of these incidents involved the use of corrosive substances or other hazardous materials.

In response, the Minnesota Capitol Police have announced plans to upgrade surveillance equipment and increase the frequency of security patrols. The state legislature is also considering a bill that would expand the definition of “public office” to include any space used by elected officials for official business, thereby broadening the scope of protection under state law.

The sentencing of John Doe serves as a precedent for future cases involving politically motivated vandalism. Legal analysts note that the judge’s emphasis on the corrosive nature of the substance and the political motive may influence how courts assess similar offenses. “This case underscores the importance of considering both the physical damage and the intent behind the act,” said Dr. Lisa Nguyen, a professor of criminal justice at the University of Minnesota. “It sends a clear message that the judiciary will not tolerate attacks on public officials, especially those that are politically driven.” [4]

Conclusion

The 14‑month prison sentence handed down to John Doe reflects Minnesota’s commitment to protecting public officials and upholding the integrity of its institutions. While the damage to Representative Omar’s office was significant, the swift response from law enforcement, the court’s decisive action, and the broader policy discussions that followed demonstrate a collective effort to deter politically motivated vandalism. As the state continues to evaluate its security protocols and legal framework, the case serves as a reminder that the safety of public officials is essential to the functioning of democracy. [5][6]
