Is Zelensky right to say data centre strikes have taken down Russian bots?

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-21
When President Volodymyr Zelensky said that Ukrainian strikes on Russian data centres had “taken down” Russian bots, the comment sparked a debate that crossed battlefield lines into cyber‑law, information warfare and international norms. The claim stemmed from a series of precision attacks carried out in late March. Some praised it as a clever counter‑propaganda tactic, while others dismissed it as exaggerated propaganda. In the weeks that followed, analysts, officials and scholars examined the evidence, the technical realities of bot networks and the legal questions that arise when civilian infrastructure becomes a target in a hybrid war.

What happened

On 28 March, Ukrainian forces struck a cluster of data‑centre facilities in the Russian‑occupied Donetsk region that were believed to host servers for disinformation bots. Colonel Oleksandr Petrov, spokesperson for the Ukrainian Cyber Forces, said the attacks were “precision strikes aimed at disabling the cloud infrastructure that the bots relied on” and that “the bot‑generated content on social media dropped noticeably in the following weeks”<sup>1</sup>. The strikes combined kinetic munitions with cyber‑operations that targeted the physical and digital layers of the facilities, according to a Ukrainian Ministry of Defence press release<sup>2</sup>.

Russian officials denied that any significant damage had occurred. Deputy Minister of Digital Development Alexei Ivanov called the attacks “a violation of international law and a threat to civilian infrastructure” and asserted that the bot networks had simply migrated to other servers<sup>3</sup>. No independent verification of the damage or the operational status of the bots was released by either side.

Independent analysts, drawing on open‑source intelligence, satellite imagery and leaked logs, offered a more nuanced view. Bellingcat researcher Alexey Glebov noted that “while some servers were offline, the bot network displayed a high degree of redundancy and rapid redeployment capabilities”<sup>4</sup>. A Maxar analyst, citing satellite imagery from 28 March, observed a “reduction in activity at the data‑centre complex” but also pointed out that “the infrastructure remained largely intact”<sup>5</sup>. CrowdStrike’s 2024 Global Threat Report, which tracks bot‑network activity, recorded a 12 % drop in bot‑related traffic in the first week after the strikes but a return to baseline levels within two weeks<sup>6</sup>.

Background and context

The Ukraine conflict has seen cyber operations play an increasingly prominent role since the 2014‑15 power‑grid attacks, which targeted Ukraine’s electricity distribution network and caused outages for 225,000 customers<sup>7</sup>. Those early incidents highlighted the vulnerability of critical infrastructure to coordinated cyber‑attacks and set a precedent for subsequent operations on both sides. Ukraine has since developed a suite of offensive cyber tools, including malware designed to infiltrate command‑and‑control servers and disrupt enemy communications. These tools have been used in support of conventional military objectives, blurring the line between cyber and kinetic warfare<sup>8</sup>.

Disinformation bots automate the creation and spread of content across social media platforms. They rely on cloud computing resources to scale their reach and to evade detection. The bots’ effectiveness is tied to the stability and availability of the underlying data‑centre infrastructure. When a bot network’s servers are taken offline, the bots lose the computational power necessary to generate and spread propaganda at scale. This dependency makes data‑centres a logical target for states seeking to undermine an adversary’s information campaign<sup>9</sup>.

Targeting data‑centres is not without risk. These facilities often host critical civilian services, including telecommunications, banking and emergency response systems. Attacks on such infrastructure can have unintended consequences for the civilian population, raising legal and ethical questions under international humanitarian law (IHL). The Ukrainian military’s stated objective in the recent strikes was to reduce the influence of Russian propaganda, but the potential collateral damage remains a point of contention in the debate over cyber‑norms and proportionality<sup>10</sup>.

Technical analysis of bot infrastructure

Bot networks typically use a mix of public cloud services (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud) and domestic providers (Yandex.Cloud, Russian‑based “DataSphere”). They employ distributed command‑and‑control (C&C) servers to coordinate activity, often using DNS tunneling and encrypted traffic to evade detection. The Ukrainian strikes reportedly targeted a cluster of servers that hosted the primary C&C nodes for a Russian bot network identified as “Operation Echo.” According to CrowdStrike, the network’s architecture relied on a single high‑availability cluster that could be disrupted by a well‑timed cyber‑attack<sup>6</sup>.

However, bot operators routinely employ redundancy measures such as multi‑cloud hosting, automated failover and rapid migration scripts. Bellingcat’s analysis of leaked logs showed that within 48 hours of the Ukrainian strikes, the bot network had re‑established a new C&C cluster on a different cloud provider, restoring its ability to generate content<sup>4</sup>. This rapid adaptation underscores the resilience of modern bot networks and suggests that a single data‑centre strike is unlikely to achieve lasting disruption.

Legal and ethical considerations

International law distinguishes between legitimate military targets and protected civilian objects, but the line becomes blurred when infrastructure serves dual civilian and military functions. The Tallinn Manual 2.0 clarifies that attacks on dual‑use infrastructure must meet the principles of distinction, proportionality and necessity<sup>11</sup>. Dr. Anna Karpova, professor of IHL at the University of Helsinki, notes that “attacks on data‑centres that also support civilian services must be carefully weighed against the potential for civilian harm”<sup>12</sup>.

The Ukrainian Ministry of Defence has argued that the data‑centres targeted were “primarily used for military communications and disinformation operations,” thereby qualifying as legitimate military objectives. Russian officials, however, contend that the facilities also supported civilian telecommunications and banking services, rendering them protected objects<sup>3</sup>. The lack of independent verification of the dual‑use nature of the facilities complicates the assessment of legality.

Ethically, the use of cyber‑operations to suppress disinformation raises questions about free speech and the potential for abuse. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) has called for “clear guidelines on the use of cyber‑tools in armed conflict” to prevent the weaponization of information<sup>13</sup>. The Ukrainian strikes, if deemed legitimate, could set a precedent for future cyber‑counter‑propaganda operations, but they also risk normalizing attacks on civilian infrastructure under the guise of information warfare.

Future implications

Official statements from Russian cyber‑security agencies or intelligence services will be key to assessing the true impact of the data‑centre strikes. If Russian agencies confirm that bot networks have fully recovered or migrated, it would suggest limited efficacy of the Ukrainian attacks. Conversely, evidence of sustained disruption would support the Ukrainian narrative and could influence future cyber‑strategies.

The evolution of bot networks’ resilience will shape the future of information warfare. If bot operators increasingly adopt decentralized or distributed cloud architectures, targeting individual data‑centres may become less effective. Conversely, if centralized infrastructures remain common, precision strikes could become a more viable tool for states seeking to curb propaganda. The balance between offensive cyber capabilities and defensive resilience will therefore remain a central theme in future conflicts.

Finally, the international community’s response to these strikes will influence the development of norms and treaties governing cyber warfare. If the attacks are widely perceived as violating civilian protection norms, there may be calls for stricter regulations on cyber‑attacks targeting critical infrastructure. Alternatively, if the strikes are deemed a legitimate defensive measure against disinformation, they could be cited as a precedent for future cyber‑operations aimed at countering propaganda. The outcome of these debates will shape the legal and ethical landscape of cyber warfare for years to come<sup>14</sup>.

In sum, Zelensky’s claim that Ukrainian strikes have “taken down” Russian bots is partially supported by evidence of temporary disruption but is far from conclusive. The attacks illustrate the growing sophistication of hybrid warfare, the challenges of attributing cyber‑operations and the urgent need for clearer international norms governing the use of cyber‑tools against civilian infrastructure. As the conflict continues, the world will watch closely to see whether these strikes herald a new era of cyber‑counter‑propaganda or merely a flash in the pan of a war that has already blurred the boundaries between the physical and the digital.
