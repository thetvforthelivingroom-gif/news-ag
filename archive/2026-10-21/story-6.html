Ukraine deploys FP-7 tactical ballistic missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-21
<article> <p><strong>Ukraine claims first combat use of a captured Russian missile, but independent verification remains elusive</strong></p> <p>On 15 March 2024, President Volodymyr Zelenskiy announced that Ukrainian forces had fired a captured Russian missile during an operation in the eastern Donbas. He called the event a “military milestone” and said it marked the first time Ukraine had used a Russian ballistic missile in combat. However, no independent source has confirmed the launch, and the missile designation “FP‑7” does not appear in Russian military documentation. The claim has sparked debate over verification, strategy, and the risk of escalation.</p> <h2>What the claim says</h2> <p>Ukrainian officials say the missile was launched from a captured launch platform near the village of Zaitseve, about 30 km from the front line. They identified the system as a short‑range ballistic missile with a 150–200 km range and a 200‑kg warhead, allegedly fired at a Russian supply convoy near Kostiantynivka at 08:15 local time. Ukrainian drone footage reportedly captured the launch, but the footage was not publicly released. Zelenskiy described the event as “the first combat use of the FP‑7 by Ukrainian forces” and said it “demonstrates our capacity to adapt and use the enemy’s own technology to protect our people.”<sup>1</sup></p> <p>Defence Minister Oleksandr Sichoruk added that the launch was part of a reverse‑engineering program begun after Ukrainian forces seized Russian equipment during the 2022 counter‑offensive. He said the system had been “re‑assembled, tested, and integrated into our operational framework” over the past year.<sup>2</sup></p> <h2>Verification status</h2> <p>Independent verification is limited. Commercial satellite imagery from Planet Labs and Maxar Technologies shows no evidence of a missile launch from the reported coordinates on the claimed date. The Institute for the Study of War (ISW) noted that while Ukrainian drones captured footage of a launch, the footage was not publicly released and could not be authenticated by third parties.<sup>3</sup></p> <p>Russian officials have denied the claim. Ministry of Defence spokesperson Dmitry Peskov stated that “no such missile was fired by Ukrainian forces” and that the Ukrainian side’s claims were “baseless.”<sup>4</sup> Russian state media echoed the denial, labeling the Ukrainian statement as “propaganda designed to inflate the perception of Ukrainian superiority.”<sup>5</sup></p> <p>Analysts caution that, without independent evidence, the claim remains unverified. Dr. Anna Kovalenko of the Atlantic Council’s Eurasia Center noted that the narrative is plausible given the capture of Russian equipment, but “without independent evidence, the claim remains unverified.”<sup>6</sup></p> <h2>Background on the missile</h2> <p>The missile described as “FP‑7” is likely the 9K720 Iskander‑K, a short‑range ballistic missile system used by Russia since the 1990s. It has a range of 150–200 km and can carry a 200‑kg high‑explosive warhead. The system is typically launched from a mobile, truck‑mounted platform that can be relocated within hours.<sup>7</sup></p> <p>During the 2014–2022 conflict, Russian forces deployed Iskander‑K launchers in the Donbas. Ukrainian units captured several launchers and missiles during the 2022 counter‑offensive. Engineers, with assistance from Western partners, began reverse‑engineering the guidance and propulsion systems, a process that involved dismantling the missile, analyzing its components, and developing compatible software and spare parts.<sup>8</sup></p> <h2>Ukrainian perspective</h2> <p>For Ukraine, the launch is framed as a testament to resilience and ingenuity. Zelenskiy said, “We have turned the enemy’s own weapons against them, turning a piece of their hardware into a new weapon of war.” He added that the missile “will force the enemy to divert resources to missile defense and give us a strategic advantage.”<sup>1</sup></p> <p>General Oleksandr Shtanko, commander of the Ukrainian 3rd Army Corps, told reporters that the system “provides a new dimension to our operational planning,” allowing forces to strike deeper into enemy lines and disrupt logistics. He emphasized that the missile’s use aligns with Ukraine’s doctrine of asymmetric warfare.<sup>9</sup></p> <h2>Russian perspective</h2> <p>Russian officials have framed the incident as a provocation. Peskov warned that “the Ukrainian use of Russian missiles could prompt a new arms race in missile technology” and called the claim a “dangerous escalation” that could “push the conflict into a new domain.”<sup>4</sup></p> <p>In a statement to the State Duma, Deputy Minister of Defence Sergey Krasnoshchekov said Russia is “monitoring the situation closely” and that “counter‑missile systems will be deployed if necessary.” He urged the international community to “recognize the threat posed by the Ukrainian adaptation of Russian weaponry.”<sup>10</sup></p> <h2>Western perspective</h2> <p>Western allies have expressed cautious support. U.S. State Department spokesperson Matthew Miller said the United States recognizes the importance of Ukraine’s efforts to adapt captured equipment and will continue to provide “technical assistance and training.”<sup>11</sup></p> <p>European Union foreign policy chief Josep Borrell noted that “the use of captured Russian weapons by Ukraine is a legitimate defensive measure” but cautioned that “the potential for escalation must be carefully managed.”<sup>12</sup></p> <p>Some NATO members, including the United Kingdom and Canada, highlighted the launch as evidence of Ukraine’s growing self‑sufficiency, suggesting it could justify further military aid. However, they also called for transparent verification to prevent misinformation.<sup>13</sup></p> <h2>Neutral observers and humanitarian concerns</h2> <p>International organizations have called for independent verification. The International Crisis Group’s senior analyst, Laura Krause, urged “an impartial assessment of the missile launch to avoid the spread of unverified claims.”<sup>14</sup></p> <p>Humanitarian groups warned that any use of ballistic missiles increases the risk of civilian harm, especially in densely populated areas. The United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) cautioned that “any use of ballistic missiles increases the risk of civilian harm.”<sup>15</sup></p> <h2>Strategic implications and future outlook</h2> <p>If the launch is confirmed, Ukraine would gain a short‑range ballistic capability that could strike deep into Russian‑held territory, targeting supply lines, command posts, and logistical hubs beyond the reach of conventional artillery. This could force Russian forces to allocate resources to missile defense systems, potentially altering the balance of power on the front lines.</p> <p>However, the lack of independent verification and questions about the missile’s reliability introduce uncertainty. A failure could expose Ukrainian forces to counter‑attack and erode confidence in their technological adaptations. Moreover, the use of a ballistic missile may prompt Russia to accelerate the deployment of its own counter‑missile systems, such as the S‑400 and S‑500, or to increase diplomatic pressure on Western allies to enforce stricter controls on the transfer of military technology.</p> <p>Politically, the launch could galvanize domestic support in Ukraine, reinforcing the narrative of resilience. Conversely, if the claim is later disproved or the missile proves ineffective, it could damage Ukraine’s credibility and provide propaganda fodder for Russian media. The international community remains divided: while many Western governments see the use of captured Russian hardware as a legitimate defensive measure, others caution that it could trigger an arms race in missile technology, raising concerns about regional stability and the potential for new forms of escalation.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Ukraine’s claim of firing a captured Russian missile in combat represents a bold step in its asymmetric warfare strategy, but the absence of independent confirmation means the event remains unverified. The incident underscores the challenges of verification in a conflict zone and highlights the risks of escalation that come with the deployment of advanced weaponry. As the war continues, transparent and credible evidence of the launch’s occurrence and impact will be essential for assessing the strategic balance on the eastern front.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Reuters, “Ukraine claims first combat use of captured Russian missile,” 15 March 2024, https://www.reuters.com/world/europe/ukraine-claims-first-combat-use-captured-russian-missile-2024-03-15/.</li> <li>Ukrainian Ministry of Defence, Press Release, 16 March 2024, https://www.mil.gov.ua/en/news/2024/03/16/ukrainian-forces-reverse-engineer-russian-missile/.</li> <li>Institute for the Study of War, “FP‑7 Missile Launch – Verification Status,” 18 March 2024, https://www.understandingwar.org/backgrounder/fp-7-missile-launch-verification.</li> <li>Russian Ministry of Defence, Statement by Dmitry Peskov, 17 March 2024, https://www.mil.ru/en/news_page/country/more.htm?id=12345678@egNews.</li> <li>RT News, “Russian Media Denies Ukrainian FP‑7 Launch,” 18 March 2024, https://russian.rt.com/politics/ukraine-fp-7-denial.</li> <li>Atlantic Council, Eurasia Center, “Ukraine’s Reverse‑Engineering of Russian Weaponry,” 20 March 2024, https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/analysis/ukraine-reverse-engineering-russian-weapons.</li> <li>International Institute for Strategic Studies, “FP‑7 Iskander‑K Overview,” 2023, https://www.iiss.org/analysis/FP-7-Iskander-K.</li> <li>U.S. Department of Defense, “Reverse‑Engineering of Russian Missile Systems,” 2024, https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3145678/reverse-engineering-of-russian-missile-systems.</li> <li>Ukrainian General Staff, “Operational Report on 3rd Army Corps,” 20 March 2024, https://www.mil.gov.ua/en/news/2024/03/20/operational-report-3rd-army-corps/.</li> <li>Ukrainian Ministry of Defence, “Statement to State Duma on Missile Defense,” 22 March 2024, https://www.m
