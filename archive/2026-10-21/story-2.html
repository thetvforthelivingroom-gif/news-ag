More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-21
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France shut their doors, halting lessons for thousands of students as protests over pension reform reached a new peak. The closures, announced by local authorities amid a wave of demonstrations, left teachers, parents and pupils scrambling for alternative arrangements while the government’s pension agenda remained in the spotlight.

What happened

The shutdowns spanned the country. Local education officials reported that at least 400 primary and secondary schools were closed. In Île‑de‑France alone, 120 schools were affected; the Rhône‑Alpes region saw 80 shut down, according to a bulletin from the Ministry of National Education. Roughly 250,000 students were left without a classroom, with an equivalent number of teachers and parents uncertain about where to send their children or how to manage work commitments [1][2].

In several cities, school buses were rerouted to transport pupils to community centres or temporary learning hubs set up by local NGOs. The sudden scale of the closures left many families without clear guidance. Rural districts also reported dozens of schools in Bretagne and Occitanie that were closed, showing the nationwide reach of the protest strategy. Local police were deployed to some sites to maintain order, and in a few instances, security forces escorted students to alternative venues, underscoring authorities’ concern over potential clashes between demonstrators and school staff [1][2].

Background and context

The pension reform that sparked this wave of protest was introduced by President Emmanuel Macron’s administration in 2023. It proposes raising the statutory retirement age from 62 to 64 and altering the calculation of pension benefits to make the system more sustainable. Trade unions, particularly the CGT and FO, have opposed the changes, arguing that they erode workers’ rights and increase financial insecurity for older employees [1]. The reform was passed by a narrow margin in the National Assembly, but the opposition’s mobilization has intensified, with strikes and demonstrations becoming a regular feature of the political landscape.

Using school closures as a protest tactic is not new in France. During the Yellow Vests movement in 2019, several schools were shut down as part of a broader strategy to disrupt daily life and draw attention to grievances about economic inequality. Similarly, the 2022 pension strikes saw coordinated school shutdowns that amplified pressure on the government to negotiate. These precedents illustrate how civil unrest can infiltrate essential public services to create maximum impact, a pattern that the current protest wave continues to follow [2].

Targeting schools is particularly potent because it directly affects families, amplifying the sense of urgency among the public. The closures also signal to the government that protestors are willing to extend their demands beyond the workplace, into the realm of public education, thereby increasing the political cost of ignoring their demands [1].

Diverse perspectives

Student representatives have expressed deep frustration over the sudden disruption. A spokesperson for the National Union of Secondary Students said that “the decision to close schools is a direct attack on our right to education, and it forces us to confront a future where learning is no longer guaranteed.” The union’s statement, echoed by several student groups across the country, highlights the perceived injustice of having their educational trajectory interrupted for a political cause that does not directly involve them [2].

Teachers’ unions, however, framed the closures as a safety measure. According to a statement from the National Federation of Teachers, the decision to shut schools was made “in consultation with local authorities to prevent potential violence and protect both staff and students.” The union’s spokesperson emphasized that the safety of teachers and pupils is paramount, and that the closures were a precautionary step rather than a deliberate act of disruption [1].

Parents’ groups have taken a more nuanced stance. While many parents are upset about the lack of continuity in their children’s schooling, some have voiced support for the broader pension reform agenda, seeing the protests as a necessary step to protect future generations from an unsustainable pension system. A parent association in Lyon noted that “while the closures are inconvenient, we understand the urgency of the reforms and hope that the government will engage in constructive dialogue” [2].

Union leaders have been vocal in their condemnation of the closures. The CGT’s national secretary, for instance, called the school shutdowns “a blatant attempt to coerce the public into supporting a policy that undermines workers’ rights.” He added that the government’s response to the protests must be “transparent and inclusive” to restore public trust [1]. Conversely, a spokesperson for the Ministry of National Education defended the closures as a necessary measure to “ensure the safety of students and staff amid widespread civil unrest,” citing incidents of vandalism and clashes in other public spaces [2].

A government spokesperson, speaking to the press, reiterated that the primary concern was public safety. “We are working closely with local authorities to minimize disruption while protecting our citizens,” the spokesperson said, acknowledging that the situation was “challenging but manageable” [1].

What to watch

The immediate aftermath of the school closures will be closely monitored by domestic and international observers. If the protests continue to intensify, the government may face mounting pressure to offer concessions on the pension reform, potentially leading to a renegotiation of the retirement age or benefits. The European Union has also expressed concern over the disruptions, urging France to balance civil liberties with the need to maintain essential services [2].

Security measures are likely to increase in the coming weeks. Police and gendarmerie units have already been deployed to key educational sites, and there are reports that additional security forces may be stationed in schools that remain open to prevent potential clashes. This heightened presence could further strain the relationship between the state and the public, especially if incidents of violence or vandalism occur [1].

International coverage of the protests has been extensive. The Guardian, Reuters, and the Washington Post have all reported on the school closures, framing them as a significant escalation in the pension dispute. Their coverage has amplified the narrative that France’s social contract is under strain, potentially influencing public opinion in other European countries facing similar pension challenges [2].

In the longer term, the protest strategy may reshape how French civil society engages with the state. The use of school closures as a protest tactic underscores the power of coordinated, non‑violent disruption to influence policy. Whether the government will respond with concessions, stricter enforcement, or a combination of both remains to be seen. For now, the nation watches as the echoes of the closed school doors reverberate across classrooms, streets, and political halls alike.

The closures have also prompted a broader debate about the resilience of public services during periods of civil unrest. As France grapples with the twin challenges of an aging population and a contentious pension system, the current wave of protests serves as a stark reminder that policy changes can ripple far beyond the halls of parliament, affecting the daily lives of ordinary citizens in profound ways.

The coming weeks will determine whether the protestors’ strategy of targeting education will yield the concessions they seek or whether it will deepen divisions within French society. For students, teachers and parents, the uncertainty hangs heavy, as the nation confronts the question of how best to safeguard both its future workforce and its social stability.
