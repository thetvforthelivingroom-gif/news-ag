More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-10
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France will close as protests intensify, marking a new chapter in the country’s long‑running struggle between citizens and the state. The shutdowns come amid a wave of demonstrations that have already disrupted public services, leaving teachers, students and local authorities caught in the cross‑fire.

What happened

A coordinated effort by protest groups has led to the shutdown of over 400 educational institutions, from primary schools to secondary academies, in cities and rural communes alike. The Ministry of Education has yet to issue a formal directive authorising the closures, raising questions about the legality of the action and the extent of the protestors’ influence on local decision‑making [4]. In the past week, more than 400 separate protests have been documented across the country, many of them centred on the same demands that have driven the current wave of civil unrest [4].

The shutdowns are part of a broader strategy to pressure the government ahead of upcoming legislative debates. By halting the daily rhythm of schooling, protestors aim to bring the issue of workers’ rights and public spending to the forefront of the national conversation. The closures have already prompted a flurry of responses from teachers’ unions, local councils, and law‑enforcement agencies, each grappling with the logistical and legal implications of shutting down public schools without an official mandate.

Background and context

The current wave of protests is not an isolated phenomenon. France’s history of civil disobedience offers a clear backdrop. In 2018, the Yellow Vests movement erupted in response to fuel‑tax hikes and broader economic grievances, and it quickly grew into a nationwide protest movement that disrupted transportation, commerce and public services [3]. A few years later, in 2023, a nationwide strike over pension reforms saw thousands of workers, including teachers, walk out of their jobs, further illustrating the capacity of organised labour to influence national policy through coordinated action [2].

These movements share a common thread: a perception that the state is pursuing reforms that erode workers’ rights and increase living costs without adequate consultation. The current protests echo those sentiments, with protestors arguing that the government’s fiscal policies disproportionately burden ordinary citizens and that public services are being cut or restructured in ways that undermine quality and accessibility [5]. The decision to target schools is a strategic escalation, signalling that the protestors are willing to disrupt the most fundamental public service in an effort to compel policy change.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Protestors

For many protestors, the school closures are a form of civil disobedience aimed at drawing attention to systemic inequities. In interviews with local activists, the narrative is clear: the state has repeatedly failed to address the erosion of workers’ rights and the rising cost of living, and the only way to force a conversation is to disrupt the status quo. Protestors describe the closures as a “necessary sacrifice” that will ultimately benefit students and teachers by forcing the government to reconsider its fiscal agenda [1].

Teachers and students

Teachers have expressed mixed feelings. While some see the closures as a powerful statement against a government they view as unsympathetic to public‑sector concerns, others worry about the practical implications for their students. In rural areas, where schools often serve as community hubs, the closures could mean longer travel distances for children and a loss of essential services such as meals and after‑school care. Teachers’ unions have called for a clear legal framework to guide the closures, emphasizing the need for protection of teachers’ job security and safety during the protests [6].

Students, particularly those in remote regions, have reported uncertainty about where they will attend classes. Some schools have tried to pivot to online learning, but internet access remains uneven across the country. The closures have sparked a debate about the digital divide and the adequacy of remote‑education infrastructure in France [7].

Local authorities

Municipal councils are in a difficult position. While they are legally empowered to make decisions about school operations, the lack of a formal directive from the Ministry of Education leaves them in a legal gray area. Some local officials have expressed willingness to support the protestors’ demands, citing the need to protect workers’ rights, while others fear the potential backlash from parents and the national government. The tension between local autonomy and national policy is a recurring theme in French governance, and the current crisis has amplified it [8].

Police and law enforcement

Police forces have been dispatched to several schools to maintain order and prevent vandalism. While most protests have remained peaceful, there have been sporadic clashes between protestors and law‑enforcement officers. Police statements emphasise that their role is to ensure safety and that any violence will be met with a forceful response. The presence of police has added a layer of complexity to the situation, raising questions about the appropriate balance between maintaining public order and respecting the right to protest [9].

Media framing

The media coverage of the school closures has been sharply divided. Some outlets portray protestors as “freedom fighters” who are standing up against a government that prioritises austerity over public welfare. Others frame the closures as “disruptive agitators” who are causing unnecessary hardship for students and teachers. This dichotomy reflects the broader polarization in French society over the role of the state and the legitimacy of civil disobedience as a political tool [10].

What to watch (future implications)

Legal challenges

The absence of a formal directive from the Ministry of Education opens the door for legal challenges. Courts may be asked to determine whether the closures violate statutory protections for public education and the rights of students and teachers. The outcome could set a precedent for how protest‑driven actions are treated under French law, potentially influencing future labour disputes.

Political fallout

If the closures persist, the government may face mounting pressure to negotiate with protestors or to introduce new legislation that addresses their concerns. Politicians at all levels will need to balance the demands of the protest movement with the practicalities of maintaining essential public services. The situation could also influence upcoming elections, as voters weigh the cost of disruption against the promise of reform.

Social cohesion

The school closures have highlighted deep divisions within French society. While some view the protests as a legitimate expression of dissent, others see them as an attack on the nation’s educational system. How the government and civil society respond will determine whether the country can move forward with a renewed sense of unity or whether the fractures will widen.

Long‑term reforms

The current crisis may accelerate discussions about the future of public education in France. Questions about funding, curriculum, and the role of technology in learning are already on the agenda. The protestors’ demands for greater transparency and worker protection could lead to reforms that reshape the relationship between teachers, students and the state.

In the coming weeks, the nation will watch closely as the government, protestors, teachers, students and local authorities navigate a complex landscape of legal, political and social challenges. The outcome will shape not only the immediate future of France’s schools but also the broader trajectory of civil engagement and public policy in the country.
