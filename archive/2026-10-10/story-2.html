More than 400 schools in France to be closed on Monday as more protests

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-10
On Monday, more than 400 schools across France shut their doors, turning classrooms into silent witnesses to a protest that has once again turned the nation’s education system into a battleground for a pension reform many workers say is unjust. The closures, announced by unions early that morning, halted learning for roughly 400,000 students and left teachers scrambling for alternative arrangements, while the government warned of legal repercussions for the striking sectors.

What happened

Union leaders representing teachers, civil servants, and other public employees announced that all state schools would be closed for the day as part of a coordinated strike that also targeted transport services and public administration. The decision, taken by the French National Union of Teachers (SNF) and the Union of Public Service Employees (UNES), aimed to pressure the government into revisiting its pension reform plan. The closures were announced on social media and confirmed by the Ministry of National Education in a brief statement that noted the “unprecedented scale of the action” but assured that “remote learning options would be made available” [T2].

In cities from Paris to Marseille to Lyon, school buses were parked and teachers were left without classrooms. Some schools tried to shift to online lessons, but internet connectivity issues and the lack of digital resources for many students made the transition uneven. In the suburbs of Paris, a school in the 12th arrondissement reported that a group of students had to leave the premises after a confrontation with police who were attempting to secure the building for safety reasons [T3].

The government’s response was swift. Prime Minister Élisabeth Borne issued a statement that the state would “take all necessary measures to ensure the safety of students and staff” and warned that the union’s actions “could lead to legal action against those who violate the law” [T2]. The Ministry of Labour also released a press brief, noting that the closures were “in direct violation of the national education law” and that “the state reserves the right to prosecute those responsible for disrupting the educational system” [T2].

Background and context

Pension reform

The root of the protest lies in President Emmanuel Macron’s pension reform, which proposes to raise the retirement age from 62 to 64, extend the calculation period for pension points, and reduce the number of years required to qualify for a full pension. The reform also seeks to merge the country’s 42 separate pension schemes into a single, points‑based system. While the government argues that the changes are necessary to ensure the sustainability of France’s pension system, unions contend that they disproportionately affect lower‑wage workers and those in physically demanding jobs, who would have to work longer for less benefit [T2].

Union strategy

The union strategy has evolved over the past few years. In 2016–2017, during the first wave of pension reform protests, unions used school closures as a tactic to disrupt daily life and draw attention to their cause. Those closures were limited to a few dozen schools and met with limited public backlash. The 2019 Yellow Vests movement also saw sporadic school closures, but these were largely spontaneous and localized. The current wave of closures marks a significant escalation, with more than 400 schools participating, the largest coordinated shutdown in recent French history [T3].

Historical echoes

The pattern of using school closures as a protest tactic is not new in France. During the 2016–2017 pension reform protests, unions closed about 200 schools, disrupting learning for approximately 200,000 students. The 2019 Yellow Vests protests saw a handful of schools shut down in response to local demonstrations, but these were largely uncoordinated and had no national impact. The current closures are the most extensive since those earlier waves, indicating a shift in the unions’ willingness to leverage education as a bargaining tool [T2].

The bigger picture

Human impact

For students, the closures mean a sudden halt to in‑person instruction and a shift to remote learning that is unevenly distributed. In rural areas, where broadband access is limited, many students have no viable alternative. Teachers face the challenge of preparing online lessons on short notice, often without adequate training or resources. A teacher in Lyon, who wished to remain anonymous, expressed frustration: “We are being asked to do something we were not trained for, and we are being forced to take a stand that affects our students’ futures” [T3].

Parents are also caught in the crossfire. A mother in Marseille reported that her son, a 10th‑grade student, had to miss a critical exam because the school was closed. “I understand the protest, but my son’s education is being compromised,” she said. “We need a solution that does not leave students behind” [T3].

Public safety and continuity

The government’s emphasis on safety led to the deployment of police officers to secure school premises during the closures. In several instances, officers escorted students out of schools, citing concerns about potential clashes between demonstrators and staff. While no major incidents were reported, isolated cases of vandalism and property damage occurred in a few schools that were not adequately secured [T2].

To mitigate the impact on learning, the Ministry of National Education announced that it would provide additional resources for remote learning, including the distribution of laptops and internet vouchers to students in underserved areas. The rollout is expected to take several weeks, meaning that many students will face a gap in their education for at least a week [T2].

Political stakes

The protests come at a critical juncture in French politics. President Macron’s government is under pressure from both the left and the right to deliver on its promises of economic reform while maintaining social cohesion. The upcoming municipal elections, scheduled for March, could be influenced by public sentiment toward the pension reform. If the protests continue to disrupt daily life, the government may face mounting calls to either roll back the reforms or offer concessions to the unions.

In a recent interview, a senior union official from the SNF said the closures were a “last resort” and that the unions were open to dialogue if the government shows willingness to negotiate. “We are not against progress, but we demand fairness,” the official said [T3]. The government, meanwhile, has reiterated that the pension reform is “necessary for the long‑term health of the nation” and will not be altered in the face of protests [T2].

What to watch

Potential concessions

If the protests intensify, the government may be forced to consider concessions. In 2016, the government temporarily suspended the pension reform in the face of widespread strikes, only to revive it later. Observers will watch closely for any signs that the government might offer a compromise, such as a gradual increase in the retirement age or additional benefits for certain sectors.

Legal ramifications

The threat of legal action against union leaders and striking workers is a significant factor. The Ministry of Labour’s statement that “the state reserves the right to prosecute those responsible for disrupting the educational system” could lead to court cases that may deter future large‑scale closures. However, unions argue that such legal action would deepen the divide and that the protests are a legitimate exercise of collective bargaining rights.

Impact on education policy

The current wave of school closures may prompt a reevaluation of France’s approach to education policy during periods of civil unrest. The government may consider establishing contingency plans that allow schools to remain open or to transition more smoothly to remote learning in the event of future strikes. The experience of the current closures could also influence how the government balances the need for public order with the protection of students’ right to education.

Public sentiment

Public opinion polls indicate a growing fatigue with the pension reform debate. While a majority of French citizens support the need for pension reform, a significant minority feel that the reforms are too harsh. The ongoing school closures may shift public sentiment, either galvanizing support for the unions or eroding sympathy for their tactics. The government will need to gauge this sentiment carefully as it navigates the political landscape leading up to the municipal elections.

In the weeks ahead, the French government and the unions will continue to negotiate, while students, teachers, and parents will navigate the immediate fallout of the closures. The outcome of these negotiations will likely shape the future of France’s pension system, its educational continuity, and the broader relationship between the state and its civil servants. The school closures, a new front in the pension reform battle, underscore how deeply intertwined policy, protest, and everyday life can become in a nation grappling with change.
