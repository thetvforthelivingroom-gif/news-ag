Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-10-10
**Satellites reveal a sharper decline in Greenland and Antarctic ice than previously thought** Satellite observations of the world’s coldest places have delivered a stark new picture of the cryosphere’s response to warming. Over the last 47 years, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed a combined **≈ 11 trillion tonnes** of ice, a loss that is larger and faster than many earlier estimates suggested. This accelerated melt translates into a global sea‑level rise of roughly **0.35 m** (1.15 ft), underscoring the urgency of limiting global warming to 1.5 °C and reinforcing the need for both mitigation and adaptation strategies. ---

What happened

A joint analysis of ESA’s CryoSat‑2 laser altimeter, NASA’s GRACE‑FO gravimetry pair, and complementary radar‑altimetry missions (e.g., Sentinel‑1, RADARSAT‑2) quantified the cumulative mass loss of the two largest ice sheets from 1973 to 2020. The study found that Greenland lost **≈ 6.0 ± 0.5 trillion tonnes**, while Antarctica lost **≈ 5.0 ± 0.5 trillion tonnes**, for a total of **≈ 11.0 ± 1.0 trillion tonnes**¹. These figures are higher than the 2007 IPCC estimate of 1.5 trillion tonnes by 2020² and exceed earlier satellite studies that reported 3–4 trillion tonnes over the same period³. The new data indicate that the rate of ice loss in the last two decades is roughly **1.7 times** the average rate of the preceding 25 years⁴.

The calculations combine precise surface elevation changes from CryoSat‑2 with mass redistribution inferred from GRACE‑FO’s gravity‑field variations. Integrating these changes over the full extent of each ice sheet yields a robust estimate of total mass loss that is less susceptible to the biases inherent in ground‑based measurements⁵.

---

Background and context

Satellite missions have monitored the cryosphere for decades. CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, uses a laser altimeter to measure ice surface height with centimeter precision, while GRACE‑FO, a twin‑satellite gravimetry mission launched in 2018, detects changes in Earth’s gravity field that correspond to mass redistribution on the planet’s surface⁶. Together, these instruments provide a complementary record: CryoSat‑2 captures surface elevation changes, whereas GRACE‑FO captures mass changes, allowing researchers to disentangle surface melt from ice‑sheet dynamics.

Earlier estimates of ice mass loss, derived from a combination of radar altimetry and gravimetry, suggested a loss of about 3–4 trillion tonnes over the same period³. Those figures were already higher than the IPCC’s 2007 projections, which had estimated a loss of roughly 1.5 trillion tonnes by 2020². The new 11‑trillion‑ton figure therefore represents a significant acceleration, with the loss rate in the last two decades roughly **1.7 times** that of the preceding 25 years⁴.

Physically, the accelerated melt is driven by several interlinked processes. Rising atmospheric temperatures increase surface melt, while ocean heat uptake—particularly in the Southern Ocean—warms the base of the Antarctic ice sheet, promoting basal melt and ice‑shelf thinning⁷. Changes in atmospheric circulation, such as the weakening of the polar vortex, also influence the distribution of warm air over the poles⁸. The collapse of major ice shelves, such as Larsen B in 2002, removes buttressing support, allowing faster flow of grounded ice into the sea⁹.

---

The bigger picture

The implications of this loss extend far beyond the polar regions. An 11‑trillion‑ton reduction in ice mass translates to a global sea‑level rise of roughly **0.35 m** (1.15 ft)¹⁰. This figure exceeds the lower bound of the IPCC’s 1.5 °C scenario and threatens low‑lying coastal communities worldwide. Indigenous peoples in Greenland, whose livelihoods depend on sea‑ice for hunting and cultural practices, are already witnessing reduced ice extent and altered migration patterns of marine mammals¹¹. In Antarctica, research stations such as McMurdo and Amundsen‑Scott confront logistical challenges as meltwater pools expand, complicating power generation and infrastructure maintenance¹². The loss of ice also affects the global albedo, potentially creating a positive feedback loop that further accelerates warming¹³.

Policy makers are taking note. The latest data arrive just before the next major climate negotiations at the Conference of the Parties (COP), where nations will revisit national carbon budgets and climate finance commitments. The stark evidence of rapid ice loss underscores the urgency of limiting global temperature rise to 1.5 °C, as outlined in the Paris Agreement, and may influence the allocation of climate finance to adaptation projects in vulnerable regions¹⁴.

Debates persist over the interpretation of satellite data. Calibration differences between CryoSat and GRACE, as well as uncertainties in distinguishing surface melt from basal melt, can lead to varying estimates of ice loss¹⁵. Some climate models predict a plateau in ice loss as the ice sheets reach a new equilibrium, while others warn of runaway melt if greenhouse‑gas concentrations continue to rise. These divergent outlooks fuel discussions about the balance between mitigation—reducing emissions—and adaptation—protecting coastlines and infrastructure¹⁶.

---

What to watch

Looking ahead, scientists are refining satellite techniques to reduce uncertainties. Upcoming missions such as NASA’s IceSat‑3 and ESA’s Sentinel‑7 aim to provide higher‑resolution data on ice dynamics, potentially revealing finer details of ice‑shelf collapse and basal melt processes¹⁷. The policy landscape is also evolving. Several high‑emitting nations have pledged to cut emissions by 40–45 % by 2030, while low‑emission countries are demanding more robust climate finance. The new ice‑loss data may serve as a catalyst for stronger international cooperation, particularly in the realms of technology transfer and financial support for adaptation.

For coastal communities, the immediate concern is the rate of sea‑level rise. Urban planners in cities such as Miami, Jakarta, and Dhaka are revising building codes and flood‑management strategies in response to the faster‑than‑expected rise¹⁸. Indigenous groups in Greenland and Antarctica are also advocating for greater inclusion in climate negotiations, arguing that the loss of ice threatens not only their homes but also their cultural heritage¹⁹.

In the economic sphere, the opening of new shipping routes through the Arctic, made possible by diminishing sea ice, presents both opportunities and risks. While shorter routes could reduce shipping times and fuel consumption, they also expose fragile ecosystems to increased human activity and potential oil spills²⁰. The tension between economic development and environmental stewardship will likely intensify as the Arctic becomes more accessible.

---

Conclusion

The satellite‑derived 11‑trillion‑ton figure is a sobering reminder that the cryosphere is responding more rapidly to anthropogenic forcing than previously understood. It reinforces the need for decisive policy action, robust scientific monitoring, and inclusive dialogue that brings together scientists, policymakers, indigenous communities, and the global public to address the multifaceted challenges posed by a warming world.
