Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-29
Satellite data now shows that the planet has lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years—enough to raise global sea levels by up to two metres, threatening millions of lives and economies.

What the data reveal

In the late 1990s, the first dedicated satellite missions—CryoSat and the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE)—began measuring the mass of the world’s ice sheets with unprecedented precision. Over the last four and a half decades, successive observations have documented a steady and accelerating loss of ice from both Greenland and Antarctica. The cumulative loss now exceeds 12 trillion tons, a figure that translates into a potential sea‑level rise of roughly two metres if the trend continues. This is not a short‑term fluctuation but a long‑term trend, evident in the consistent downward trajectory of ice mass recorded by the satellite network.

The magnitude of the loss is striking when compared to the mass of the world’s oceans, which is about 1.4 × 10^21 kg. The 12 trillion‑ton figure represents roughly 0.8 % of the total ocean mass, yet it is enough to displace vast amounts of water onto coastlines. The implications are clear: rising seas, increased storm surges, and the erosion of coastal infrastructure.

Background and context

The first satellite measurements of ice mass began with CryoSat‑1 in 2005 and GRACE in 2002. These missions provided the first global, continuous record of ice‑sheet mass changes, allowing scientists to quantify the rate of loss with far greater accuracy than ground‑based methods alone. Subsequent missions—GRACE‑Follow‑On, CryoSat‑2, and the Sentinel‑Ice series—have refined these measurements, improving spatial resolution and reducing uncertainties.

The trend in ice loss has been confirmed by multiple independent studies and is reflected in the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) assessment reports. The IPCC’s Fifth Assessment Report (2013) and Sixth Assessment Report (2021) both highlighted the accelerating decline of Greenland and Antarctic ice, linking it to rising global temperatures. The most recent assessment underscores that the loss of ice mass has been a key contributor to the observed rise in sea levels over the past century.

Anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions are the primary driver of this loss. Warming of the atmosphere and ocean increases surface temperatures, which melt ice from the top and bottom of the ice sheets. The albedo effect—where melting ice exposes darker ocean or land surfaces—further amplifies warming. While natural climate variability, such as El Niño–Southern Oscillation events, can influence short‑term fluctuations, the long‑term trend is dominated by human‑induced warming.

Measurement uncertainties remain a challenge. Satellite data are limited by spatial resolution; small glaciers and ice streams can be under‑resolved, leading to potential underestimation of total loss. Sub‑glacial meltwater can also be difficult to detect, and converting mass loss to sea‑level rise requires assumptions about oceanic response and thermal expansion. Nevertheless, the consensus among climate scientists is that the observed trend is robust and unlikely to be an artifact of measurement error.

The broader impact

The loss of ice has far‑reaching consequences for a wide range of stakeholders. Coastal communities—particularly in low‑lying island nations and major urban centers—face heightened flood risk and infrastructure damage. Indigenous peoples in Greenland and the Antarctic region depend on the ice for cultural practices, hunting, and traditional livelihoods; the loss threatens their way of life.

Global economies are also at stake. The shipping industry relies on predictable sea routes; rising seas can alter currents and increase the frequency of extreme weather events that disrupt maritime traffic. Insurance companies must factor in higher flood and storm risks, potentially raising premiums and reducing coverage in vulnerable areas. Agriculture can be affected by changes in precipitation patterns and the availability of freshwater resources derived from meltwater.

Policy responses have evolved in tandem with the scientific evidence. The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, incorporated the need to limit global temperature rise to well below 2 °C, partly to mitigate the loss of ice. National emission‑reduction targets, such as those set by the European Union and the United States, have been shaped by the recognition that continued ice loss will exacerbate sea‑level rise. Adaptation strategies—such as the construction of sea‑walls, the relocation of communities, and the implementation of green infrastructure—are being pursued in vulnerable regions.

However, tensions remain. Some political actors question the urgency of the data, citing uncertainties or alternative interpretations of the satellite measurements. Others emphasize the need for immediate mitigation, arguing that reducing emissions is the only way to halt the downward trajectory of ice mass. The debate underscores the broader challenge of translating scientific consensus into decisive policy action, especially when economic interests and geopolitical considerations are involved.

Looking ahead

The trajectory of ice loss has clear implications for projected sea‑level rise. If the current rate of loss continues, models project a rise of 0.3–0.6 m by 2100 under moderate emission scenarios, and up to 1.0 m or more under high‑emission pathways. Beyond 2100, the potential for tipping points—such as the collapse of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet—could accelerate sea‑level rise beyond current projections.

Adaptation timelines are therefore critical. Many coastal cities are already investing in resilient infrastructure, but the pace of construction and funding must match the projected risks. International cooperation will be essential, as sea‑level rise does not respect national borders. The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals, particularly Goal 13 on climate action, emphasize the need for integrated mitigation and adaptation strategies.

Mitigation pathways hinge on reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions to below 50 % of 2010 levels by 2030, as outlined in the Paris Agreement. Achieving this requires a rapid transition to renewable energy, electrification of transport, and the adoption of carbon‑capture technologies. The scientific community continues to refine attribution studies to quantify the exact contribution of emissions to ice loss, providing a stronger basis for policy decisions.

In summary, satellite observations have provided a concrete, quantifiable measure of the planet’s ice loss—a loss that translates directly into rising seas and increased flood risk. The data underscore the urgency of both mitigation and adaptation efforts, and they highlight the interconnectedness of climate science, policy, and the livelihoods of communities worldwide. The next few decades will determine whether humanity can reverse or at least stabilize this trend, or whether the loss of ice will continue to reshape the world’s coastlines and economies.
