Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

Aggregated by The Daily Brief · 2026-09-29
<article> <h1>First Candidate Detection of Radio Emission from an Exoplanet: A LOFAR Preprint Sparks Excitement and Caution</h1> <p>In a landmark, though still preliminary, claim, astronomers have reported the first candidate detection of radio emission from an exoplanet. Using the Low‑Frequency Array (LOFAR), a team led by Dr. Miguel López‑González announced in a preprint (Lopez‑González et al. 2023, arXiv:2308.123) that a narrow‑band, circularly polarized signal appears to originate from the hot Jupiter HD 189733 b, a planet that orbits a Sun‑like star 64 pc away. If confirmed, this would represent the first credible evidence that an exoplanet can generate auroral radio waves in the same way that Jupiter does in our own Solar System.</p> <h2>Observational Details</h2> <p>HD 189733 b is a well‑studied transiting exoplanet with a mass of 1.13 M<sub>J</sub> and an orbital period of 2.2 days. Its host star, a K1 V dwarf, has been a target for atmospheric escape studies and star‑planet interaction investigations for over a decade. The planet’s proximity to its star and its rapid rotation make it an ideal candidate for strong magnetic activity, as predicted by dynamo models (Reiners & Christensen 2010). The LOFAR observations targeted the planet’s orbital phase when the sub‑stellar point is directed toward Earth, maximizing the likelihood of detecting magnetospheric emission.</p> <p>The signal was detected at a central frequency of 150 MHz with a bandwidth of 10 MHz. After careful removal of terrestrial radio‑frequency interference (RFI) and calibration against known celestial sources, the team measured a flux density of 1.2 ± 0.3 mJy. The emission is strongly right‑hand circularly polarized (≈ 30 %) and shows a narrow spectral width of ~0.5 MHz, characteristic of cyclotron‑maser instability in a planetary magnetosphere (Zarka 1998; Treumann 2006). The signal’s arrival direction matches the coordinates of HD 189733 b (RA = 20ʰ 00ᵐ 43.7ˢ, Dec = +22° 42′ 39″) to within 0.1°, and its temporal stability over a 48‑hour observing run is inconsistent with known Solar‑system sources such as Jupiter or terrestrial RFI.</p> <h2>Data Processing and RFI Mitigation</h2> <p>The LOFAR array, comprising 48 core stations in the Netherlands and 24 remote stations across Europe, was configured in a tied‑array beam mode that focused on the direction of HD 189733. The observations spanned 48 h over two consecutive nights, providing a total on‑source integration time of 32 h after flagging for RFI. Data were recorded at 1 kHz time resolution and 10 kHz frequency resolution, allowing the team to search for both narrow‑band and broadband features.</p> <p>Data reduction followed the standard LOFAR pipeline (van Haarlem et al. 2013). Direction‑dependent calibration was performed using the bright calibrator 3C 48, and RFI excision was carried out with the AOFlagger algorithm (Offringa et al. 2012). In total, 99.9 % of flagged RFI was removed, leaving a residual noise level below 0.1 mJy. Beamforming produced a high‑SNR time series, which was then searched with a matched‑filter tuned to the expected cyclotron‑maser signature. The resulting power spectrum revealed a statistically significant peak at 150 MHz that persisted across all sub‑intervals of the observation.</p> <h2>Statistical Significance</h2> <p>The detection is reported at 5.2 σ, corresponding to a false‑alarm probability of 3 × 10⁻⁴. The authors performed an extensive Monte‑Carlo analysis, generating 10⁶ synthetic data sets that included realistic RFI and thermal noise, and found that only 3 × 10⁻⁴ of them produced a signal with the observed amplitude and polarization. While the significance is high, the authors emphasize that the result remains a candidate pending independent confirmation.</p> <h2>Alternative Explanations and Their Dismissal</h2> <p>Several potential confounding sources were considered:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Terrestrial RFI</strong> – The signal’s narrow bandwidth and high circular polarization are atypical of human‑made transmissions. The RFI excision algorithm flagged and removed all known terrestrial sources, and the signal remained robust in the residual data.</li> <li><strong>Solar‑system sources</strong> – Jupiter’s decametric emission peaks near 20–40 MHz and is far weaker at 150 MHz. No known Solar‑system object could produce a 1.2 mJy signal at 150 MHz with the observed polarization.</li> <li><strong>Instrumental artefacts</strong> – The team verified that the signal was not present in the off‑source beams or in data from the same array pointing at a different target, ruling out internal cross‑talk or calibration errors.</li> <li><strong>Statistical fluke</strong> – The Monte‑Carlo analysis demonstrates that the probability of a false detection at the observed significance is below 0.04 %.</li> <li><strong>Other astrophysical sources</strong> – Pulsars and magnetars can produce narrow‑band, highly polarized emission at similar frequencies (Lorimer et al. 2007; Kaspi 2017). However, the temporal stability and lack of periodicity in the signal argue against a pulsar or magnetar origin. Flaring M dwarfs and other stellar sources were also ruled out by the absence of contemporaneous optical or X‑ray flares.</li> </ul> <p>Given these checks, the authors conclude that the most plausible origin is auroral radio emission from HD 189733 b.</p> <h2>Broader Context of Exoplanet Radio Searches</h2> <p>Since the 1990s, astronomers have searched for exoplanetary radio emission using low‑frequency telescopes such as the UTR‑2, the VLA, and the GMRT. These efforts were limited by sensitivity, narrow bandwidth, and the difficulty of distinguishing planetary signals from the bright Galactic background and ionospheric scintillation (Farrell et al. 1999; Zarka 2007). Theoretical work by Zarka (1998) and Treumann (2006) predicted that close‑in gas giants could produce cyclotron‑maser emission up to a few hundred megahertz, with flux densities of a few millijanskys at distances of tens of parsecs. The LOFAR detection builds on a decade of technological advances: larger collecting area, improved RFI mitigation, and sophisticated data‑processing pipelines that can handle the enormous data volumes generated by wide‑band, high‑time‑resolution observations.</p> <h2>Implications for Exoplanet Science</h2> <h3>Magnetospheres and Atmospheric Escape</h3> <p>A planet’s magnetic field shields its atmosphere from stellar wind erosion. By measuring the radio emission, scientists can directly estimate the magnetic field strength and topology, providing a new diagnostic for atmospheric retention and habitability. For HD 189733 b, the inferred field strength of ~10 G (Reiners & Christensen 2010) suggests a magnetosphere capable of deflecting the intense stellar wind from its host star, which may help preserve its atmosphere over billions of years.</p> <h3>Planetary Formation and Evolution</h3> <p>The detection offers a new probe of the internal dynamics of hot Jupiters. The magnetic field is generated by a dynamo in the planet’s metallic hydrogen layer; its strength depends on rotation rate, interior composition, and heat flux. The measured field provides a benchmark for models of planetary interiors and dynamo theory.</p> <h3>SETI and Technosignatures</h3> <p>While the signal is consistent with natural auroral processes, the discovery also underscores the need to consider exoplanetary radio emission in the search for technosignatures. Future surveys will need to discriminate between natural planetary signals and potential artificial transmissions, a challenge that will shape the design of next‑generation radio telescopes.</p> <h3>Future Observations and Theoretical Work</h3> <p>Follow‑up observations with LOFAR, the upgraded Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (uGMRT), and the forthcoming Square Kilometre Array (SKA) will be essential to confirm the detection and to search for similar emission from other hot Jupiters.
