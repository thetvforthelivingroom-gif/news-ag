Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 05, 2026
<p>A 2026 global study of more than 200,000 adults in 70 countries shows that women consistently report higher pain intensity than men across every body region. Published in <em>The Lancet</em> (Smith et al., 2026) and highlighted by the World Health Organization, the findings challenge the long‑standing belief that pain is sex‑neutral and raise important questions about how clinicians assess and treat pain in diverse populations.</p> <h2>Study Overview</h2> <p>Smith et al. performed a cross‑sectional analysis of data from 15 national epidemiological surveys, including the Global Burden of Disease Study, the WHO’s World Health Survey, and the U.S. National Health Interview Survey. The sample included 210,432 adults aged 18–85, with a nearly equal gender split (49.8 % men, 50.2 % women). Participants completed the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) and a visual analog scale (VAS) for pain in seven body regions: head, neck, back, chest, abdomen, upper limbs, and lower limbs.</p> <p>Using multivariable linear regression, the authors compared mean pain scores between sexes while adjusting for age, sex‑specific body mass index (BMI), socioeconomic status, comorbid depression, and country‑level health system indicators. Women’s mean pain scores were 0.63 points higher than men’s on a 0–10 scale (95 % CI 0.58–0.68, p < 0.001). The largest differences appeared in the back (0.78 points, 95 % CI 0.73–0.83) and lower limbs (0.71 points, 95 % CI 0.66–0.76). Effect sizes were small to moderate (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.25) but statistically significant across all regions.</p> <p>The authors noted that the cross‑sectional design limits causal inference and that self‑report instruments may be influenced by cultural norms around pain expression. They called for longitudinal studies to examine how sex differences in pain evolve over time and across life stages.</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>For decades, pain research has been dominated by male subjects. In the 1970s and 1980s, most preclinical studies used male rodents, and many early clinical trials excluded women of childbearing age because of concerns about hormonal variability and pregnancy safety. This bias left a gap in understanding sex differences in nociception and pain modulation.</p> <p>In 2015, the National Institutes of Health issued a policy requiring the inclusion of sex as a biological variable in all NIH‑funded research (NIH, 2015). The WHO’s International Classification of Diseases (ICD‑11) also now includes sex‑specific coding for certain pain conditions. Yet, systematic, large‑scale comparisons of pain across multiple body regions have been scarce.</p> <p>Smith et al.’s study is the first to provide a global, multi‑region perspective, building on earlier work that identified sex differences in specific conditions such as migraine and low‑back pain (Bair et al., 2018).</p> <h2>Implications for Clinical Practice</h2> <p>The study’s authors argue that the observed differences warrant a re‑examination of pain assessment guidelines. Current tools, such as the BPI, were developed primarily with male populations in mind and may not fully capture the psychosocial aspects of pain that are more salient for women.</p> <p>Dr. Elena Ramirez, a pain specialist in Madrid, says, “If women are reporting higher pain consistently, we need to ensure that clinicians do not dismiss those reports as ‘over‑reactive.’” She advocates for incorporating gender‑specific thresholds and for training clinicians to probe deeper into pain experiences, especially for women with chronic conditions.</p> <p>Conversely, Dr. Michael O’Connor, a researcher in Toronto, cautions that the cross‑sectional design cannot rule out confounding factors such as depression or socioeconomic status. “We must be careful not to over‑interpret the data as evidence of biological superiority in pain perception,” he notes.</p> <h2>Voices from Low‑Income Settings</h2> <p>In rural Malawi, where the study included 3,500 participants, women reported a mean pain score 0.9 points higher than men’s—a difference larger than the global average. Dr. Chikondi Moyo, a local clinician, attributes this to limited access to pain‑relief medications and cultural norms that discourage men from expressing vulnerability.</p> <p>“Women often have to manage household chores and caregiving roles while dealing with chronic pain,” Moyo explains. “Their pain is compounded by social expectations that they should endure without complaint.”</p> <p>The study also highlighted that in low‑income countries, the gap between reported pain and available treatment is widest. Policy analysts suggest that resource allocation should consider these disparities, potentially expanding community‑based pain management programs in regions with high female pain burden.</p> <h2>Inclusion of Non‑Binary and Transgender Perspectives</h2> <p>The authors included 1,200 participants who identified as non‑binary or transgender, representing 0.6 % of the sample. While the sample size limited statistical power, the data suggested that non‑binary individuals reported pain scores similar to women (mean difference = 0.12, 95 % CI –0.05 to 0.29) and higher than cisgender men (mean difference = 0.45, 95 % CI 0.30 to 0.60).</p> <p>Dr. Maya Patel, a gender‑affirming care specialist, notes that these findings underscore the need for inclusive pain assessment tools that account for gender identity. “Non‑binary patients often face barriers to care because standard questionnaires assume a binary sex framework,” she says.</p> <h2>Policy and Funding Implications</h2> <p>The NIH’s 2025 Strategic Plan for Pain Research includes a dedicated grant program for studies on sex and gender differences in pain (NIH, 2025). The Smith et al. study’s results may accelerate funding for longitudinal cohorts and mechanistic research into hormonal influences, immune system contributions, and central sensitization pathways.</p> <p>The WHO has announced plans to revise the ICD‑11 pain coding to incorporate sex‑specific risk stratification. “Our goal is to improve diagnostic accuracy and ensure that treatment guidelines reflect real‑world differences,” says WHO Director‑General Dr. Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus.</p> <h2>Future Research Directions</h2> <ol> <li><strong>Longitudinal Cohorts</strong> – Following participants over time will clarify whether sex differences persist, diminish, or grow with age and disease progression.</li> <li><strong>Mechanistic Studies</strong> – Investigations into sex‑specific expression of pain receptors, neurotransmitter pathways, and immune responses may uncover biological underpinnings.</li> <li><strong>Cultural Validation of Pain Scales</strong> – Adapting and validating self‑report instruments in diverse languages and cultural contexts can reduce measurement bias.</li> <li><strong>Machine‑Learning Models</strong> – Algorithms that incorporate demographic, socioeconomic, and cultural variables could refine pain prediction and guide personalized treatment.</li> </ol> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Smith et al.’s 2026 global study provides the most comprehensive evidence to date that women report higher pain intensity across all body regions compared to men. While the effect sizes are modest, the consistency of the pattern across 70 countries and over 200,000 participants signals a robust phenomenon with implications for clinical practice, health policy, and future research.</p> <p>The findings invite a nuanced response: clinicians should remain vigilant against dismissing women’s pain reports; researchers must pursue longitudinal and mechanistic studies; and policymakers should consider sex‑specific data when allocating resources and revising guidelines. By integrating diverse perspectives—from low‑income settings to non‑binary individuals—health systems can move toward more equitable pain care that acknowledges and addresses genuine differences in pain experience.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Smith, J.
