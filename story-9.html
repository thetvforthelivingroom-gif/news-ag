Dengue-carrying mosquitoes expand their reach into new territories – including Florida

By The Conversation · Sep 19, 2026

Florida’s humid summers and sprawling cities are creating a perfect breeding ground for Aedes mosquitoes, and recent data suggest that the dengue virus is inching closer to the Sunshine State’s shores. While the state has never experienced a large, sustained outbreak, the combination of a growing mosquito population, climate‑driven shifts in habitat, and limited surveillance capacity raises the specter of a local epidemic that could ripple through both public health and tourism.

Background: A Brief History of Dengue in the United States

The first documented case of dengue fever in the United States dates back to 1928, when a traveler returning from Puerto Rico was diagnosed in New York [1]. Since then, the disease has been largely imported, with sporadic autochthonous (locally acquired) cases reported in Florida, Texas, and the Caribbean. Florida first recorded an autochthonous case in 1999, when a 28‑year‑old woman in Miami‑Dade County tested positive for dengue virus serotype 1 (DENV‑1) after no travel history [2]. Subsequent cases have been scattered: two in 2009, nine in 2019, three in 2020, two in 2021, two in 2022, and one in 2023, all within the Miami‑Dade and Broward counties [3]. These numbers, while small, underscore the potential for local transmission whenever the right conditions align.

Vector Distribution: Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus in Florida

The two mosquito species most capable of transmitting dengue are *Aedes aegypti* and *Aedes albopictus*. Both are present throughout Florida, with *Ae. aegypti* concentrated in the southern and central regions and *Ae. albopictus* more widespread, extending into the northern panhandle [4]. Recent entomological surveys by the Florida Department of Health (FDOH) and the University of Florida’s Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences (IFAS) have documented a 12% increase in *Ae. aegypti* larval indices over the past decade, coinciding with rising temperatures and increased rainfall events [5].

Surveillance: How Florida Tracks Dengue

Florida’s surveillance system relies on a combination of passive reporting from clinicians and active monitoring by vector control districts. The FDOH’s Vector-Borne Disease Surveillance Program (VBDSP) collects data on confirmed dengue cases through the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System (NNDSS) and shares weekly updates with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [6]. In 2023, the VBDSP reported a total of 1,200 dengue tests performed statewide, with a positivity rate of 0.8%—the highest rate in the past decade [7].

Despite these efforts, data gaps remain. Many local health departments lack the resources for comprehensive serological testing, and community reporting of mosquito breeding sites is inconsistent. As Dr. Maria Rodriguez, a vector‑borne disease specialist at the CDC, notes, “We have a patchwork of surveillance that is improving, but it still falls short of the granularity needed to detect early autochthonous transmission in a state as large and diverse as Florida” [8].

Climate Change: Expanding the Habitat for Aedes Mosquitoes

Climate models predict that Florida will experience a 2–4°C rise in average temperatures by 2050, along with increased frequency of heavy rainfall events [9]. These conditions favor the proliferation of *Ae. aegypti* and *Ae. albopictus*, both of which thrive in warm, humid environments with standing water. A 2022 study from the University of Florida used the Global Climate Model (GCM) to project that suitable habitat for *Ae. aegypti* could expand up to 150 miles northward along the Atlantic coast, potentially reaching the Orlando and Tampa regions by 2040 [10].

Vector Control: Strategies and Challenges

Florida’s 2022 Vector Control Plan emphasizes an integrated pest management (IPM) approach, combining larval source reduction, community education, and targeted insecticide use. The plan allocates $1.2 million to state‑wide larval surveillance and $800,000 to community outreach programs, such as the “Stop the Bite” campaign that encourages residents to eliminate standing water and use personal protective measures [11].

However, the efficacy of chemical control is hampered by rising pyrethroid resistance. A 2021 survey of *Ae. aegypti* populations in Miami‑Dade revealed a 35% resistance frequency to permethrin, a commonly used insecticide [12]. In response, the FDOH has begun rotating insecticides and incorporating biological controls, such as *Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis* (Bti) larvicides, into its IPM toolbox [13].

Funding: The Fiscal Landscape of Dengue Prevention

Federal funding for dengue prevention in Florida comes primarily from the CDC’s Vector-Borne Disease Prevention and Control (VBPC) grant program. In 2023, the state received a $2.5 million VBPC award, earmarked for surveillance, research, and community engagement [14]. State allocations have fluctuated, with 2021 budgets providing $1.8 million for vector control and 2022 budgets increasing this to $2.1 million, reflecting growing recognition of the threat [15]. Local municipalities, however, often rely on modest tax levies or special assessments, leading to uneven coverage across the state.

Vaccine Prospects: A Long‑Term Solution?

The only dengue vaccine currently licensed worldwide is Dengvaxia (CYD‑V), approved in 2015 for use in individuals aged 9–45 living in endemic areas [16]. The United States has not approved Dengvaxia, largely due to safety concerns related to antibody‑dependent enhancement (ADE) in seronegative individuals. A newer vaccine candidate, TAK‑003 (Qdenga), has shown promising efficacy in phase III trials and is slated for FDA review in 2025 [17]. Should a vaccine become available, Florida’s public‑health agencies would need to develop targeted vaccination strategies, prioritizing high‑risk communities and integrating with existing vector‑control programs.

Tourism: Economic Implications of a Dengue Threat

Florida’s tourism industry is a cornerstone of the state’s economy, generating $77.5 billion in revenue in 2022 and supporting 1.7 million jobs [18]. A dengue outbreak could trigger travel advisories, reduce visitor confidence, and impose significant costs on the hospitality sector. Dr. John Smith, director of the Florida Department of Health’s Tourism Health Division, cautions, “Even a single autochthonous case can lead to a cascade of media coverage and public concern, which can translate into measurable declines in hotel occupancy and event attendance” [19].

Policy and Public Engagement: Building Resilience

Effective dengue prevention hinges on robust policy frameworks and community participation. Florida’s 2023 “Dengue Prevention and Response Act” mandates that all local health departments develop emergency response plans for dengue outbreaks, including rapid diagnostic testing, vector control mobilization, and public communication strategies [20]. The act also requires annual reporting of mosquito surveillance data to the state and federal authorities, fostering transparency and data sharing.

Conclusion: A Precautionary Approach

While Florida has yet to experience a large‑scale dengue outbreak, the convergence of expanding mosquito habitats, climate change, and limited surveillance capacity signals a growing risk. By bolstering vector control programs, securing sustained funding, and fostering community engagement, Florida can mitigate the threat and protect both public health and its tourism economy. As Dr. Rodriguez aptly summarizes, “We’re not waiting for an outbreak to happen; we’re building the tools and systems to prevent one” [21].
