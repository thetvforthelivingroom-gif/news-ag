Women report higher pain intensity than men across the body: A global study

A worldwide survey of more than 1.2 million adults revealed that women consistently report greater pain intensity than men in every body region examined. Published in *Cell Reports Medicine* in 2023, the study is the most comprehensive epidemiological investigation of gender differences in pain to date and offers new insights into the biological and social mechanisms that may underlie these disparities.

Study design and key findings

Powell, Hernandez, and Lee (2023) [1] collected self‑reported pain intensity scores from 1,245,378 participants (51 % women) who completed an online questionnaire covering 12 body sites: head, neck, shoulders, back, abdomen, chest, hips, knees, ankles, hands, feet, and an overall pain rating. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 80 years and represented diverse ethnicities, socioeconomic backgrounds, and health conditions. Recruitment came from online panels, community outreach, and health‑care registries.

Across all 12 sites, women reported significantly higher pain intensity than men. The largest differences appeared in the lower back (mean difference 1.2 on a 0–10 scale, Cohen’s d = 0.35) and the hips (mean difference 1.0, d = 0.32). Even after adjusting for age, body mass index, comorbidities (e.g., arthritis, diabetes), and medication use, the gender differences remained statistically significant (p < 0.001). The authors noted that the effect sizes, while moderate, were consistent across all regions, suggesting a robust pattern of higher pain perception in women.

Biological mechanisms from animal models

To explore potential biological pathways, the research team performed parallel experiments in a mouse model of chronic constriction injury (CCI), a standard model of neuropathic pain. They examined the chemokine receptor CX3CR1, which is highly expressed on microglia—the resident immune cells of the central nervous system. Inhibition of CX3CR1 signaling with a selective antagonist reduced pain hypersensitivity in female mice but had no effect in male mice, indicating a sex‑specific pathway that amplifies pain signals in females [2].

The authors argue that these findings align with emerging evidence that estrogen and other sex hormones modulate microglial activation and cytokine release, thereby influencing nociceptive processing. “The convergence of epidemiological data and preclinical mechanisms strengthens the case for a biological basis to the gender disparity in pain,” Powell said in a statement to the *New England Journal of Medicine*.

Historical context and prior evidence

Pain research has long been criticized for under‑representing women in clinical trials and for treating pain as a gender‑neutral experience. Early studies in the 1970s and 1980s documented that men were more likely to receive potent opioid analgesics for comparable conditions, a pattern that has persisted in many health systems [3]. A 2024 Guardian article highlighted that women are often undertreated for pain, citing a retrospective analysis of opioid prescriptions that found men were prescribed higher doses on average [4].

The current study builds on a growing body of literature that documents sex differences in pain sensitivity. A meta‑analysis of laboratory pain studies found that women exhibit lower pain thresholds and higher pain ratings in response to thermal and mechanical stimuli [5]. However, those studies were limited in sample size and geographic scope. Powell et al.’s work represents the first global survey to systematically compare pain intensity across a wide range of body sites.

Cultural and social influences

The authors acknowledge that sociocultural norms can shape pain expression. In many societies, women are encouraged to be more expressive about discomfort, whereas men are socialized to “tough it out” [6]. These expectations can influence both self‑reporting and clinical assessment, potentially amplifying observed differences. The study’s authors emphasize that biology and culture interact in complex ways and that future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and objective measures of pain.

Clinical implications

The findings have immediate relevance for clinicians. Pain assessment tools that rely solely on self‑report may systematically underestimate pain in women if clinicians assume that objective signs are similar across genders. The American College of Physicians has already begun revising its chronic pain guidelines to incorporate sex as a factor in treatment decisions [7]. The Powell study provides empirical support for these revisions and suggests that clinicians should consider gender‑specific pain thresholds when evaluating patients.

Pharmaceutical and regulatory impact

Pharmaceutical companies are taking note of the data. The study’s authors point out that drug efficacy and dosing may differ by sex, a consideration that has historically been under‑examined in analgesic trials. The FDA’s 2018 guidance on the inclusion of women in clinical trials for analgesics has been reinforced by the new evidence, and several companies are now designing trials that stratify participants by sex and evaluate pharmacodynamic differences [8].

Advocacy and policy responses

Women’s health advocacy groups have called for increased research funding into chronic pain conditions that disproportionately affect women, such as fibromyalgia, temporomandibular disorders, and chronic pelvic pain. Meanwhile, men’s health advocates caution against over‑pathologizing male pain, emphasizing that individual variability remains paramount. The study’s authors respond by clarifying that their results do not imply that men never experience severe pain, but rather that gender differences in reporting and biology warrant tailored approaches.

Policy makers are already debating how to integrate these findings into national pain management strategies. Several countries are piloting gender‑sensitive training for clinicians, while others are exploring reimbursement models that incentivize equitable pain care. The long‑term success of these initiatives will hinge on robust data collection and continuous evaluation of outcomes across genders.

Future directions

The Powell study sets the stage for several critical developments:

1. **Longitudinal research** will be needed to disentangle causal pathways and to determine whether interventions that target the identified cellular mechanisms can reduce pain disparities. 2. **Health‑system protocols** may need to incorporate validated, gender‑neutral scales that account for cultural differences in expression. 3. **Opioid stewardship programs** could benefit from gender‑specific prescribing guidelines, reducing the risk of over‑prescription in men while ensuring adequate analgesia for women.

Ultimately, the study underscores that pain is not a one‑size‑fits‑all phenomenon. By revealing systematic differences in how men and women experience and report pain, it challenges clinicians, researchers, and policymakers to rethink entrenched assumptions and to develop more nuanced, equitable approaches to suffering. The work of Powell et al. marks a pivotal moment in pain research, opening the door to a future where gender‑sensitive care is the norm rather than the exception.
