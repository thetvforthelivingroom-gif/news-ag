Women Report Higher Pain Across All Body Regions in a Global Survey, Study Finds

A multinational study of 32,457 adults in 60 countries has found that women, on average, rate pain intensity about 20 % higher than men in every body region examined. The data, published last month in *Pain* (Torres et al., 2023, DOI 10.1097/j.pain.0000000000002745), suggest that sex differences in pain are not limited to a handful of cultures or a few body parts but may be a universal pattern that clinicians and policymakers must now consider.

---

The Study in a Nutshell

The “Global Pain Survey 2023” was a cross‑sectional, self‑report study conducted by researchers from the University of Barcelona, the University of Toronto, and the University of Nairobi, in partnership with the World Health Organization (WHO) and the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP). The questionnaire, translated into 12 languages, asked participants to rate pain intensity in nine body regions—head, neck, upper back, lower back, chest, abdomen, upper limbs, lower limbs, and visceral organs—on a 0–10 numeric rating scale. Demographic variables (age, sex, gender identity, ethnicity, socioeconomic status), health status (body mass index, chronic disease, medication use), and psychosocial factors (pain catastrophizing, depression, anxiety) were also collected.

Key quantitative results (mean ± SD):

| Body region | Women | Men | Difference | p‑value | |-------------|-------|-----|------------|---------| | Head | 3.8 ± 2.1 | 3.3 ± 2.0 | +0.5 | <0.001 | | Neck | 4.1 ± 2.3 | 3.6 ± 2.2 | +0.5 | <0.001 | | Upper back | 4.4 ± 2.4 | 3.9 ± 2.3 | +0.5 | <0.001 | | Lower back | 5.1 ± 2.5 | 4.3 ± 2.4 | +0.8 | <0.001 | | Chest | 3.9 ± 2.2 | 3.4 ± 2.1 | +0.5 | <0.001 | | Abdomen | 4.6 ± 2.4 | 4.0 ± 2.3 | +0.6 | <0.001 | | Upper limbs | 3.7 ± 2.0 | 3.3 ± 1.9 | +0.4 | <0.001 | | Lower limbs | 4.0 ± 2.2 | 3.5 ± 2.1 | +0.5 | <0.001 | | Visceral | 4.2 ± 2.3 | 3.7 ± 2.2 | +0.5 | <0.001 |

After adjusting for body mass index, chronic disease burden, medication use, and psychosocial variables, the differences remained statistically significant (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.4–0.6 across regions). The authors note that, while the effect sizes are moderate, they are clinically meaningful given the widespread prevalence of chronic pain.

---

Why This Matters

Dr. Maria L. Torres, lead author and professor of pain medicine at the University of Barcelona, explains: “For decades, clinical trials and basic science studies have largely used male animals and male volunteers, under the assumption that pain mechanisms are universal. This study shows that, at least in terms of reported intensity, women consistently experience more pain across the body. That has implications for diagnosis, treatment, and even health‑care policy.” (Torres et al., 2023)

The study aligns with a growing body of evidence that sex differences in pain are not merely a matter of reporting bias. Biological mechanisms—such as estrogen modulation of nociceptive pathways, differences in pain‑related gene expression, and immune‑system variability—may amplify pain signals in women (Kosek et al., 2017). Psychosocial factors—like gender norms that encourage women to express distress, or social support networks that differ by sex—also play a role (Giorgio & McCracken, 2020).

---

A Historical Lens

The male bias in pain research is well documented. In the 1970s, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) began requiring the inclusion of women in clinical trials, but many studies still under‑represented them. Landmark studies in the 1990s, such as Apkarian et al.’s work on sex differences in pain perception, highlighted that women often report higher pain thresholds and more intense pain in experimental settings (Apkarian et al., 1997). Those studies were limited to small, homogeneous samples.

The Global Pain Survey 2023 is the first large‑scale, multinational attempt to quantify sex differences in pain intensity across the entire body. By including participants from diverse ethnic, cultural, and socioeconomic backgrounds, the study challenges the notion that pain differences are culturally specific. Instead, it points to a more universal pattern that transcends geography.

---

Methodological Caveats

The authors are careful to acknowledge limitations. The cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference; we cannot say that being female causes higher pain, only that the association exists. Self‑report data are subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Cultural differences in how people describe pain may also influence the results. Finally, the study did not assess hormonal status or menstrual cycle phase, which could affect pain perception.

---

Implications for Practice

If women experience more pain across all body regions, clinicians should consider sex‑specific screening tools and treatment plans. Pain management guidelines that rely on male‑centric data may underestimate women’s needs. Pharmacologic therapies that target estrogen pathways or immune modulation could offer new avenues for relief. Moreover, health‑care systems should monitor pain outcomes by sex to identify gaps in care.

---

Looking Ahead

Future research should explore the underlying biological mechanisms in greater depth, including hormone levels, genetic markers, and immune profiles. Longitudinal studies could determine whether the observed differences persist over time and how they interact with aging. Interventions that address psychosocial factors—such as coping strategies and social support—may also reduce the pain gap.

In the meantime, the Global Pain Survey 2023 provides a compelling call to action: pain is not a gender‑neutral experience, and acknowledging this reality is essential for equitable care.
