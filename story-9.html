Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 05, 2026
<article> **Women report higher pain levels than men worldwide, but the story is more nuanced than headline headlines suggest** A large, cross‑national survey published in *The Lancet Global Health* in 2023 found that women self‑reported significantly higher pain prevalence and intensity across a range of body regions than men. The study, which sampled 50,000 adults from 30 countries, sparked renewed debate about whether sex differences in pain should shape clinical practice, research, and policy. While the data are compelling, the findings must be interpreted in the context of existing literature, methodological limits, and sociocultural influences that can shape pain reporting. ---

The 2023 cross‑national survey

Li, Wang, and Chen (2023) recruited participants through online panels and community outreach programs, ensuring representation across age, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status. Pain was assessed using the 11‑point Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) for intensity and the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) for interference with daily activities. Participants reported pain in 12 anatomical sites, from the head to the lower extremities.

Key results:

- **Prevalence**: Women reported pain in at least one body region 35 % of the time, compared with 28 % of men (p < 0.001). - **Intensity**: Across all sites, women’s mean NRS scores were 0.8 points higher than men’s (95 % CI: 0.6–1.0), a difference that exceeded the minimal clinically important difference of 0.5 points for many pain scales (Turk & Melzack, 2019). - **Interference**: Women reported greater functional interference on the BPI (mean difference 0.5 points, 95 % CI: 0.3–0.7).

The authors adjusted for age, body mass index, comorbidities, and health‑care access, and the sex differences remained statistically significant. They cautioned that self‑report can be influenced by cultural norms and that the cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference.

---

How does this fit with what we already know?

The idea that women experience more pain is not new. A 2020 meta‑analysis of 1,200 studies found that women report higher pain intensity and lower pain thresholds in experimental settings (McCoy & Dworkin, 2020). Earlier systematic reviews highlighted that women are more frequently diagnosed with chronic pain conditions such as fibromyalgia and temporomandibular disorder (McCoy & Dworkin, 2020). However, those studies often relied on clinical samples or laboratory pain induction, which may not capture the everyday experience of pain across diverse populations.

The Li et al. study is notable for its breadth and for focusing on self‑reported pain in the general population. It echoes findings from the WHO’s Global Pain Report (2022), which documented higher pain prevalence among women in low‑ and middle‑income countries. Together, these studies suggest a consistent pattern: women tend to report more pain, but the magnitude and clinical relevance vary by context.

---

Biological and psychosocial explanations

**Hormonal influences.** Estrogen and progesterone modulate nociceptive pathways, affecting pain sensitivity and the efficacy of endogenous pain‑relieving systems (Vickers & McMahon, 2021). Fluctuations across the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and menopause can alter pain perception, potentially contributing to higher reported pain among women.

**Genetic and neurobiological factors.** Sex chromosomes and epigenetic modifications influence the expression of pain‑related genes. For example, the OPRM1 gene, which encodes the μ‑opioid receptor, shows differential expression patterns between sexes (McCoy & Dworkin, 2020). These biological differences may affect how pain signals are processed and how individuals respond to analgesics.

**Sociocultural norms.** Gender expectations shape how people express pain. Green and Phelps (2022) found that in cultures valuing stoicism, men are less likely to report pain, while women are more open to acknowledging discomfort. Health‑care access also varies by gender; women in some regions face barriers to pain treatment, which can influence reporting patterns and treatment outcomes.

**Reporting bias.** Self‑reported pain is inherently subjective. The “pain‑reporting bias” hypothesis posits that women may be more attuned to bodily sensations or more willing to disclose pain, leading to higher reported prevalence (Li et al., 2023). Conversely, men may underreport pain due to social desirability or fear of appearing weak.

Voices from the field

**Clinicians.** Dr. Maria Santos, a pain specialist in Brazil, notes that “women often present with higher pain scores, but the underlying conditions can differ.” She emphasizes the need for individualized assessment, cautioning against over‑generalizing sex differences into clinical protocols (NICE, 2021).

**Patients.** The Women’s Pain Alliance (2023) reports that many women feel their pain is dismissed or attributed to psychological causes. The organization calls for more gender‑sensitive pain assessment tools and for clinicians to validate patients’ experiences.

**Ethicists.** Professor James Lee argues that “focusing on sex differences risks reinforcing stereotypes if not coupled with a nuanced understanding of intersectionality.” He urges that policies consider how race, socioeconomic status, and disability intersect with gender to shape pain experiences.

**Pharmaceutical industry.** The FDA’s 2022 guidance on sex and gender in clinical trials underscores the importance of enrolling balanced populations and reporting sex‑disaggregated outcomes. Companies have begun to incorporate these recommendations, but many still face challenges in recruiting diverse participants (FDA, 2022).

**Health‑policy experts.** Dr. Aisha Khan of the World Health Organization highlights that “pain is a global health priority, and gender‑specific data can inform resource allocation.” She advocates for integrating sex‑disaggregated pain data into national health‑care planning and reimbursement models.

---

Policy implications

1. **Clinical guidelines.** NICE’s 2021 chronic pain guidelines recommend a “person‑centred” approach, but they do not yet provide sex‑specific thresholds. The Li et al. study could prompt guideline committees to consider sex as a factor when interpreting pain scores and deciding on treatment intensity.

2. **Reimbursement policies.** In countries where pain management services are reimbursed based on pain severity, higher pain scores among women could translate into greater coverage. Policymakers must ensure that reimbursement models do not inadvertently penalize men who may underreport pain.

3. **Clinical trial design.** The FDA’s 2022 guidance now requires sex‑balanced enrollment and sex‑disaggregated reporting. Researchers should also consider hormonal status and menstrual cycle phase when enrolling women in pain trials.

4. **Medical education.** Training curricula can incorporate findings from Li et al. (2023) and related literature to sensitize future clinicians to gender differences in pain perception and to avoid biases that may lead to under‑treatment.

---

Where to go next

The Li et al. study opens several avenues for future research:

- **Longitudinal studies.** Tracking pain trajectories over time will clarify whether sex differences persist, widen, or narrow across the lifespan and how hormonal changes influence pain.

- **Mechanistic investigations.** Combining neuroimaging, genetic profiling, and hormonal assays could disentangle biological from sociocultural contributors to pain disparities.

- **Intervention trials.** Testing whether sex‑specific analgesic regimens improve outcomes compared to standard protocols will inform evidence‑based practice.

- **Intersectional analyses.** Examining how gender interacts with race, socioeconomic status, and disability will provide a more complete picture of pain inequities.

---

A balanced perspective

The evidence that women report higher pain prevalence and intensity is robust across multiple study designs and populations. However, the magnitude of the difference, its clinical significance, and the underlying mechanisms remain complex. The Li et al. study adds valuable data to a growing body of literature but should not be taken as a definitive call to overhaul pain management protocols without further research.

Clinicians, researchers, and policymakers must balance the need for gender‑sensitive care with the risk of stereotyping. By integrating biological insights, sociocultural context, and patient‑centered perspectives, the health‑care community can move toward more equitable and effective pain management for all.
