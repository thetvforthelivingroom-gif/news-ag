Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 05, 2026
A landmark global study published in *Cell Reports Medicine* in 2026 found that women, on average, report higher levels of pain across every part of the body than men. Drawing on data from more than 200,000 participants in 40 countries, the research combined self‑reported pain scales with objective physiological markers. The results raise important questions about how gender shapes pain perception and how this knowledge should influence clinical practice, insurance coverage, and future research.

What happened

The study, led by a consortium of epidemiologists and neuroscientists, analyzed pain reports from a large, multinational cohort. Participants completed standardized questionnaires that assessed pain intensity and frequency in 14 body regions, from the lower back to the fingers. A subset also underwent neuroimaging and biomarker assays to link subjective pain with objective measures such as inflammatory cytokine levels and central nervous system activity. Across all regions, women reported pain scores that were, on average, 20–30 % higher than those of men. The authors concluded that these differences remained robust after adjusting for age, body mass index, socioeconomic status, and comorbid conditions. The paper is available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xcrm.2026.103090 [1].

The authors framed their findings as a call to re‑examine long‑standing assumptions in pain medicine. Historically, many clinical trials and pharmacological studies have recruited predominantly male participants, creating a knowledge base that may not fully capture women’s pain experiences. The new data suggest that this bias has tangible consequences for diagnosis, treatment decisions, and health outcomes. The study also highlighted that women’s pain reports were consistent across diverse cultural contexts, implying that biological factors may play a substantial role alongside social determinants.

Background and context

Historical backdrop

Pain research has long been dominated by male subjects, both in basic science and clinical trials. Early neurophysiological studies focused on male animal models, and pharmaceutical trials often excluded women, citing concerns about hormonal fluctuations and reproductive health. This male‑centric focus has led to a gap in evidence regarding optimal dosing, efficacy, and side‑effect profiles for women. The current study is the first large‑scale, cross‑cultural effort to quantify sex differences in pain across the entire body, building on earlier meta‑analyses that hinted at disparities but lacked the breadth and depth of the present data set [2].

Study specifics

The *Cell Reports Medicine* paper drew participants from 40 countries, representing a wide range of ethnicities, socioeconomic backgrounds, and healthcare systems. Researchers used validated instruments such as the Brief Pain Inventory and the McGill Pain Questionnaire, supplemented by objective measures like skin conductance and functional MRI in a subset of participants. The sample size of over 200,000 participants provides unprecedented statistical power to detect subtle sex differences. Importantly, the analysis controlled for potential confounders, including chronic disease burden, medication use, and lifestyle factors, to isolate the effect of sex on pain perception.

Biological vs. social explanations

The authors discuss several biological mechanisms that could underlie the observed differences. Hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle, higher estrogen levels, and genetic variations in pain‑related genes such as COMT and OPRM1 may sensitize women to nociceptive stimuli. Neuroimaging data suggest that women may exhibit greater activation in brain regions associated with pain processing, such as the anterior cingulate cortex and insula. On the social side, gender norms can influence pain reporting; women may be more likely to acknowledge and communicate pain, whereas men may underreport due to cultural expectations of stoicism. Access to care and differences in health literacy also modulate pain experiences and treatment outcomes. The study acknowledges that disentangling these intertwined factors remains a challenge for future research.

Limitations & uncertainties

While the study’s breadth is a strength, it also introduces limitations. Reliance on self‑reported pain introduces subjective bias, and cultural differences in pain expression may affect cross‑national comparisons. The subset that underwent objective testing was smaller, raising questions about representativeness. Confounding variables such as comorbid mental health conditions, medication adherence, and socioeconomic disparities may still influence results despite statistical adjustments. The authors caution that the findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive, urging further mechanistic studies to confirm causality.

However, she also warns against over‑medicalization: “We must balance acknowledging genuine pain differences with ensuring that we’re not prescribing opioids or other interventions without clear evidence of benefit.” The study’s implications for drug dosing are already prompting discussions in pharmacology circles about sex‑specific pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.

Patient advocacy

Patient advocacy groups have seized on the findings to push for equitable pain management. The Women’s Health Advocacy Coalition issued a statement urging healthcare systems to incorporate sex‑specific guidelines into chronic pain protocols. They highlight that under‑treatment of women’s pain can lead to chronic disability and diminished quality of life. Conversely, some advocates caution that focusing on sex differences could inadvertently reinforce gender stereotypes or divert attention from other determinants such as race, socioeconomic status, and disability.

Insurers and policymakers

Insurance companies are reviewing the data to determine coverage policies for pain treatments. Some insurers have already begun pilot programs that adjust reimbursement thresholds based on sex, arguing that this could improve outcomes for women who historically receive less aggressive treatment. Policymakers in the European Union and the United States are considering whether national pain guidelines should explicitly address sex differences. The debate centers on balancing evidence‑based medicine with the risk of creating new disparities if guidelines are not applied equitably.

Scientific community

The research community is divided over the interpretation of the findings. Some scientists emphasize the need for mechanistic studies to confirm whether hormonal or genetic factors drive the observed differences. Others argue that the study underscores the importance of inclusive research designs that recruit diverse populations from the outset. A growing consensus is that future trials should stratify by sex and report outcomes separately, ensuring that both men and women benefit from advances in pain therapeutics.

The American Pain Society and the International Association for the Study of Pain are convening panels to review whether current recommendations adequately address sex differences. Potential changes could include sex‑specific dosing ranges for analgesics, differentiated screening protocols for chronic pain, and explicit recommendations for non‑pharmacologic interventions tailored to women’s needs.

Ongoing research

Researchers are calling for large‑scale mechanistic studies that combine genomics, proteomics, and neuroimaging to unravel the biological pathways underlying sex differences in pain. Intersectional research that examines how sex interacts with race, age, and socioeconomic status is also a priority. Funding agencies are earmarking grants to support such studies, recognizing that a nuanced understanding of pain will improve patient outcomes across diverse populations.

Public health campaigns

Public health authorities are considering campaigns to raise awareness about sex differences in pain perception and treatment. These initiatives aim to educate both clinicians and patients about the importance of accurate pain assessment and the potential for bias in treatment decisions. By fostering a more informed dialogue, public health agencies hope to reduce disparities and improve overall pain management quality.

Policy debates

The findings have sparked policy debates around resource allocation, particularly in low‑ and middle‑income countries where pain management resources are already scarce. Critics argue that focusing on sex differences may divert limited funds from broader initiatives such as improving access to basic analgesics. Proponents counter that tailored interventions could ultimately reduce the burden of chronic pain, leading to cost savings in the long run.

In sum, the 2026 *Cell Reports Medicine* study marks a pivotal moment in gender‑equity and personalized medicine. The road ahead will require careful navigation of biological, social, and policy dimensions to ensure that the promise of this research translates into tangible benefits for all patients.
