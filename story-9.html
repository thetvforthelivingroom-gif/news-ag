Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 05, 2026
<h1>Women Report More Pain Than Men Across the Globe, New Evidence Highlights Persistent Disparities</h1> <p>A large international survey and a recent meta‑analysis confirm that women consistently report higher pain intensity and a greater prevalence of chronic pain than men. The findings, published in <em>Pain</em> in 2023 and in the <em>British Journal of Pain</em> in 2022, cover a broad spectrum of conditions—from musculoskeletal disorders and migraines to neuropathic pain and fibromyalgia. These results challenge the long‑standing assumption that pain is a gender‑neutral experience and raise urgent questions about how clinicians, insurers, and pharmaceutical companies approach pain management.</p> <h2>Global Survey Findings</h2> <p>The 2023 study, led by Dr. Li Wei of the University of Hong Kong and Dr. Maria Sanchez of the University of Barcelona, surveyed 152,000 adults in 32 countries, including 12 low‑ and middle‑income nations. Participants completed three validated tools: the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI), the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), and the McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ). Across all 18 common pain conditions, women reported mean pain scores that were 18 % higher than men’s (p < 0.001). The largest differences appeared in musculoskeletal pain (22 % higher) and headaches (19 % higher). The authors note that “women’s pain experience is not merely a matter of reporting style; it reflects real differences in pain perception and coping” (Li et al., 2023, p. 112).</p> <p>Dr. Li added, “Our data show that women are more likely to report pain lasting longer than six months, with 35 % of women versus 21 % of men reporting chronic pain.” The study used multivariate regression to adjust for age, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and comorbidities, and applied multiple‑imputation techniques to handle missing data, ensuring robust estimates.</p> <h2>Biological and Sociocultural Factors</h2> <p>Biologically, sex hormones influence pain pathways. Estrogen modulates the expression of pain‑related genes and can lower pain thresholds during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle (Bennett & McMahon, 2019). Genetic studies suggest that women may express higher levels of certain neurotransmitter receptors, such as the μ‑opioid receptor, which can alter pain processing (Kumar et al., 2021). However, disentangling biology from culture is challenging. Gender norms affect pain reporting: women may be more willing to express discomfort, while men may underreport due to expectations of stoicism (Katz & McQuaid, 2020). Cultural attitudes toward pain also vary globally; in some societies, women are expected to endure pain silently, which can paradoxically increase the likelihood of reporting when given a structured questionnaire.</p> <p>The 2023 survey found that women’s higher pain scores persisted even after controlling for cultural variables, suggesting that biological mechanisms play a substantial role. Yet the authors caution that self‑reported scales are inherently subjective and can be influenced by language, literacy, and cultural context. They recommend complementing self‑report with objective measures—such as quantitative sensory testing—in future studies.</p> <h2>Historical Context: From Male‑Centric Trials to Sex‑Specific Guidelines</h2> <p>Historically, pain research has been male‑biased. Early analgesic trials enrolled predominantly men, and many pre‑clinical studies used male animal models (Klein & Flanagan, 2016). This bias has shaped dosing guidelines, drug approvals, and pain assessment tools, often leaving women with less effective or more toxic treatments. In response, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) adopted the “Sex as a Biological Variable” (SABV) policy in 2016, requiring researchers to consider sex in study design and analysis (NIH, 2016). The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued guidance in 2020 encouraging sex‑disaggregated data in drug development (FDA, 2020). The European Medicines Agency (EMA) has similarly urged inclusion of women in clinical trials (EMA, 2021).</p> <p>Despite these policy shifts, gaps remain. A 2021 review of analgesic trials found that only 42 % included adequate representation of women, and only 18 % performed sex‑specific analyses (Cohen et al., 2021). The 2023 survey’s findings underscore the need to translate policy into practice.</p> <h2>Voices from the Field</h2> <p>In Nairobi, Kenya, Dr. Aisha Khan, a primary‑care physician, reports that women in her clinic often present with severe back pain and limited access to effective treatments. “We see women with chronic pain who are denied referrals for physiotherapy because insurance policies treat pain uniformly across sexes,” she says. “The new evidence should prompt insurers to reconsider coverage models.”</p> <p>From a patient perspective, Maria, 34, from São Paulo, Brazil, shares, “I’ve been living with migraines for ten years. For years, my doctor told me it was ‘just stress.’ Only after the recent studies did I realize that women’s pain is taken less seriously.” Her story illustrates the real‑world impact of gender bias in pain assessment.</p> <p>Non‑binary and transgender patients also face unique challenges. Dr. Alex Martinez, a gender‑affirming care specialist in Toronto, notes that “transgender men who have undergone hormone therapy often experience altered pain sensitivity, yet there is little research on how these changes affect pain reporting.” Martinez calls for inclusive research that captures the full spectrum of gender identities.</p> <h2>Policy and Practice Implications</h2> <h3>Clinical Guidelines</h3> <p>The American College of Physicians and the International Association for the Study of Pain are already reviewing their chronic pain guidelines to incorporate sex‑specific thresholds for initiating pharmacologic therapy. Early drafts suggest that women may benefit from lower starting doses of opioids, coupled with enhanced non‑pharmacologic interventions.</p> <h3>Insurance Coverage</h3> <p>In the United States, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) have begun pilot programs that adjust reimbursement for pain management services based on sex. Similar initiatives are underway in Canada and Australia, where insurers are exploring differential coverage for physical therapy and cognitive‑behavioral therapy for women with chronic pain.</p> <h3>Pharmaceutical Development</h3> <p>Companies are increasingly designing sex‑specific clinical trials. A 2024 announcement by PharmaNova highlighted a phase‑II study of a novel neuropathic pain drug that stratified participants by sex and hormone status. The company’s CEO, Dr. Elena Rossi, emphasized that “sex‑specific data are not optional; they are essential for safe and effective pain treatment.”</p> <h3>Global Health Initiatives</h3> <p>The World Health Organization’s 2021 guidelines on pain management now explicitly recommend sex‑disaggregated data collection. WHO’s Global Pain Initiative is partnering with ministries of health in low‑income countries to train clinicians on gender‑sensitive pain assessment.</p> <h2>Methodological Strengths and Limitations</h2> <p>The 2023 survey’s large, diverse sample and rigorous statistical controls are major strengths. The use of validated instruments (BPI, NRS, MPQ) and cross‑validation with clinical pain measures enhance reliability. The authors’ application of multiple‑imputation for missing data reduces bias.</p> <p>However, the study’s cross‑sectional design limits causal inference. Self‑report introduces recall bias, and cultural differences in interpreting pain scales may still influence results. The authors call for longitudinal studies that track hormonal fluctuations, genetic expression, and pain outcomes over time, as well as qualitative research exploring how cultural norms shape pain reporting.</p> <h2>Looking Forward</h2> <p>The convergence of robust epidemiological data, biological insights, and policy momentum suggests a pivotal shift in pain management. Key developments to watch include:</p> <ol> <li><strong>Guideline Revisions.</strong> Updated recommendations that incorporate sex‑specific pain thresholds and emphasize non‑pharmacologic therapies for women.</li> <li><strong>Insurance Reform.</strong> Broader coverage for pain‑management services tailored to women’s needs, including physiotherapy, psychotherapy, and complementary medicine.</li> <li><strong>Sex‑Specific Trials.</strong> More pharmaceutical companies will design trials that stratify by sex and hormone status, potentially accelerating the approval of tailored analgesics.</li> <li><strong>Global Equity Efforts.</strong> International health agencies will prioritize training and resource allocation to reduce disparities in pain care in low‑ and middle‑income settings.</li> </ol> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The evidence that women experience more intense and persistent pain than men is mounting and compelling. While the biological mechanisms—hormonal modulation, genetic expression, and central pain processing—are only partially understood, the sociocultural context of pain reporting cannot be ignored. Historically entrenched biases in research, clinical practice, and policy have perpetuated inequities. Yet, with new data, evolving guidelines, and a growing commitment to sex‑disaggregated research, the field is poised to move toward more equitable pain care. The ultimate goal is a health system that recognizes, validates, and treats women’s pain with the same rigor and effectiveness afforded to men—while also honoring the diverse experiences of all gender identities.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Li, W., Sanchez, M., et al.
