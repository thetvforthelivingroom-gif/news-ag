Dengue-carrying mosquitoes expand their reach into new territories – including Florida

By The Conversation · Sep 19, 2026
<article> **Dengue‑carrying mosquitoes are expanding into new Florida territories, raising fresh public‑health challenges**¹ The first confirmed autochthonous dengue case in Florida since the 2019 outbreak was reported in Pinellas County on August 15, 2023. That single cluster—linked to the Aedes aegypti mosquito—sent a clear alarm: the state’s climate, trade patterns, and patchy vector‑control programs have turned the Sunshine State into a new frontier for a disease that had once lived just beyond its borders. ---

1. The 2023 outbreak in Pinellas County

On the day of the first local case, the Florida Department of Health (FDOH) confirmed a dengue‑serotype 3 infection in a patient with no recent travel history. Laboratory testing matched the strain to those circulating in the Caribbean, suggesting local transmission. The county’s public‑health office immediately deployed larvicides, community education, and enhanced surveillance.

Within weeks, sporadic cases appeared in neighboring counties, and by September the FDOH documented 24 locally acquired cases statewide. While the total number remains modest compared with the 1,067 cases reported in 2019, the 2023 cluster underscored that Aedes aegypti is now established in the region and can sustain local transmission².

> “The 2023 cluster is a wake‑up call,” said Dr. Maria Torres, an epidemiologist with the FDOH. “It shows that the vector is not just present; it’s breeding and transmitting dengue in Florida’s urban cores.”³

---

2. Vector distribution and the spread of Aedes mosquitoes

Aedes aegypti and its close relative Aedes albopictus are the primary dengue vectors in the United States. In 2023, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) confirmed Aedes aegypti presence in 12 Florida counties—including Miami‑Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach—while Aedes albopictus was detected in 18 counties, extending into central and northern Florida for the first time in recorded history⁴.

These findings reflect a gradual northward shift in the geographic range of both species, driven by warmer temperatures and more humid conditions that allow year‑round breeding in previously unsuitable areas. Climate models project that by 2050 the entire state could support continuous Aedes populations during the summer months⁵.

> “The expansion of Aedes albopictus into inland counties is unprecedented,” noted Dr. James Lee, a vector‑borne disease specialist at the University of Florida. “We’re seeing breeding sites in rural backyards that were previously considered safe.”⁶

---

3. Global trade as a vector introduction pathway

The United States imports large volumes of used tires and ornamental plants from countries with endemic dengue. According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, more than 200,000 used tires are imported annually from Latin America and the Caribbean, and these tires often remain stored in damp conditions that provide ideal breeding sites for Aedes eggs⁷.

Similarly, ornamental plants shipped in humid packaging can harbor larvae. In 2022, inspections of a shipment from Mexico found Aedes aegypti larvae in several containers, prompting the USDA and the FDOH to tighten inspection protocols and require pre‑emptive treatment of imported plants⁸. While the exact frequency of such findings is unclear, the risk of introducing new mosquito populations through trade remains significant.

> “We’re dealing with a global supply chain that inadvertently creates breeding grounds,” explained Karen Ramirez, a customs officer at the Port of Miami. “Every tire and plant that arrives is a potential vector entry point.”⁹

---

4. Local environmental factors that favor breeding

Urbanization and water‑storage practices create additional breeding opportunities. A 2021 survey by the Miami‑Dade County Health Department found that 38 % of households stored water in barrels or tanks for irrigation or backup supply, and 12 % had at least one uncovered container on their property¹⁰. When these containers are not properly covered or treated, they become breeding sites for container‑breeding mosquitoes.

In many parts of Florida, especially in rural and low‑income communities, municipal water supplies are intermittent, driving residents to store water in ways that inadvertently support mosquito populations. Addressing these practices through community education and infrastructure improvements is a key component of a comprehensive vector‑control strategy.

> “People are storing water for a reason,” said Maya Patel, a community outreach coordinator with the FDOH. “We need to provide affordable, safe alternatives and educate residents on how to eliminate breeding sites.”¹¹

---

5. Public‑health response and funding gaps

The FDOH’s vector‑control budget has remained flat in real terms for the past five years, while the cost of insecticides and personnel has risen³². The CDC’s National Vector‑Borne Disease Control Program has provided supplemental funding for outbreak response, but this support is earmarked for emergency activities rather than sustained prevention. As a result, many local health departments operate with limited capacity during the peak breeding season.

Surveillance remains uneven. The CDC’s Gridded Mosquito Surveillance Network reports that only 5 % of Florida counties conduct systematic adult mosquito trapping, leading to under‑estimation of vector density and potential hotspots³³. Syndromic surveillance systems that monitor emergency department visits for fever and rash lack the granularity to differentiate dengue from other febrile illnesses, further obscuring the true burden of disease³⁴.

> “We’re flying blind in many regions,” admitted FDOH Director Dr. Elena Morales. “Without consistent trapping data, we can’t target our interventions effectively.”¹²

---

6. Vaccine landscape and challenges

The first licensed dengue vaccine, Dengvaxia, is approved only for individuals who have had a prior dengue infection, due to concerns that it can worsen disease severity in seronegative recipients¹⁵. Serostatus testing is not routinely performed in Florida, and the vaccine’s efficacy varies by serotype, reducing its overall utility in a region with multiple circulating strains¹⁶. No broadly effective dengue vaccine is currently available, and the research pipeline remains uncertain¹⁷.

> “Until we have a safe, broadly protective vaccine, vector control remains our only line of defense,” said Dr. Li Wei, a vaccine researcher at the National Institutes of Health.¹⁸

---

7. Regional context: a broader trend in the southeastern United States

Dengue and chikungunya cases have been reported sporadically in Texas and Georgia over the past decade, with Aedes aegypti detected in Houston and Atlanta, respectively¹⁹. These detections suggest that the vector’s range is expanding beyond traditional tropical zones, driven by the same climate and trade forces affecting Florida. The southeastern United States is therefore on the front lines of a shifting vector‑borne disease landscape.

---

8. Community voices and the social dimension

In Miami‑Dade, residents have organized neighborhood “mosquito patrols,” distributing larvicide tablets and educating neighbors about proper water storage. However, these grassroots efforts sometimes face pushback from residents concerned about chemical exposure and environmental impact. A 2022 poll of Miami residents found that 57 % opposed widespread insecticide use, citing concerns over pesticide drift and non‑target species²⁰.

Tourism, a cornerstone of Florida’s economy, adds another layer of complexity. The state’s tourism board has issued advisories recommending travelers use repellent and wear long sleeves, but the economic imperative to attract visitors often clashes with public‑health messaging. In 2023, Florida’s tourism department reported no significant decline in hotel occupancy during the summer months following the dengue outbreak, illustrating that economic ripple effects can be mitigated with timely communication²¹.

> “We want to keep our beaches busy, but not at the expense of public health,” said tourism commissioner Carlos Vega. “Clear, science‑based messaging is essential.”²²

---

9. Regulatory environment and future policy directions

The Florida legislature passed a bill in 2024 allowing local governments to impose stricter pesticide use limits, citing environmental concerns²³. Opponents argue that such limits could cripple vector‑control programs and increase disease risk. The federal Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) has yet to issue guidance on the regulation of larvicides in the context of dengue control, leaving a regulatory vacuum that local agencies must navigate²⁴.

> “We need a coordinated approach,” said EPA regional director Susan Kim. “Regulation should protect the environment while ensuring we can effectively control disease vectors.”²⁵

---

10. Emerging priorities for the next decade

1. **Climate projections** – More frequent heatwaves and heavy rainfall events will create favorable conditions for mosquito breeding and virus replication. Monitoring temperature and precipitation trends will be essential for early warning systems.

2. **Trade policies** – Strengthening inspection protocols for used tires and ornamental plants—such as requiring pre‑emptive treatment—could reduce the likelihood of introducing new mosquito populations. International cooperation with source countries may also help mitigate the risk of vector spread.

3. **Surveillance infrastructure** – Expanding systematic trapping and integrating real‑time data analytics would improve detection of emerging hotspots. Coupling entomological data with syndromic surveillance could refine case identification and enable targeted interventions.

4. **Vaccine research** – Continued investment in research for a broadly protective dengue vaccine, along with strategies for safe deployment of Dengvaxia, could alter the public‑health calculus. Until such tools are available, vector control and community education will remain the primary defense.

5. **Stakeholder collaboration** – Coordinated efforts between state health departments, local municipalities, tourism boards, environmental regulators, and community groups can align priorities and resources, ensuring that interventions are both scientifically sound and socially acceptable.

---

11. Conclusion

The emergence of dengue‑carrying mosquitoes in Florida is a multifaceted challenge that reflects broader trends in climate change, global trade, and public‑health infrastructure. Addressing it will require sustained investment, cross‑sector collaboration, and a commitment to evidence‑based policy. By strengthening surveillance, improving vector‑control capacity, and engaging communities in prevention efforts, Florida can reduce the risk of future outbreaks and protect both residents and visitors from this expanding threat.
