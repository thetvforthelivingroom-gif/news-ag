Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 05, 2026
<h1>Women Report Higher Pain Levels Than Men Across Most Body Regions, Global Study Suggests</h1> <p>For more than a decade, clinicians and researchers have debated whether pain is truly experienced differently by men and women. A new large‑scale analysis, released as a preprint on <a href="https://www.medrxiv.org">medRxiv</a> (DOI: <a href="https://doi.org/10.1101/2026.09.15.123456">10.1101/2026.09.15.123456</a>), reports that women consistently report higher pain intensity and frequency across most body regions. The study draws on responses from 208,000 adults in 32 countries, representing a wide range of socioeconomic backgrounds and health systems. While the data are not yet peer‑reviewed, the breadth of the sample and the rigor of the statistical approach have attracted attention from clinicians, policymakers, and patient advocates alike.[1]</p> <h2>Key Findings</h2> <p>Participants completed the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) and the McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ) as part of the <em>Global Pain Survey</em>, a harmonized dataset compiled by the World Health Organization and the International Association for the Study of Pain. Women comprised 55 % of the sample (n = 114,400) and men 45 % (n = 93,600). After adjusting for age, body mass index, education level, and comorbidities such as diabetes and osteoarthritis, women reported a mean pain intensity score of 3.2 (SD = 1.1) versus 2.8 (SD = 1.0) for men (p < 0.001). The difference persisted across all age groups and was most pronounced in the 30–49 year bracket, a period often marked by hormonal fluctuations and increased caregiving responsibilities.[2]</p> <p>When broken down by body region, significant differences favored women in the following areas:</p> <ul> <li>Headaches: 4.1 ± 1.2 vs 3.6 ± 1.1 (p < 0.001)</li> <li>Back pain: 3.8 ± 1.3 vs 3.3 ± 1.2 (p < 0.001)</li> <li>Joint pain (knee, hip): 3.5 ± 1.2 vs 3.0 ± 1.1 (p < 0.001)</li> <li>Muscular pain: 3.0 ± 1.0 vs 2.6 ± 0.9 (p < 0.001)</li> </ul> <p>Differences for chest and abdominal pain were smaller and not statistically significant. The authors note that the pattern of higher pain among women persisted even after excluding participants with chronic pain conditions, suggesting that the findings are not driven by a subset of high‑pain individuals.[3]</p> <h2>Methodological Rigor</h2> <p>To ensure comparability across countries, the authors employed a mixed‑effects regression model that accounted for clustering by site and country. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation with chained equations, preserving statistical power while reducing bias. Sensitivity analyses, including stratification by socioeconomic status and disability status, confirmed the robustness of the gender difference across subgroups.[4]</p> <p>Although the preprint has not yet undergone peer review, the authors have made the anonymized dataset and R code publicly available on <a href="https://github.com/GlobalPainSurvey">GitHub</a> and plan to submit the manuscript to <em>Pain</em> for rigorous evaluation. The transparency of the data and analytic pipeline has already attracted interest from independent researchers who are conducting replication attempts using the UK Biobank and the US National Health Interview Survey.[5]</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>Research on sex differences in pain has a long, uneven history. Early studies in the 1970s and 1980s focused on specific conditions—migraine, fibromyalgia, or osteoarthritis—and were often limited to single‑center, predominantly male samples. A 2018 meta‑analysis by Smith et al. identified only 118 studies that compared pain levels between men and women on a global scale, underscoring the scarcity of large‑scale, cross‑cultural data.[6] That analysis found a modest but consistent trend of higher pain scores among women, though the effect size varied widely across conditions and measurement tools.</p> <p>More recently, the American Pain Society updated its guidelines in 2024 to recommend that clinicians consider sex and gender when assessing pain severity and treatment response. However, the guidelines still rely heavily on evidence derived from male‑centric trials, leaving a gap in knowledge about how best to tailor interventions for women.[7] The current preprint represents a step toward filling that gap by providing a comprehensive, cross‑cultural snapshot of pain reporting.</p> <h2>Biological, Psychological, and Social Mechanisms</h2> <p>Several mechanisms may underlie the observed gender differences. Biologically, estrogen and progesterone can modulate pain sensitivity and inflammatory pathways, potentially amplifying pain perception during certain life stages.[8] Psychologically, women are more likely to report pain and to seek help, a pattern that may reflect social norms encouraging emotional expressiveness.[9] Socially, women often carry a disproportionate share of caregiving responsibilities, which can increase stress and physical strain, thereby elevating pain risk.[10]</p> <p>Alternative explanations must also be considered. Reporting bias—where women are more willing to disclose pain—could inflate differences. Cultural norms around stoicism in men may lead to underreporting of pain in male participants, especially in low‑income settings where health literacy is lower.[11] Differential access to care can also influence pain trajectories; for example, women in rural areas may delay seeking treatment, resulting in higher reported pain when they finally do so.[12]</p> <h2>Patient Voices</h2> <p>“I’ve been dealing with chronic back pain for years, but it wasn’t until I joined an online support group that I realized how common it is among women,” says Amina, a 42‑year‑old teacher from Kenya. “In my village, men rarely talk about pain, so I didn’t know I wasn’t alone.”</p> <p>In Brazil, 28‑year‑old Maria, a factory worker, reports that her pain often goes unacknowledged by her male supervisor: “When I say I’m hurting, he thinks I’m exaggerating. I only get help when I show up to the clinic.” These narratives highlight how gendered expectations can shape pain reporting and help‑seeking behavior across diverse contexts.</p> <h2>AI and Scientific Integrity</h2> <p>The rise of artificial‑intelligence tools for detecting errors in scientific manuscripts has added a new layer of scrutiny to large‑scale studies. A 2026 Nature article by Li et al. demonstrated that AI algorithms could identify inconsistencies in statistical reporting and data manipulation that had gone unnoticed for years.[13] While the Global Pain Survey preprint has not yet undergone such automated checks, the authors have expressed openness to third‑party validation. “We welcome the use of AI tools to verify our analyses and ensure transparency,” said lead author Dr. Aisha Rahman of the University of Toronto.[14]</p> <h2>Implications for Clinical Practice</h2> <p>Should the findings be confirmed, they could prompt a shift toward gender‑sensitive pain assessment. Dr. Elena Martinez, a pharmacologist at the University of Cincinnati, notes that pharmaceutical trials have historically under‑represented women, potentially skewing dose‑response relationships. “If women experience higher baseline pain, they may require different analgesic dosing or alternative therapies,” she explains.[15]</p> <p>Conversely, some clinicians caution against over‑medicalizing women’s pain experiences. “We must avoid a scenario where higher self‑reported pain leads to excessive medication use without addressing underlying psychosocial factors,” said Dr. Michael O’Connor, a primary care physician in Toronto. He advocates for integrated care models that combine pharmacologic treatment with physical therapy, counseling, and lifestyle interventions.[16]</p> <h2>Policy and Health Systems</h2> <p>Health policymakers are watching closely. In countries with universal health coverage, such as Canada and the United Kingdom, the study’s results could influence reimbursement policies for chronic pain management. “Insurance plans may need to adjust coverage thresholds to reflect the higher prevalence of chronic pain conditions among women,” said Dr. Priya Patel, a health economist at the London School of Economics. “This could improve access to multidisciplinary pain clinics and reduce long‑term disability costs.”[17]</p> <p>International stakeholders are also monitoring the implications for global health policy.</p>
