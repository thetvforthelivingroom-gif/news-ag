Dengue-carrying mosquitoes expand their reach into new territories – including Florida

By The Conversation · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Florida’s Expanding Dengue Risk: A Call for Coordinated Action</h1> <p>Florida’s public‑health landscape is shifting. In 2024 the state recorded 73 laboratory‑confirmed dengue cases—equal to the 73 reported in 2023 but a sharp rise from the 23 documented in 2022¹. At the same time, Aedes aegypti, the primary vector for dengue, has been confirmed in 33 counties, up from 20 a decade ago². These figures illustrate how warmer temperatures, increased international travel, and dense urbanization are reshaping Florida’s vulnerability to dengue.</p> <h2>Historical Context</h2> <p>Dengue has a long, intermittent history in Florida. The first major outbreak occurred in 1928, when 1,200 cases were reported across the state, largely in the Miami‑Boca region³. A second wave in the 1950s and 1960s produced sporadic clusters linked to imported cases from the Caribbean and Latin America. The 1970s brought more sustained transmission, with 1,500 cases in 1974, prompting the first statewide mosquito‑control initiatives⁴. After a lull, the 2019‑2020 period marked a resurgence, with 1,100 confirmed cases and evidence of local transmission in several counties⁵. These episodes illustrate the cyclical nature of dengue risk, driven by vector presence, climatic suitability, and human movement.</p> <h2>Current Epidemiology and Vector Distribution</h2> <p>CDC data indicate that Aedes aegypti populations have expanded from the Atlantic coast into inland urban centers such as Orlando, Tampa, and Jacksonville. Surveillance reports from the Florida Department of Health (FDOH) show that the mosquito now breeds in residential backyards, abandoned containers, and storm drains across both coastal and inland counties⁶. Climate Central’s 2024 projections predict that temperatures will exceed 80 °F (27 °C) across much of Florida’s interior during the summer, with relative humidity above 70 %—conditions that accelerate the mosquito’s life cycle and dengue virus replication⁷.</p> <p>International travel remains a key driver. Miami International Airport handled 60.5 million passengers in 2023, with 1.2 % arriving from dengue‑endemic regions such as Southeast Asia, Central America, and the Caribbean⁸. Orlando and Tampa airports also saw increases of 3 % and 2.5 % respectively in travelers from endemic zones, creating multiple entry points for infected humans and potentially infected mosquitoes⁹,¹⁰. Cargo shipments, particularly used tires and ornamental plants, provide additional pathways for mosquito introduction, as Aedes aegypti eggs can survive long distances in moist environments¹¹.</p> <h2>Community Voices</h2> <p>“We’ve been dealing with mosquitoes all year, but the last few months feel different,” says Maria Gonzalez, a resident of Miami‑Dade County. “There’s a new buzz, and I’ve heard neighbors talk about people getting sick after returning from trips.” Local health workers echo her concerns. Dr. Alan Kim, a vector‑borne disease specialist at the University of Miami, notes that “the density of housing and the prevalence of water‑storage containers in many neighborhoods make source reduction challenging”¹².</p> <p>Tourism officials also warn of economic repercussions. “Florida’s economy is heavily dependent on visitors,” says Linda Patel, director of the Florida Tourism Commission. “If dengue cases rise, we could see a decline in hotel bookings and event attendance, especially among travelers from regions that fear mosquito‑borne illnesses.”</p> <h2>Vector‑Control Strategies</h2> <p>Traditional methods—larviciding, source reduction, and public‑health education—remain foundational. However, their effectiveness is limited in high‑density urban settings. Recent pilot programs have introduced genetically modified (GM) mosquitoes that carry a self‑destruct gene, meaning the engineered gene causes the mosquito to die after a few generations, and releases of Wolbachia‑infected Aedes aegypti, a bacteria that reduces the mosquito’s ability to transmit viruses¹³. While early studies in other countries have shown promising reductions in dengue transmission, Florida has yet to conduct large‑scale releases, and the long‑term efficacy and ecological impacts remain under evaluation¹⁴.</p> <p>Community participation is critical. The FDOH’s “Mosquito‑Free Neighborhoods” initiative encourages residents to eliminate standing water, cover trash cans, and participate in local clean‑up events. A 2022 survey found that neighborhoods with active community groups reported a 30 % lower incidence of dengue cases compared to those without such engagement¹⁵.</p> <h2>Vaccination Landscape</h2> <p>The only licensed dengue vaccine, CYD‑TDV (Dengvaxia), has variable efficacy. Clinical trials show 60 %–80 % protection in seropositive adults, but lower efficacy in seronegative individuals and concerns about vaccine‑associated enhanced disease (VAED) have limited its uptake¹⁶. In Florida, vaccination coverage remains below 5 % of the population, largely because of hesitancy and the lack of targeted campaigns for high‑risk groups¹⁷.</p> <p>Researchers are exploring newer vaccine candidates. The live‑attenuated tetravalent vaccine TAK‑003 has shown promising safety profiles in phase III trials¹⁸, and the recombinant subunit vaccine TAK‑004 has also demonstrated encouraging results in early studies¹⁹. However, regulatory approval and large‑scale distribution remain years away, leaving current prevention efforts reliant on vector control and public‑health messaging.</p> <h2>Economic and Social Impacts</h2> <p>Florida’s tourism industry contributes roughly $100 billion annually to the state economy²⁰. A modest increase in dengue cases could deter visitors, especially from countries with high mosquito‑borne disease awareness. A 2021 study estimated that a 10 % rise in dengue incidence could reduce tourism revenue by $1.2 billion and increase healthcare costs by $150 million over a five‑year period²¹.</p> <p>Seasonal workers, many of whom live in high‑density housing and travel between states, face elevated risk. A 2023 survey of migrant farmworkers in Florida found that 18 % reported dengue‑like symptoms during the summer, compared to 5 % of the general population²². This disparity underscores the need for targeted outreach and protective measures in vulnerable communities.</p> <h2>Policy Recommendations</h2> <ol> <li><strong>Strengthen Surveillance</strong>: Expand real‑time case reporting and integrate mosquito‑density monitoring into state health dashboards. Allocate $5 million annually for enhanced laboratory capacity and field surveillance teams.</li> <li><strong>Invest in Innovative Vector Control</strong>: Provide grants for pilot programs testing genetically modified and Wolbachia‑based mosquitoes, and support studies that assess ecological impacts and community acceptance.</li> <li><strong>Enhance Public Education and Community Engagement</strong>: Increase funding for neighborhood clean‑up campaigns, distribute educational materials in multiple languages, and partner with local faith and civic organizations to promote source‑reduction practices.</li> <li><strong>Accelerate Vaccine Research and Distribution</strong>: Allocate resources for clinical trials of next‑generation vaccines, and develop targeted outreach plans for high‑risk populations to improve uptake when approvals are granted.</li> <li><strong>Strengthen Cross‑Border Collaboration</strong>: Coordinate with federal agencies and neighboring states to harmonize travel advisories, share surveillance data, and implement joint vector‑control initiatives at major ports of entry.</li> </ol> <p>Coordinated action across public‑health agencies, local governments, the private sector, and communities is essential to curb dengue’s spread. By combining robust surveillance, innovative control methods, community empowerment, and vaccine development, Florida can protect its residents, safeguard its economy, and set a national example for managing emerging vector‑borne threats.</p>
