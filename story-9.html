The HSV, intended to replace Hubble’s aging power system and extend its operational life, was lost when the Falcon 9 Block 5 booster—SpaceX’s latest variant of the Falcon 9 rocket—suffered a catastrophic failure during ascent. The incident has raised questions about the viability of commercial servicing missions and the regulatory framework that governs them.</p> <h2>Background: Hubble’s Legacy and the Need for Service</h2> <p>Launched in 1990, the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) has produced more than 12,000 scientific papers and transformed our understanding of the universe. Its three servicing missions—STS‑61 in 1993, STS‑82 in 1997, and STS‑125 in 2009—replaced instruments, upgraded gyroscopes, and installed new solar arrays, extending the telescope’s life well beyond its original 15‑year design life. NASA’s Office of Space Science estimates that Hubble’s current power system will remain functional until 2028, after which the telescope’s ability to conduct high‑resolution observations will be severely constrained [1].</p> <p>“Hubble is a national treasure,” said Dr. Maria Gonzalez, senior astronomer at the Space Telescope Science Institute. “Its data have been foundational for countless discoveries, from the acceleration of the universe’s expansion to the characterization of exoplanet atmospheres. Losing Hubble would be a blow to the entire scientific community.”</p> <h2>Astrobotic’s Vision for Commercial Servicing</h2> <p>Astrobotic Technology Inc., founded in 2011, has built a reputation for launching small satellites and performing robotic operations in low Earth orbit. In 2021, NASA selected Astrobotic’s HSV as one of three candidates for a commercial servicing mission under the “Commercial Resupply Services” framework [2]. The HSV was designed to rendezvous with Hubble, detach a new power module, and perform a series of maintenance tasks that would extend the telescope’s operational life by at least five years. The vehicle’s mass was projected at 1,200 kg, and its robotic arm was engineered to dock with Hubble’s existing service port.</p> <p>“We are proud of our track record in precision orbital maneuvers and robotic servicing,” said Astrobotic CEO James Lee in a statement released on 18 March 2024. “The Hubble Rescue mission was an ambitious step toward extending the life of one of NASA’s most valuable scientific assets. We are conducting a thorough investigation to understand the root causes of the failure and to implement corrective actions.”</p> <h2>The Launch Failure</h2> <p>Astrobotic launched the HSV aboard a Falcon 9 Block 5 booster from Cape Canaveral Space Force Station on 14 March 2024. At approximately 12:45 UTC, the second stage’s main engine lost thrust, sending the vehicle onto a ballistic trajectory that forced an uncontrolled re‑entry into the atmosphere. The HSV was lost over the Atlantic Ocean, and no debris was recovered. NASA’s Mission Control and SpaceX’s launch operations team confirmed the incident.</p> <p>“The launch failure was a result of a malfunction in the second stage’s main engine, which caused a loss of thrust and an uncontrolled descent,” said SpaceX launch director Kevin McCarthy. “We are conducting a full investigation in coordination with NASA and Astrobotic.”</p> <h2>Technical Analysis of the Failure</h2> <p>Preliminary reports indicate that the Falcon 9 Block 5’s Merlin 1D engine suffered a valve failure that prevented the engine from maintaining thrust during the second‑stage burn. The loss of thrust caused the vehicle to deviate from its planned trajectory, and the HSV’s onboard guidance system was unable to correct the error because of insufficient time and fuel margins. The vehicle’s reaction wheel assembly, which provides attitude control, was not engaged because the HSV had not yet entered orbit. Consequently, the HSV could not rendezvous with Hubble, and the mission was aborted.</p> <p>“The failure was a combination of a hardware defect in the launch vehicle and a design limitation in the HSV’s mission profile,” explained Dr. Elena Kovács, lead systems engineer at Astrobotic. “Our diagnostic logs show that the HSV’s fault‑tolerant system did not detect the anomaly until it was too late to initiate a safe‑mode sequence.”</p> <h2>Regulatory and Policy Implications</h2> <p>The incident has prompted a review of the commercial servicing framework. In 2020, NASA’s Commercial Resupply Services program was expanded to include “Commercial Servicing” missions, allowing private companies to perform maintenance on government spacecraft. However, the policy framework remains limited in terms of liability, insurance, and regulatory oversight. The loss of the HSV has highlighted gaps in the current system, particularly regarding launch vehicle safety and the integration of commercial payloads with government missions.</p> <p>“We are working with regulators to ensure that future commercial servicing missions meet the highest safety standards,” said NASA Administrator Bill Nelson. “The loss of the HSV underscores the need for robust oversight and clear liability frameworks.”</p> <h2>Industry Perspective</h2> <p>The commercial servicing sector has grown rapidly in the past decade. <article> <h1>Private spacecraft falls back to Earth after failing to rescue aging NASA telescope</h1>

<p>In a dramatic turn of events that has shaken both the scientific community and the burgeoning commercial space sector, a privately‑launched spacecraft lost control and re‑entered Earth’s atmosphere uncontrolled while attempting to rescue the aging <em>Hubble Space Telescope</em> (HST). The vehicle broke apart upon re‑entry, scattering debris over a sparsely populated region of the Pacific Ocean. No injuries were reported, but the loss of the spacecraft and the telescope represents a significant setback for decades of astronomical research.</p>

<p>The mission, dubbed <em>Hubble Rescue 2026</em>, was launched on 12 March 2026 from Cape Canaveral by <em>Astrobotic Inc.</em>, a private company that has built a reputation for deploying small‑satellite constellations and performing robotic servicing of the International Space Station (ISS). The spacecraft, a 1,200‑kg robotic platform equipped with a 1.5‑meter robotic arm and a suite of precision thrusters, was designed to rendezvous with HST, detach a new power module, and perform a series of maintenance tasks that would extend the telescope’s operational life by at least five years.</p>

<p>According to the launch manifest, the spacecraft was expected to reach HST’s orbit within 48 hours, perform the servicing operations over a 72‑hour window, and return to Earth for a controlled re‑entry in late May. However, on 18 March, telemetry indicated a loss of attitude control. The vehicle entered a tumble, and the onboard guidance system failed to reacquire the target. Despite attempts by Mission Control to regain control, the spacecraft was unable to correct its trajectory and was forced into an uncontrolled re‑entry trajectory that culminated in a breakup over the Pacific Ocean on 24 March.</p>
