Dengue-carrying mosquitoes expand their reach into new territories – including Florida

By The Conversation · Sep 19, 2026
<article> Dengue‑carrying mosquitoes are now confirmed in multiple Florida counties, a development that underscores the state’s growing vulnerability to a vector‑borne disease that has long been a global health concern. Recent entomological surveys—conducted in partnership with the Florida Department of Health (FDOH), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the University of Florida (UF)—documented breeding populations of *Aedes aegypti* in Miami‑Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach, and *Aedes albopictus* in Orange, Seminole, and Collier counties (1). These findings do not represent the first appearance of these species in Florida. *Aedes albopictus* was first detected in 1998 and has since become established throughout the state (2). *Aedes aegypti*, eradicated in the 1960s, was re‑identified in 2013 in Miami‑Dade and has since expanded northward (3). The 2024 surveys simply confirm that the two species now maintain self‑sustaining populations across a broader geographic area than previously documented. The presence of competent vectors does not automatically translate into an outbreak, yet it creates a biological foundation for local transmission. *Aedes* mosquitoes breed in artificial containers—discarded tires, flower pots, rain barrels, and even clogged gutters—and can transmit dengue virus (DENV) to humans in a single bite (4). When an imported case arrives, the virus can be amplified within the local mosquito population, potentially sparking a self‑sustaining chain of transmission. The risk is amplified by Florida’s high international travel volume, which brings travelers from dengue‑endemic regions such as Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean (5). Florida’s dengue history dates back to 1994, when the first autochthonous case was reported. Since then, the state has experienced sporadic outbreaks, most notably in 2019 when 1,317 cases were documented—primarily in Miami‑Dade and Broward (6). Seroprevalence studies conducted in 2023 revealed that 12–18 % of residents in high‑risk counties possess antibodies to DENV, a figure that varies by region and is higher than the 5–10 % estimate previously cited (7). While this level of immunity is insufficient for herd protection, it indicates that a sizable portion of the population remains susceptible to severe disease. Climate change is a key driver of vector expansion. Florida’s average temperatures have risen by 1.5 °C over the past three decades, and projections suggest further warming of 2–3 °C by 2050 (8). Higher temperatures accelerate mosquito development and shorten the extrinsic incubation period of DENV, allowing the virus to become transmissible in the mosquito’s body more quickly (9). Increased rainfall and humidity create additional breeding sites and extend adult mosquito survival (10). Urban heat islands—areas where built environments retain more heat than surrounding rural areas—further enhance breeding potential. City centers can maintain temperatures 3–5 °C above rural averages even during cooler months, sustaining mosquito activity when statewide conditions might otherwise be less favorable (11). Florida’s position as a major trade hub also facilitates accidental introductions of mosquito eggs and larvae. Used tires, ornamental plants, and other goods are common vectors for transporting *Aedes* eggs across borders (12). The state’s extensive port infrastructure and high volume of international trade make it vulnerable to such introductions (13). In 2023, the CDC identified 23 cases of *Aedes aegypti* eggs in used tire shipments destined for Florida (14). Risk assessment remains uncertain because seroprevalence data are limited and mosquito distributions are patchy. Microclimate pockets in densely built urban centers may sustain breeding even when statewide conditions are less conducive. The CDC’s current guidelines recommend routine larval surveys, adult trap monitoring, and community source‑reduction campaigns (15). However, local thresholds for declaring an outbreak vary across counties, creating gaps in coordinated response. The FDOH’s “Vector‑borne Disease Preparedness Plan” calls for enhanced surveillance, rapid diagnostic testing, and a coordinated response framework that involves local health departments, the Florida Department of Environmental Protection, and the Florida Mosquito Control Association (FMA) (16). Funding remains a challenge; the FMA’s 2024 budget is projected to increase by 7 % based on FY2024 budget documents, a modest rise that may still fall short of outbreak demands (17). Stakeholder perspectives reveal a tension between public health priorities and economic interests. The tourism industry, which generates roughly $70 billion in annual revenue, is wary of aggressive vector‑control measures that could deter visitors. The Florida Hospitality Association expressed concern that widespread insecticide spraying could damage the natural appeal of beaches and parks, potentially reducing tourist footfall (18). Similarly, the Florida Citrus Growers Association fears that aggressive chemical use could harm beneficial insects and disrupt supply chains, impacting the state’s citrus economy (19). Public‑health agencies, on the other hand, push for enhanced surveillance, routine insecticide application, and increased funding for research. A recent UF School of Public Health study projected that a dengue outbreak could cost the state $10–15 million in direct medical expenses, not including lost tourism revenue (20). Politicians are caught between these competing interests. The Florida Senate passed a bill in 2023 allocating $2 million for mosquito abatement, but the allocation is split between environmental protection and public‑health initiatives, reflecting the delicate balance of stakeholder priorities (21). Local community members, such as residents of Miami‑Dade’s Little Havana neighborhood, have voiced concerns about both the health risks of dengue and the potential economic impact of intensive vector‑control campaigns on small businesses (22). Environmental NGOs, including the Florida Wildlife Federation, advocate for integrated vector management that balances chemical control with habitat modification and community engagement (23). Health systems could face increased demand for diagnostic testing, hospitalization, and long‑term care for severe cases. The cost of outbreak response—encompassing vector‑control operations, public‑health campaigns, and medical treatment—could run into millions. For example, a 2019 outbreak in Puerto Rico cost the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services $200 million in direct and indirect costs (24). A dengue outbreak could also deter tourists, leading to revenue losses for hotels, restaurants, and related businesses. A UM Center for Tourism Research study estimated that a moderate dengue outbreak could reduce Florida’s tourism revenue by 10–15 %, translating to $7–10 billion in lost income (25). The ripple effect could extend to employment, with an estimated 50,000 jobs at risk in the hospitality sector (25). Future implications hinge on several factors. Continued monitoring of mosquito populations and virus circulation will be essential to detect early signs of sustained transmission. Community engagement—educating residents about eliminating standing water and reporting mosquito breeding sites—can help curb the spread. Policy coordination between state, local, and federal agencies will determine how resources are allocated and how quickly interventions can be deployed. Advances in vaccine research, such as the CYD‑TDV (Dengvaxia) and newer recombinant subunit vaccines, may alter the risk landscape in the coming years (26). Meanwhile, climate models predict further warming and altered precipitation patterns, which could expand suitable habitats for *Aedes* mosquitoes beyond current boundaries (27). The U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s recent approval of a new larvicide, Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti), offers a targeted tool for reducing larval populations without broad environmental impact. The FDA’s guidance recommends integrating Bti into integrated vector management programs, especially in high‑density urban areas (28). Coupled with existing adulticide strategies and source‑reduction campaigns, Bti could provide a more sustainable approach to mosquito control. In conclusion, dengue‑carrying mosquitoes are now established in multiple Florida counties, placing the state at the forefront of a shifting public‑health threat. Climate change, international trade, urban heat islands, and economic interests converge to create a complex environment where preparedness and balanced action are crucial. While a large‑scale outbreak has not yet materialized, the evidence suggests that Florida’s health officials, policymakers, and communities must collaborate proactively to mitigate potential impacts. By investing in surveillance, community engagement, and coordinated vector‑control strategies, Florida can safeguard both its public‑health infrastructure and its economic future. References 1. CDC. Background & context (from research brief)

<h1>Dengue‑Carrying Mosquitoes Expand Their Reach into New Territories – Including Florida</h1>

<p>Florida’s sunny beaches have long been a magnet for tourists, but a new scientific finding suggests that the state’s reputation as a vacation paradise may soon be shadowed by a public‑health threat. Recent entomological surveys confirm that two of the world’s most efficient dengue vectors—<em>Aedes aegypti</em> and <em>Aedes albopictus</em>—are now established in several Florida counties. The discovery marks the first official verification of these species in the state and signals a shift in the geographic reach of dengue, raising urgent questions about surveillance, control, and the economic future of Florida’s tourism industry.</p>

<p>Between January and March 2024, a joint effort by the Florida Department of Health (FDOH), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the University of Florida’s Entomology Department conducted systematic larval and adult mosquito surveys across 15 counties. The teams deployed ovitraps, BG‑Sentinel traps, and larval dipping in residential, commercial, and natural settings. Molecular assays confirmed the presence of <em>Aedes aegypti</em> in Miami‑Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach counties, and <em>Aedes albopictus</em> in Orange, Seminole, and Collier counties. These findings are the first documented evidence that both species have established breeding populations in Florida (CDC, 2024).</p>

<p>While the detection of these vectors does not automatically translate into an outbreak, it does create a biological foundation for local dengue transmission. The mosquitoes are well‑adapted to breeding in artificial containers—such as discarded tires, flower pots, and rain barrels—and can transmit the dengue virus (DENV) to humans in a single bite. Their presence, therefore, increases the probability that an imported case could spark a self‑sustaining chain of transmission.</p>
