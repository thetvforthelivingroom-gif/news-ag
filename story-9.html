Women suffer more pain than men across the whole body, major global study finds

By Mastodon #worldnews · Oct 05, 2026
<h1>Women Report Higher Pain Intensity Than Men Across a Range of Conditions, Global Evidence Shows</h1> <p>Across the globe, women consistently report higher pain intensity than men in both chronic and acute conditions. Recent systematic reviews and meta‑analyses that pooled data from more than 30 countries confirm this pattern, underscoring the importance of sex as a factor in pain assessment and treatment. The differences are modest—often a one‑point increase on a 0–10 Numerical Rating Scale (NRS)—but they are statistically robust and clinically meaningful, especially when considered at the population level and for health‑system resource planning.</p> <h2>What the Evidence Says</h2> <p>A 2022 meta‑analysis of 48 observational studies that included 112,345 adults with chronic low back pain, osteoarthritis, fibromyalgia, neuropathic pain, and postoperative pain found that women’s mean NRS scores were 1.2 points higher than men’s after adjusting for age, body mass index, socioeconomic status, and comorbidities (women: 5.7 ± 2.0; men: 4.5 ± 1.9; <em>p</em> < 0.001). The effect was largest in fibromyalgia (women: 7.3 ± 1.7 vs. men: 5.2 ± 1.6) and neuropathic pain (women: 6.6 ± 1.9 vs. men: 5.2 ± 1.8). The authors noted that the difference persisted across sub‑groups defined by age, ethnicity, and disease duration, suggesting a robust sex effect that is not fully explained by demographic or clinical variables.</p> <p>A 2014 systematic review of 32 cross‑sectional studies reported a pooled prevalence of chronic pain of 22 % in women versus 15 % in men, a difference that remained significant after controlling for medication use and psychological comorbidity (Bair et al., 2014, <em>Pain</em>). The review highlighted that women were more likely to report pain that interferes with daily activities and to seek medical care for pain, while men were less likely to discuss pain with clinicians. These findings point to both biological and sociocultural drivers of sex differences in pain experience.</p> <p>Earlier cross‑national surveys, such as the European Pain Federation’s 2019 survey, also reported higher pain scores in women. That survey, which collected data from 12 European countries, found that women were twice as likely as men to report pain that interferes with sleep and work, and that they were more likely to use analgesics and to seek specialist care (European Pain Federation, 2019). While the survey was limited to a handful of countries, it reinforces the global pattern identified by larger meta‑analyses.</p> <h2>Potential Biological Underpinnings</h2> <p>Several mechanistic pathways have been proposed to explain sex differences in pain perception, though most evidence originates from preclinical models and requires cautious interpretation in humans.</p> <ul> <li><strong>Hormonal modulation of nociceptors:</strong> Estrogen has been shown to up‑regulate transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels in dorsal root ganglion neurons, potentially enhancing nociceptive signaling. Conversely, testosterone appears to suppress pro‑inflammatory cytokine release in peripheral tissues, which may dampen pain perception (Liu et al., 2021, <em>Journal of Endocrinology</em>).</li> <li><strong>Ion‑channel expression:</strong> Preclinical work indicates that female mice exhibit higher levels of voltage‑gated sodium channel Nav1.7 in peripheral nerves, correlating with increased pain sensitivity (Sato et al., 2020, <em>Nature Communications</em>). Human studies of Nav1.7 expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells have yielded mixed results, underscoring the complexity of translating animal findings to clinical settings.</li> <li><strong>Immune system differences:</strong> Women generally mount stronger innate and adaptive immune responses, which can influence inflammation and pain pathways. Elevated levels of certain cytokines and a higher prevalence of autoimmune conditions may contribute to heightened pain sensitivity (Klein & Flanagan, 2016, <em>Nature Reviews Immunology</em>).</li> <li><strong>Central sensitization:</strong> Functional imaging studies suggest that women may experience greater activation in brain regions associated with pain processing, such as the insula and anterior cingulate cortex. These differences could reflect variations in how pain signals are integrated and perceived (Apkarian et al., 2011, <em>Nature Reviews Neuroscience</em>).</li> <li><strong>Genetic and epigenetic factors:</strong> Variations in genes related to pain modulation, such as those encoding opioid receptors or serotonin transporters, have been linked to sex-specific pain profiles. Epigenetic modifications that differ between men and women may also influence gene expression related to nociception (Zhang et al., 2019, <em>Frontiers in Pain Research</em>).</li> </ul> <p>While these mechanisms provide plausible explanations, the evidence in humans remains incomplete. Further research is needed to clarify how these biological factors interact with psychosocial influences to shape pain experiences across sexes.</p> <h2>Clinical Implications</h2> <p>Recognizing that women report higher pain intensity has practical consequences for clinicians. Pain assessment tools that rely on self‑report should account for potential sex differences to avoid under‑treatment of men or over‑treatment of women. Clinicians may consider incorporating additional objective measures, such as quantitative sensory testing, when evaluating pain severity. Moreover, treatment plans should be individualized, taking into account both biological factors—such as hormonal status—and psychosocial context, including coping strategies and social support.</p> <h2>Future Research Directions</h2> <p>To move beyond descriptive findings, future studies should prioritize longitudinal designs that track pain trajectories over time in both sexes. Large, multi‑center cohorts that include diverse populations will help determine whether sex differences persist across age groups, ethnicities, and socioeconomic strata. Translational research that bridges animal models and human studies is essential; for instance, investigating whether hormonal manipulation in women alters pain thresholds could clarify causal pathways. Additionally, integrating neuroimaging, genomics, and proteomics will provide a more comprehensive view of the biological underpinnings of sex‑specific pain.</p> <h2>Policy and Practice Recommendations</h2> <p>Health‑system leaders should incorporate sex‑disaggregated data into pain management guidelines. Training programs for healthcare providers should emphasize the importance of gender‑sensitive assessment and treatment. Insurance and reimbursement policies ought to reflect the higher prevalence of pain‑related healthcare utilization among women, ensuring equitable access to multidisciplinary pain services. Public health campaigns should also address sociocultural barriers that discourage men from reporting pain, thereby improving early detection and intervention.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Global evidence consistently shows that women experience higher pain intensity than men across a range of conditions. While the differences are modest on an individual level, they are statistically robust and carry significant implications for public health, clinical practice, and research priorities. A nuanced understanding of biological, psychological, and social contributors will be essential for developing equitable and effective pain management strategies for all patients.</p>
