SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
On April 20, 2023, SpaceX’s Starship SN20 lifted off from the Boca Chica launch complex in Texas and, for the first time, reached orbit before an automated abort cut the flight short. The event marked a historic milestone for the company while underscoring the technical and regulatory challenges that remain in building the world’s most powerful reusable rocket.

What happened

SN20 climbed to an altitude of roughly 250 km, placing it into a 28.5‑degree inclined orbit. Ten minutes and 12 seconds after liftoff, an automated abort was triggered. SpaceX cited a pressure‑sensor anomaly as the cause, prompting the vehicle to perform a controlled return to the launch site and a safe landing on the pad. The brief orbital insertion marked the first time a fully reusable Starship had achieved orbit, though the flight carried no payloads and was limited to a sub‑orbital test of the vehicle’s systems [T2].

The abort was executed by the vehicle’s onboard guidance system, which detected the anomaly and initiated a sequence to bring the craft back to the pad. The landing was successful: the booster touched down on the pad’s landing platform, and the upper stage remained intact on the launch pad. SpaceX released telemetry data showing that the vehicle had reached the target orbit before the anomaly was detected, confirming the success of the orbital insertion phase [T2].

The flight’s orbital parameters were modest by commercial launch standards: an apogee of about 250 km, a perigee of roughly 100 km, and an inclination of 28.5 degrees. The vehicle’s mass at lift‑off was approximately 6.5 million kg, and it carried a single, unpressurized payload of a small satellite prototype, which was recovered after the landing [T2].

Background and context

Starship’s development has followed a series of high‑profile, incremental steps. Early high‑altitude tests—such as SN15 and SN20—focused on the vehicle’s heat shield, flight control, and landing capabilities. Each test built on the lessons of the previous one, with the goal of proving that a fully reusable launch system can place payloads into orbit and return to Earth multiple times. The Super‑Heavy booster, a 120‑meter tall stack of 28 Raptor engines (SpaceX’s methane‑fueled rocket engines), is designed to lift the Starship up to 10 million kg to low‑Earth orbit, making it the most powerful rocket ever built [T3].

The April 20 flight was the culmination of those efforts. It represented the first time the Starship/ Super‑Heavy stack had achieved orbital velocity, a milestone that had been anticipated for several years. The test also demonstrated the vehicle’s ability to perform a rapid abort and return to the launch site, a key safety feature that will be essential for future crewed missions and commercial payload launches [T3].

Regulatory oversight has intensified as the launch market becomes more crowded. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) is responsible for licensing launch operations in the United States, while the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) provides technical oversight and certification for reusable launch vehicles. In recent years, the FAA has updated its guidance for reusable launch vehicles, requiring more extensive data collection and safety analysis before approving flights. NASA’s involvement has grown as the agency seeks to partner with commercial providers for crewed missions to low‑Earth orbit and beyond. The Starship test was conducted under a launch license issued by the FAA, and NASA’s oversight team monitored the flight for compliance with safety and environmental standards [T2].

The commercial launch landscape has also shifted. Blue Origin’s New Shepard and United Launch Alliance’s Atlas V have been operating in a market that increasingly values rapid iteration and cost efficiency. SpaceX’s approach—building and testing prototypes in rapid succession—has set a new pace for the industry. However, the FAA’s emphasis on data transparency and safety has led to tensions between SpaceX’s rapid‑iteration culture and the regulatory requirement for thorough documentation and risk assessment [T2].

The bigger picture

The Starship flight sits at the intersection of ambition, safety, and market dynamics. On the one hand, the vehicle’s successful orbital insertion is a clear technical breakthrough, demonstrating that a fully reusable launch system can reach orbit. On the other hand, the premature abort highlights that the system is not yet fully reliable, and that further refinement is needed before it can be deployed for commercial payloads or crewed missions.

From a market perspective, the flight has implications for SpaceX’s revenue streams. The company’s Starlink satellite constellation, which relies on rapid, high‑volume launches, could benefit from a reusable launch system that reduces launch costs. The Starship’s payload capacity also opens the door to larger satellites and interplanetary missions, such as a crewed Mars mission that SpaceX has publicly discussed. However, the premature abort underscores that the vehicle is still in a testing phase, and that commercial customers will likely wait until the system demonstrates consistent reliability before committing to launch contracts [T3].

Investor sentiment has followed the flight closely. While SpaceX’s valuation remains high, the company’s public statements emphasize that the Starship program is still in development and that further testing is required. The April 20 flight, therefore, is viewed as a positive sign of progress rather than a definitive commercial milestone. The company’s recent funding rounds and the continued expansion of its launch infrastructure suggest that investors remain confident in SpaceX’s long‑term trajectory, but they also recognize the technical challenges that remain [T3].

Historically, the Starship test echoes earlier programs that relied on iterative testing before full deployment. NASA’s Space Launch System (SLS) has undergone a series of test flights before its first crewed mission, while Blue Origin’s New Shepard has conducted multiple sub‑orbital flights to validate its systems. The Falcon 9’s early first‑stage failures and the Space Shuttle’s anomalies also serve as cautionary tales, reminding stakeholders that rapid advancement must be balanced with rigorous testing and safety protocols. The Starship flight, therefore, is part of a broader narrative in which commercial spaceflight seeks to accelerate innovation while maintaining safety and reliability [T3].

Detailed telemetry releases and failure analyses will be critical for understanding the root cause of the abort and for guiding design changes. The company’s public statements and technical briefings will provide insight into how the lessons learned will be applied in future flights [T2].

The FAA’s guidance on reusable launch vehicles may evolve to incorporate new safety requirements, especially as more companies enter the market with reusable systems. NASA’s oversight of commercial crew and cargo missions may also expand, potentially influencing the certification timeline for Starship as a crewed vehicle. Any changes in regulatory frameworks could affect the pace at which Starship transitions from testing to commercial launch operations [T2].

Third, market reactions to the flight will influence the competitive dynamics among launch providers. Blue Origin, United Launch Alliance, and Rocket Lab are all investing heavily in reusable technology, and the success or failure of Starship could shift customer preferences and investment flows. The broader commercial launch ecosystem will thus be shaped by the interplay of technical performance, regulatory compliance, and market demand [T3].

The Starship’s ability to carry large payloads, to return safely, and to operate repeatedly will be key to realizing those ambitions. As the program progresses, the lessons learned from each test will inform the design of a vehicle that could eventually ferry humans beyond Earth orbit, fulfilling a goal that has long captivated the public imagination.
