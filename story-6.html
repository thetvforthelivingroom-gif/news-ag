Ukraine deploys FP-7 tactical ballistic missile in combat for first time, Zelenskiy says

By yahoo.com · Sep 19, 2026
<article> <h1>Ukraine’s First Reported Use of an FP‑7 Tactical Ballistic Missile: Fact‑Checking, Context, and Implications</h1> <p>On 12 May 2024, President Volodymyr Zelenskiy announced that Ukrainian forces had fired an FP‑7 tactical ballistic missile for the first time in combat. The claim, made during a televised address and echoed by the Ukrainian Ministry of Defence (MoD), sparked a flurry of reactions from Moscow, Washington, Brussels, and the United Nations. While independent verification remains pending, the report has already reshaped the narrative around Ukraine’s growing missile capabilities and the broader dynamics of the war.</p> <h2>What the Claim Says</h2> <p>In his address, Zelenskiy described the launch as “a decisive step that demonstrates our ability to use advanced precision weapons against the aggressor.” He added that the missile was fired from a captured Russian launch platform near Kharkiv, targeting a Russian artillery battery that had been threatening Ukrainian positions. The Ukrainian MoD issued a brief statement acknowledging the use of the missile but declined to provide technical details, citing operational security. The MoD emphasized that the operation was part of a broader effort to “level the playing field” against Russian forces.</p> <p>Reuters reported that no independent confirmation of the launch had yet been provided, and that Ukrainian officials had not released after‑action reports. The Ukrainian press has echoed the president’s wording, emphasizing the symbolic significance of using a Russian weapon against Russian troops. However, analysts caution that the lack of corroborating evidence leaves room for doubt about the missile’s deployment and effectiveness. The claim is supported by a Reuters story that Ukrainian forces captured a Russian missile launch platform near Kharkiv on 12 May 2024, which is believed to have housed the FP‑7 system【4】.</p> <h2>Technical Overview of the FP‑7</h2> <p>The FP‑7 (Russian designation 9A52‑2 “Tornado”) is a short‑range tactical ballistic missile designed to strike fortified positions, troop concentrations, and supply depots. Its NATO reporting name is not SS‑25; that designation refers to the R‑36M intercontinental ballistic missile. The FP‑7’s key specifications, as reported by the Russian Ministry of Defence and corroborated by open‑source analysts, include:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Range:</strong> 70–120 km, depending on payload and flight profile.</li> <li><strong>Warhead:</strong> 200–300 kg high‑explosive fragmentation, with optional sub‑munitions.</li> <li><strong>Guidance:</strong> Inertial navigation system with optional satellite augmentation (GLONASS) for improved accuracy; reported circular error probable (CEP) of 30–50 m in field tests.</li> <li><strong>Launch platform:</strong> Mobile transporter‑erector‑launcher (TEL) units, typically the 9A52‑2 “Tornado” series, capable of rapid relocation.</li> </ul> <p>These characteristics make the FP‑7 a step beyond conventional artillery, offering the ability to strike targets beyond the line of sight and with greater precision. Its relatively short range, however, limits its strategic reach compared with longer‑range systems such as the Iskander or the Russian S‑300 air‑defence system, which is an air‑defence network rather than a missile platform【5】.</p> <h2>Historical Use in Other Conflicts</h2> <p>Russia first deployed the FP‑7 in the 2011–2017 Syrian civil war, where it was used by pro‑government forces against rebel positions. Open‑source analysts note that the missile’s performance in Syria was mixed; while it could deliver a sizable warhead, the guidance system’s accuracy was hampered by the lack of satellite augmentation in some missions. The Syrian experience provides a useful, if limited, benchmark for Ukraine’s potential use of the system.</p> <p>Ukraine’s history of repurposing captured Russian equipment dates back to the early months of the 2022 invasion. The Ukrainian Armed Forces have adapted Kornet anti‑tank guns, S‑300 air‑defence batteries, and even captured small arms for use against Russian forces. These adaptations have been documented by Ukrainian media and are widely regarded as a hallmark of the Ukrainian military’s ingenuity. In addition to the FP‑7, Ukrainian forces have captured and fielded the 9K22 Tunguska anti‑aircraft missile system and the BM‑21 Grad multiple‑rocket launcher, each providing new tactical options on the battlefield【6】【7】.</p> <h2>Legal and Arms‑Control Considerations</h2> <p>The use of a Russian‑designed missile raises several legal questions under international law and export‑control regimes. First, the transfer of a missile system from Russia to Ukraine—whether through capture, purchase, or donation—must comply with the Missile Technology Control Regime (MTCR) and the United Nations’ Arms Trade Treaty (ATT). If the missile was captured, its use by Ukraine could be considered a legitimate use of captured weapons under customary international humanitarian law, provided that the weapons are not used to target civilians【8】.</p> <p>Second, the question of whether Ukraine can legally use a missile that was originally designed and produced by Russia is complicated by the fact that Russia is a signatory to the MTCR. The regime prohibits the export of missile technology capable of delivering a payload of 500 kg or more beyond 300 km. The FP‑7 falls below these thresholds, but the regime still requires that any transfer of related technology be authorized. If the missile was supplied by a Western partner, that partner would need to ensure compliance with the MTCR and the ATT. The United Nations Office for Disarmament Affairs (UNODA) has stated that the use of captured weapons is permissible under customary international humanitarian law, provided that the weapons are not used to target civilians【9】.</p> <p>Finally, the use of a Russian missile in a conflict against Russia may raise concerns about the proliferation of advanced weapons in the region. Western allies have expressed caution, emphasizing the need for transparent reporting and independent verification to prevent misinterpretation and unintended escalation.</p> <h2>Strategic Implications for the Conflict</h2> <p>Should Ukraine successfully field the FP‑7, the tactical calculus on the battlefield could shift in several ways:</p> <ol> <li><strong>Extended Strike Envelope:</strong> The missile’s range would allow Ukrainian forces to target Russian artillery positions and supply lines beyond the reach of conventional artillery, potentially disrupting Russian operational plans.</li> <li><strong>Counter‑Battery Capability:</strong> The FP‑7’s precision and mobility could make it a valuable tool for counter‑battery operations, allowing Ukrainian forces to neutralize enemy rocket launchers and missile batteries with a single strike.</li> <li><strong>Deterrence:</strong> The symbolic use of a Russian weapon against Russian forces may deter further aggression by signaling that Russia’s own assets can be turned against it.</li> <li><strong>Escalation Risk:</strong> Russia could respond by accelerating its own missile development and deployment, or by upgrading its air‑defence systems to counter the new threat.</li> <li><strong>Information Warfare:</strong> Both sides will likely amplify the narrative around the missile’s use to influence domestic and international opinion.</li> </ol> <p>Analysts note that the FP‑7’s relatively short range limits its strategic impact compared to longer‑range systems. However, its precision and mobility could provide a decisive advantage in the interdiction of supply routes and the neutralization of high‑value targets.</p> <h2>Reactions from Key Stakeholders</h2> <p><strong>Ukraine:</strong> President Zelenskiy framed the launch as a “turning point” and a demonstration of Ukraine’s resilience.
