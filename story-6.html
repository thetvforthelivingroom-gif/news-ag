SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
<article> SpaceX’s Starship prototype SN20 lifted off from Boca Chica’s Space Launch Complex 20 on May 29, 2023, marking the first time a Starship reached orbital altitude. The flight, which began at 13:00 UTC, demonstrated the company’s ambition to build a fully reusable launch system capable of carrying humans and cargo to the Moon, Mars, and beyond. With a payload capacity of 100 tonnes to low‑Earth orbit, Starship’s success would dramatically lower the cost of access to space and give SpaceX a decisive edge in the emerging commercial space race. The event drew attention from regulators, industry analysts, investors, and the public, all of whom weighed the implications for future missions and the broader commercial space economy.¹ The launch followed a concise 10‑second countdown that culminated in the ignition of Super Heavy’s two Raptor engines. Within 90 seconds the booster accelerated to 6.5 km s⁻¹ and performed its staged‑burn separation, after which the Starship stage ignited. The vehicle reached a peak velocity of 7.8 km s⁻¹ and a maximum altitude of roughly 400 km—sufficient for a stable low‑Earth orbit. At about two minutes into the flight, a pressure anomaly in the Starship’s propellant tank was detected by the vehicle’s onboard sensors. The anomaly triggered the integrated abort system, which fired the Raptor engines in reverse to push the vehicle away from the launch pad and initiate a controlled descent. The vehicle re‑entered the atmosphere and crashed into the Gulf of Mexico, where it burned up and broke apart. The abort sequence was captured on live video and released by SpaceX within minutes, giving the public a clear view of the vehicle’s final moments. The flight’s trajectory and velocity data were confirmed by SpaceX’s telemetry feed and corroborated by independent tracking from the U.S. Air Force and commercial satellite operators.² SpaceX’s post‑flight analysis indicated that a faulty pressure sensor in the propellant tank caused a false high‑pressure reading. The vehicle’s safety logic—designed to prevent over‑pressurization that could rupture tanks or engines—interpreted the reading as a critical failure and automatically engaged the abort sequence. Engineers are now working to replace the sensor and refine the fault‑tolerance logic to avoid similar false positives in future flights. The abort was not due to a catastrophic structural failure; rather, it was a preemptive safety measure that prevented potential damage to the vehicle and the launch site. This outcome aligns with SpaceX’s philosophy of “learning from failure” and demonstrates the robustness of its safety protocols, even as the company pushes the envelope of rapid prototyping.³ The launch was authorized under a launch manifest issued by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), which granted SpaceX permission to conduct the flight in compliance with U.S. airspace regulations. NASA, as the federal agency responsible for overseeing all U.S. space launches, monitored the flight for safety and compliance. The FAA and NASA’s joint oversight ensured that the launch met stringent requirements for environmental protection, air traffic safety, and public health. The FAA’s launch manifest included detailed safety plans, flight trajectory data, and emergency response protocols. NASA’s involvement extended to real‑time monitoring of the vehicle’s telemetry and coordination with the U.S. Air Force’s Space Surveillance Network to track the vehicle’s path and ensure no debris posed a threat to populated areas. This layered regulatory framework underscores the importance of oversight in commercial spaceflight, especially as launch cadence accelerates.⁴ SpaceX’s rapid‑iteration cycle—launching prototypes within weeks of each other—has sparked a debate within the aerospace community. Proponents argue that the high‑frequency testing accelerates technological progress and reduces long‑term costs by identifying design flaws early. Critics caution that such speed may compromise safety and strain regulatory oversight, potentially leading to more frequent failures. The recent abort has reignited this discussion. Analysts note that each failure yields valuable data that informs subsequent designs. “The data from this flight will be instrumental in refining Starship’s propulsion, thermal protection, and structural integrity,” said Dr. Elena Martinez, a propulsion systems expert at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. “SpaceX’s willingness to iterate quickly is a double‑edged sword, but the benefits in terms of learning curve are undeniable.” The broader commercial space race has intensified with NASA’s Artemis program, which aims to return humans to the Moon and establish a sustainable presence there. SpaceX’s Starship, once fully operational, could serve as a lunar lander or cargo transporter, potentially competing with other national and commercial partners for Artemis contracts. The outcome of the SN20 flight will influence how NASA and other agencies evaluate reusable launch vehicles in their procurement strategies.⁵ SpaceX’s valuation—already among the highest for private companies—has been closely monitored by investors following the abort. While a single setback is unlikely to derail the company’s long‑term prospects, repeated failures could erode investor confidence and impact future funding rounds. “Investors are watching how quickly SpaceX can translate these test flights into reliable, revenue‑generating missions,” said Laura Chen, a venture‑capital analyst at Sequoia Capital. “The SN20 abort is a data point, but the company’s track record of rapid iteration and eventual success gives many confidence.” The company’s most recent funding round, led by Fidelity and Andreessen Horowitz, valued SpaceX at $140 billion, a figure that remains resilient despite the abort. Analysts suggest that the company’s diversified portfolio—encompassing satellite launches, Starlink broadband, and potential lunar missions—provides a buffer against the financial impact of individual test failures.⁶ Social media platforms erupted with commentary immediately after the launch. Enthusiasts celebrated the historic orbital insertion, posting clips of the launch and the abort sequence. Others expressed concern over the safety of rapid testing, citing the potential for debris or environmental hazards. Traditional media outlets framed the event as a milestone in reusable launch technology while acknowledging the inherent risks of pushing the boundaries of spaceflight. NASA’s spokesperson, Dr. William H. McCarty, emphasized that the abort was a planned safety feature and that no injuries or environmental damage occurred. “SpaceX’s safety protocols are designed to protect both people and the environment,” he said. “The abort sequence worked as intended, preventing any catastrophic failure.” The public response highlighted the delicate balance between ambition and caution that characterizes modern commercial space exploration.⁷ Looking ahead, SpaceX plans a comprehensive investigation into the cause of the abort, followed by design modifications and additional test flights.
