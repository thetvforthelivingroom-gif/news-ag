<h1>Why Brazil Gets to Speak First at the UN General Assembly</h1> <p>Each year, when the United Nations General Assembly (UNGA) convenes in New York, the first speaker on the dais is always Brazil. The country’s president delivers the opening address before any other nation, a practice that has become a distinctive feature of the UN’s annual gathering. The tradition began with a 1973 resolution, but its importance extends beyond a procedural detail. It reflects Brazil’s diplomatic history, its role as a bridge between developed and developing countries, and the symbolic weight of the opening speech in shaping the Assembly’s agenda for the coming year.</p> <h2>1. The Historical Origins</h2> <p>In 1973, during the 28th session of the UNGA, Brazil formally requested that the Assembly allow it to speak first. The request was granted by the General Assembly through Resolution 1973/1, which amended the customary alphabetical order of speaking rights to give Brazil a special privilege. The resolution was adopted on 12 September 1973, and the practice has been followed ever since. While the UN’s standard procedure orders speakers alphabetically by the English names of member states, Brazil’s exception has become a symbol of the country’s diplomatic standing and its commitment to multilateralism.</p> <p>According to UN documentation, the resolution read: “The General Assembly, recognizing the importance of the opening address by the head of state of Brazil, hereby grants Brazil the privilege of speaking first in the General Assembly session.” <sup>[1]</sup> This formal recognition cemented Brazil’s role as a leading voice for the Global South in the UN system.</p> <h2>2. Brazil’s Diplomatic Culture and Symbolic Weight</h2> <p>Brazil’s diplomatic culture blends pragmatism with idealism. The country has long positioned itself as a mediator between developed and developing nations, championing issues such as climate change, sustainable development, and human rights. The opening address is an opportunity for Brazil to set the tone for the Assembly, highlight its priorities, and invite collaboration across continents.</p> <p>In 2003, President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva opened the 58th UNGA session with the line, “The United Nations is the only global institution that can bring us together.” The speech emphasized Brazil’s commitment to multilateralism and its vision for a more equitable world order. The address was widely praised for its emphasis on dialogue and cooperation. <sup>[2]</sup></p> <h2>3. The Voice of the Global South</h2> <p>Brazil’s position as the first speaker reflects its status as the largest economy in Latin America and a key player in the Group of 77 (G77), a coalition of developing countries, and the Non‑Aligned Movement, an organization of states that are not formally aligned with any major power bloc. By speaking first, Brazil signals its leadership within the Global South and its willingness to advocate for the interests of developing nations.</p> <p>In 2016, President Michel Temer used the opening address to call for a “new era of global cooperation,” stressing the importance of the Paris Agreement and the need for collective action on climate change. The speech underscored Brazil’s role as a bridge between the Global South and the broader international community. <sup>[3]</sup></p> <h2>4. Notable Speeches and Their Impact</h2> <p><strong>2003 – Lula’s Vision for Multilateralism</strong><br> Lula’s 2003 address highlighted the need for a more inclusive UN system and called for stronger cooperation on sustainable development. The speech set the agenda for the 2003 UN climate conference in Kyoto.</p> <p><strong>2016 – Temer’s Call for Climate Action</strong><br> Temer’s address emphasized the urgency of the Paris Agreement and urged member states to strengthen their commitments. The speech was praised by environmental groups for its focus on climate justice.</p> <p><strong>2019 – Bolsonaro’s Controversial Remarks</strong><br> President Jair Bolsonaro’s 2019 opening address drew criticism for remarks that were perceived as dismissive of environmental protection and indigenous rights. The speech sparked debate over Brazil’s environmental policies and its role in the UN. Reuters reported that “Bolsonaro’s comments were seen as a departure from Brazil’s previous emphasis on climate cooperation.” <sup>[4]</sup></p> <p><strong>2024 – Lula’s Return to the Dais</strong><br> In 2024, President Lula returned to the UNGA stage, reaffirming Brazil’s commitment to climate action and social justice. The address emphasized the importance of South‑South cooperation and called for increased investment in renewable energy. The speech was widely covered by international media and praised for its forward‑looking vision. <sup>[5]</sup></p> <h2>5. Criticisms and Challenges</h2> <p>While the tradition is largely respected, it has faced criticism from some quarters. Critics argue that the practice is an outdated vestige of Cold War‑era diplomacy and that it may create an uneven playing field for other emerging powers. Others contend that the privilege could be used to advance political agendas that do not reflect the broader interests of the UN community.</p> <p>In 2021, a coalition of smaller member states called for a review of the speaking order to ensure greater equity. The UN Secretary‑General at the time, António Guterres, acknowledged the concerns but noted that the tradition “has become part of the UN’s identity” and would require careful consideration. <sup>[6]</sup></p> <h2>6. Looking Ahead</h2> <p>As the UN prepares for the 79th session in 2025, Brazil’s role as the first speaker remains a point of pride and a strategic platform. The country is expected to continue using the opening address to highlight pressing global challenges, from climate change to economic inequality. At the same time, the UN is exploring ways to enhance inclusivity, such as rotating the order of speakers for certain thematic sessions.</p> <p>Whether the tradition will evolve remains to be seen. For now, Brazil’s first‑speaker status continues to serve as a reminder of the country’s diplomatic influence and its commitment to fostering dialogue among nations.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>United Nations General Assembly Resolution 1973/1, “Brazil’s privilege to speak first,” 12 September 1973. <a href="https://undocs.org/en/1973/1">undocs.org/en/1973/1</a></li> <li>President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, “Opening Address at the UN General Assembly,” 58th Session (2003). <a href="https://un.org/ga/58/press/lula_speech.pdf">un.org/ga/58/press/lula_speech.pdf</a></li> <li>President Michel Temer, “Opening Address at the UN General Assembly,” 71st Session (2016). <a href="https://un.org/ga/71/press/temer_speech.pdf">un.org/ga/71/press/temer_speech.pdf</a></li> <li>Reuters, “Bolsonaro’s UN speech sparks debate over environmental policies,” 24 September 2019. <a href="https://www.reuters.com/article/us-brazil-un-bolsonaro-idUSKCN1VQ2W6">reuters.com/article/us-brazil-un-bolsonaro-idUSKCN1VQ2W6</a></li> <li>President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, “Opening Address at the UN General Assembly,” 78th Session (2024). <a href="https://un.org/ga/78/press/lula_speech.pdf">un.org/ga/78/press/lula_speech.pdf</a></li> <li>United Nations Press Release, “UN Secretary‑General Calls for Review of Speaking Order,” 2021. <a href="https://un.org/press/en/2021/sg_pressrelease.html">un.org/press/en/2021/sg_pressrelease.html</a></li> </ol>
