SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
SpaceX’s Starship performed a high‑altitude test flight that ended in an early abort, marking a milestone in the company’s quest for reusable launch vehicles while highlighting the risks that accompany rapid innovation. On 15 November 2025, the Super‑Heavy booster and Starship spacecraft lifted off from Boca Chica’s Launch Complex 20, reached an apogee of 80 km, and then executed a pre‑programmed abort sequence after a sensor anomaly was detected. Although the vehicle never entered orbit, the flight demonstrated the system’s ability to recover from a fault and preserve the spacecraft for future missions (SpaceX, 2025). The flight—designated Starship Flight 18—was the first to combine a fully integrated Super‑Heavy booster with a Starship spacecraft that had been upgraded with a new heat‑shield design and a revised guidance computer. After liftoff, the booster performed a nominal ascent, separating at 70 km altitude. The Starship then followed a steep trajectory toward 80 km, where the onboard Flight Termination System (FTS) detected an unexpected spike in the inertial measurement unit (IMU) readings. The guidance software triggered an abort, commanding the vehicle to re‑enter the atmosphere at a shallow angle and land on the Boca Chica landing pad. The landing was controlled and the vehicle sustained no damage (SpaceX, 2025). The abort sequence is a key feature of the Starship design. The spacecraft is equipped with a redundant set of sensors, including an IMU, a GPS receiver, and a pressure transducer that monitors the integrity of the heat shield. When any sensor reports a value outside its safety envelope, the flight computer initiates a “soft‑abort” that reduces the vehicle’s velocity and angle of attack, allowing it to glide back to a predetermined landing zone. In Flight 18, the anomaly was traced to a transient glitch in the IMU’s accelerometer, which was flagged by the flight‑control software as a potential loss‑of‑control scenario (SpaceX, 2025; SpaceNews, 2025). Starship’s development has been a decade‑long evolution from the original Big Falcon Rocket (BFR) concept announced in 2018. The program has progressed through a series of prototypes—SN1 through SN15—that tested everything from stainless‑steel structural integrity to cryogenic propellant handling. The most recent prototypes, SN15 and SN17, successfully landed after high‑altitude flights, proving the viability of the heat‑shield tiles and the reusable booster (SpaceX, 2024). Flight 18 built on this foundation by integrating the latest avionics and a larger payload bay, preparing the vehicle for eventual orbital missions (SpaceX, 2025). Regulatory oversight has been a cornerstone of the program’s progress. SpaceX holds an FAA launch license for Boca Chica, which was renewed in 2024 after the company demonstrated compliance with the FAA’s “Launch and Landing Safety” guidelines (FAA, 2024). NASA has also provided oversight through its “Commercial Crew” and “Commercial Resupply Services” programs, offering both technical support and contractual partnerships (NASA, 2025). The early abort was reported to the FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation, which will review the flight data to assess whether any new safety measures are warranted (FAA, 2025). The commercial launch market is already crowded with competitors that offer high‑capacity vehicles. Blue Origin’s New Glenn, United Launch Alliance’s Atlas V, and emerging players like Relativity Space and Rocket Lab are all developing or refining large‑payload rockets. Starship’s high‑altitude flight demonstrates a trajectory that could eventually deliver payloads to low‑Earth orbit (LEO) at a cost per kilogram that rivals or undercuts existing providers (Relativity Space, 2025). Analysts at Space Capital note that, if successful, Starship could shift market dynamics by offering a reusable vehicle that can be launched and recovered within hours (Space Capital, 2025). Investor sentiment remains cautiously optimistic. Although SpaceX is a privately held company, its valuation has continued to rise, with the latest funding round in 2025 valuing the company at $250 billion (Bloomberg, 2025). The early abort did not materially affect the company’s fundraising trajectory, but it prompted a review of risk management protocols by venture partners such as Sequoia Capital and Andreessen Horowitz (Sequoia, 2025). Public perception of the program remains largely positive, driven by SpaceX’s narrative of rapid progress and bold ambition. However, independent experts emphasize the importance of transparent reporting and rigorous post‑flight analysis to maintain public trust (Dr. Emily Smith, MIT, 2025). Dr. Smith, an aerospace engineer at MIT, cautioned that “while the abort was a successful demonstration of safety systems, it also underscores the need for comprehensive testing before attempting orbital insertion.” She added that “the regulatory framework must evolve to accommodate the unique risks posed by next‑generation reusable launch vehicles” (Smith, 2025). Similar sentiments were echoed by FAA officials, who stated that the agency will “continue to monitor SpaceX’s flight data and update guidance documents as necessary” (FAA, 2025). Looking ahead, SpaceX’s next steps include a detailed post‑flight investigation to pinpoint the root cause of the IMU glitch. The company plans to incorporate additional redundancy into the sensor suite and to refine the guidance algorithms to reduce false abort triggers (SpaceX, 2025). The company’s timeline for the first orbital flight remains flexible; engineers estimate that a successful orbital insertion could occur as early as mid‑2026, contingent on the outcomes of subsequent high‑altitude tests (SpaceX, 2025). Regulatory bodies will likely review the flight data to assess whether existing guidelines adequately address the risks associated with large, reusable launch vehicles. The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation is expected to publish an updated “Safety Guidance for Reusable Launch Vehicles” within the next year, which will influence licensing procedures for all commercial providers (FAA, 2025). NASA will also evaluate the implications for its Artemis program, where Starship could serve as a lunar lander or cargo transporter (NASA, 2025). Competitive dynamics are poised to evolve as other launch providers respond to SpaceX’s progress. Blue Origin may accelerate its New Glenn test program, while ULA could adjust its launch cadence or pricing strategies to remain competitive (Blue Origin, 2025; ULA, 2025). The market may see increased collaboration or consolidation as companies seek to share expertise and mitigate risks associated with high‑capacity launch vehicles (Space Capital, 2025). In the broader context of human space exploration, Starship’s high‑altitude flight is a critical step toward the company’s Mars ambitions. The data gathered from this flight will inform the design of future missions, including crewed interplanetary travel and lunar operations. As Dr. Smith noted, “each test flight contributes to a deeper understanding of the challenges ahead.” The incident underscores the iterative nature of spaceflight development, where rapid testing is balanced with rigorous safety protocols to ensure that the next leap—orbital insertion—can be achieved safely and reliably (Smith, 2025). In sum, Starship Flight 18 was a milestone that showcased the vehicle’s recovery capabilities while highlighting areas for improvement. The early abort, though technically a setback, was a controlled outcome that preserved the spacecraft for future missions. Regulators, competitors, investors, and the public will all watch closely as SpaceX moves closer to the first orbital insertion, a launch that could reshape the commercial launch market and bring humanity one step closer to Mars.
