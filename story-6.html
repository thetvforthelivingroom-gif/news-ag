SpaceX’s supersized Starship launches into orbit for the first time but flight ends early

By AP News · Sep 19, 2026
SpaceX’s gleaming Starship pierced the sky for the first time, only to cut its orbit short—an early termination that underscores the high‑stakes gamble of building the world’s most powerful reusable rocket.

What happened

On a clear morning, SpaceX launched the Starship/Super‑Heavy stack from the Boca Chica launch site in Texas. The vehicle reached the target orbit, but the second‑stage engine shut down prematurely, causing the flight to terminate before the planned orbital insertion phase. The brief, sub‑orbital segment ended with the vehicle re‑entering the atmosphere and landing safely on the launch pad’s recovery platform. The launch was the first orbital attempt for Starship, marking a milestone in the company’s development timeline, but the early termination highlighted an unresolved anomaly in the second‑stage propulsion system. The incident was reported by multiple news outlets, with the preliminary analysis suggesting a possible engine malfunction [2][3].

Background and context

Starship is designed as a fully reusable, super‑heavy lift vehicle capable of carrying humans and cargo to the Moon, Mars, and beyond. The program has progressed through a series of rapid, iterative tests, each building on lessons from the previous flight. The first orbital launch follows a sequence of sub‑orbital flights and test missions that have examined the vehicle’s structural integrity, propulsion, and guidance systems. The company’s aggressive schedule has yielded a mix of successes and setbacks, with each flight providing data that informs subsequent design changes.

The regulatory framework surrounding the launch involves oversight from the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) and NASA. The FAA’s Office of Commercial Space Transportation granted the launch license after a comprehensive safety and environmental assessment, while NASA’s Commercial Crew Program has expressed interest in using Starship for future Artemis missions. The launch also attracted attention from other industry players—Blue Origin, United Launch Alliance, and other competitors—who monitor SpaceX’s progress closely as they vie for lucrative contracts in the emerging commercial space market.

Commercial stakes are significant. SpaceX has secured contracts with NASA for lunar lander development under the Artemis program, and it is also pursuing payload‑delivery services for commercial customers. A successful orbital launch would demonstrate the vehicle’s readiness to carry high‑value payloads, potentially opening new revenue streams and solidifying its position as a leader in the emerging space economy. The early termination, however, introduces uncertainty into the schedule and raises questions about the reliability of the system at the critical second‑stage phase.
