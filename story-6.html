Scientists Detect Radio Signals from an Exoplanet for the First Time in History

By Mastodon #science · Sep 24, 2026
A faint whisper from a distant world may have finally reached Earth. On 12 October 2026, the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) consortium announced that a narrow‑band radio signal, centered at 1.42 GHz, had been detected in the direction of the G‑type star Kepler‑442, about 1,200 light‑years away. The signal, with a bandwidth of less than 1 kHz and a flux density of roughly 0.3 µJy, appeared in three separate observing runs spaced over a month. The SKA team suggested that the emission could be associated with Kepler‑442b, a super‑Earth that orbits within the star’s habitable zone. While the claim is tantalizing, it remains preliminary and awaits independent verification and peer review. The announcement was accompanied by a preprint posted to arXiv (arXiv:2609.0012) and a press release issued by the SKA Science Working Group. No peer‑reviewed article has yet been published, and the data have not been formally vetted by the broader astronomical community. The preprint itself acknowledges that the detection is preliminary and that independent confirmation is essential before any definitive claims can be made.

The SKA and its role in radio SETI

The SKA is a next‑generation radio telescope array that will consist of thousands of dishes and aperture‑array stations spread across South Africa and Australia. Its Phase 1 implementation (SKA‑L1) is currently being commissioned, with the MeerKAT array in South Africa already operational and serving as a precursor. The SKA’s design allows for extremely high sensitivity and wide‑field imaging, making it a powerful tool for searching for faint, narrow‑band signals that could indicate extraterrestrial technology or natural planetary radio emission.

Radio SETI (Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence) has a long history of using large radio dishes to scan the sky for narrow‑band emissions that are unlikely to arise from natural astrophysical processes. The most famous example is the 1974 “Wow!” signal, a 72‑second burst at 1420 MHz recorded by the Big Ear telescope (Gehrels et al. 1977). Since then, systematic surveys such as the Breakthrough Listen project (Ellingsen et al. 2019) and the SETI Institute’s SERENDIP program (Tarter et al. 2001) have scanned thousands of stars, but none have yielded a confirmed extraterrestrial signal.

The SKA’s data pipeline involves real‑time RFI (radio‑frequency interference) excision, calibration, and imaging performed by the Science Data Processor (SDP). Candidate narrow‑band signals are identified using matched‑filter techniques and cross‑checked against known astrophysical sources. The SKA team’s claim relies on this pipeline, but the details of the detection algorithm and the statistical significance of the signal are not fully disclosed in the preprint.

Assessing the evidence

The reported signal’s characteristics—narrow bandwidth, persistence, and spectral shape—are unusual compared to typical astrophysical emissions. However, several natural processes can produce narrow‑band radio emission:

1. **Planetary magnetospheric radio waves**: Planets with strong magnetic fields, such as Jupiter, emit decametric radiation through cyclotron‑maser instability. These emissions are broadband and highly variable, unlike the reported narrow‑band signal (Zarka 1998).

2. **Auroral and lightning phenomena**: Coherent radio bursts from auroras or lightning can be narrowband, but they are usually transient and associated with specific atmospheric conditions (Hess et al. 2015).

3. **Astrophysical masers**: Water and hydroxyl masers in star‑forming regions produce narrowband lines, but their frequencies are well known and differ from the 1.42 GHz hydrogen line (Elitzur 1992).

The 1.42 GHz frequency coincides with the hydrogen line, a natural spectral line that is ubiquitous in the interstellar medium. While this makes the frequency a logical target for SETI, it also increases the likelihood of confusion with natural emission. The SKA team’s assertion that the signal originates from Kepler‑442b is based on the alignment of the emission with the planet’s orbital phase and the absence of known natural sources in that direction. Yet, without a detailed analysis of the spatial resolution and the beam pattern, it is difficult to rule out a background source.

Statistically, the preprint reports a signal‑to‑noise ratio of 5.3 in a single observation, rising to 7.8 when data from multiple epochs are combined. In radio astronomy, a 5σ detection is generally considered robust, but the presence of RFI and the large number of trials performed in a blind search can inflate the false‑alarm probability. The authors acknowledge that further observations are required to confirm the persistence of the signal.

Independent verification and the peer‑review process

The SKA consortium has invited other observatories to test the claim. The Very Large Array (VLA) in the United States and the Five‑hundred‑meter Aperture Spherical Telescope (FAST) in China have scheduled follow‑up observations. The Breakthrough Listen team, which operates a dedicated 1.5‑GHz receiver on the Green Bank Telescope, has also expressed interest in re‑observing the target. The MeerKAT array will provide additional coverage in the southern hemisphere.

Until these independent observations confirm the signal, the claim remains provisional. Peer review will play a crucial role in assessing the methodology, statistical significance, and potential natural explanations. The scientific community will also scrutinize the data processing pipeline, the RFI mitigation strategies, and the calibration procedures. Only after a rigorous review process can the claim be elevated from a preliminary observation to a confirmed discovery.

Scientific and ethical implications

If the signal were confirmed to originate from Kepler‑442b, it would represent the first detection of radio emission from an exoplanet. Such a finding would open a new window onto exoplanetary magnetospheres, atmospheric chemistry, and the potential for technological activity. It would also provide a unique laboratory for studying radio propagation in an exoplanetary environment and for testing models of planetary dynamos.

The possibility of an artificial origin—i.e., deliberate transmission by an extraterrestrial civilization—would have profound implications for humanity. The SETI Institute has long advocated for a cautious, coordinated approach to handling potential extraterrestrial signals (Heller & Sagan 2016). The United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA) has issued guidance on the disclosure and management of such findings, emphasizing transparency, international collaboration, and risk assessment.

The debate over whether to disclose the signal immediately or to withhold it until confirmation echoes the 1974 Wow! episode, where the lack of a formal protocol left the scientific community to navigate the situation on its own. The current claim underscores the need for an internationally agreed framework that balances scientific openness with prudence, ensuring that any potential contact is approached responsibly and transparently.

Public reaction and responsible science communication

The announcement triggered a flurry of activity on social media, with hashtags such as #ExoplanetSignal and #SETI trending worldwide. While the excitement is understandable, many journalists and science communicators cautioned against sensationalism. The American Association for the Advancement of Science (AAAS) issued a statement urging the public to view the claim as preliminary and to await independent verification (AAAS 2026).

The SKA consortium’s decision to make the raw data publicly available is a commendable step toward transparency. Citizen‑science platforms such as Zooniverse have already begun to engage volunteers in inspecting the data for RFI and other artifacts. However, the complexity of radio data analysis means that professional astronomers will likely lead the verification effort.

Next steps and future prospects

The immediate priority is to obtain independent observations with the VLA, FAST, MeerKAT, and other capable radio telescopes. These observations will test the signal’s persistence, spectral shape, and spatial origin. If the signal is confirmed, the next phase will involve detailed modeling of the emission mechanism, including magnetospheric dynamics and potential artificial modulation patterns.

In parallel, the scientific community will refine detection algorithms, improve RFI mitigation, and develop protocols for rapid communication of potential extraterrestrial signals. The SKA project will also continue to expand its capabilities, with Phase 2 slated for the 2030s, offering even greater sensitivity and resolution.

Conclusion

The claim that a radio signal has been detected from the exoplanet Kepler‑442b is intriguing but remains unverified. No peer‑reviewed article has yet confirmed the result, and independent observations are essential before any definitive statements can be made. The scientific and societal implications of such a discovery—whether natural or artificial—are profound, and they underscore the importance of rigorous methodology, transparent communication, and international cooperation in the search for extraterrestrial intelligence.
