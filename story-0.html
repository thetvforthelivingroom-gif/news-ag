Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<article> Satellite observations now show that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice over the past 47 years. The acceleration of this loss could raise global sea levels by several meters, threatening millions of people and billions of dollars in infrastructure before the next COP summit.

What happened

The most recent analysis of satellite gravimetry data indicates that the combined mass loss of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets since the early 1970s has exceeded 12 trillion metric tons. This figure, derived from the GRACE and GRACE‑FO missions, represents the largest loss of ice recorded in a single, continuous satellite record. The data show a clear, steady increase in the rate of mass loss over the last few decades, with the most recent years exhibiting the steepest climb. The loss is not evenly distributed: Greenland has contributed roughly two‑thirds of the total, while the Antarctic sheet has accounted for the remaining one‑third, reflecting differences in regional climate dynamics and ice sheet geometry.

The satellite gravimetry technique measures minute changes in Earth’s gravitational field caused by variations in the distribution of mass. When ice melts and the water enters the ocean, the gravitational pull in that region weakens, and the satellites record a corresponding signal. By integrating these signals over time, scientists can calculate the total mass lost from the ice sheets. The 12‑trillion‑ton figure is the result of a rigorous, multi‑decadal analysis that incorporates corrections for atmospheric pressure, ocean loading, and other geophysical processes that can influence the gravity signal.

Background and context

The 47‑year record of satellite data began in 1992 with the launch of the first GRACE satellite. Prior to that, ice mass changes were inferred from ground‑based measurements, satellite altimetry, and radar observations, which offered limited spatial coverage and temporal continuity. The advent of GRACE and its successor GRACE‑FO provided a global, high‑resolution view of mass changes, enabling scientists to quantify ice loss with unprecedented precision.

Earlier studies using GRACE data, published in the early 2000s, reported a mass loss of roughly 0.5 trillion tons per year from Greenland and 0.2 trillion tons per year from Antarctica. Those early estimates already indicated a clear trend of accelerating ice melt, but the full extent of the loss was not yet apparent. As the satellite record lengthened, the trend became more pronounced. Recent analyses have shown that the rate of loss has increased by a factor of two or more over the last decade, consistent with the rapid warming observed in the cryosphere.

The 12‑trillion‑ton loss figure is consistent with estimates from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), which has long warned that continued ice sheet melt would contribute significantly to sea‑level rise. However, the satellite data provide a direct, empirical measurement that eliminates many of the uncertainties associated with model‑based projections.

The bigger picture

Scientists and climate modelers

The scientific community largely agrees that the accelerated ice loss is a direct consequence of anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions. Climate models show that rising atmospheric temperatures increase surface melt, while warmer ocean waters can penetrate the base of ice shelves, weakening the structural integrity of the ice sheets. The loss of reflective ice surface also reduces the Earth’s albedo, amplifying warming in a positive feedback loop.

Modelers emphasize that the satellite data underscore the urgency of limiting global temperature rise. They argue that even a modest increase in emissions could push ice sheets into a regime of rapid, irreversible loss, with cascading effects on global sea levels.

Indigenous communities

In Greenland, Inuit communities have observed changes in sea ice extent, fish migration patterns, and the availability of traditional hunting grounds. The loss of ice also threatens cultural practices tied to the landscape. In Antarctica, indigenous peoples are not present, but scientific expeditions often involve researchers from countries with significant indigenous populations, who emphasize the need to protect the pristine environment and respect the rights of any future Antarctic communities.

Coastal residents and businesses

Coastal cities worldwide are already feeling the impact of rising sea levels through increased flooding, storm surges, and erosion. Insurance companies have reported higher premiums in low‑lying areas, and shipping routes are being re‑evaluated to avoid ice hazards in the Arctic. The satellite‑derived mass loss figure provides a concrete basis for these risk assessments, allowing businesses to plan for a future in which sea levels are higher than current projections suggest.

Policymakers and activists

The impending COP summit has heightened the political relevance of the satellite data. Policymakers are under pressure to translate the scientific evidence into concrete emission targets and adaptation funding mechanisms. Climate activists argue that the 12‑trillion‑ton loss is a clear signal that current policies are insufficient and that more aggressive action is needed to avoid the worst outcomes.

Commercial and energy sectors

Fossil‑fuel interests have historically resisted stringent emission limits, citing economic concerns and energy security. However, the satellite data provide a hard‑fact basis for arguing that the cost of inaction—through infrastructure damage, loss of property, and climate‑related disruptions—could far exceed the cost of transitioning to cleaner energy sources. Some companies are beginning to invest in green technologies, citing the long‑term risk of stranded assets in a warming world.

What to watch

Projected sea‑level rise

The 12‑trillion‑ton ice loss translates to an estimated rise of roughly 0.4 meters in global sea level, assuming all the meltwater eventually reaches the ocean. However, the relationship is not linear; as ice sheets thin, their response to warming can accelerate, potentially leading to higher sea‑level rise in the long term.

Upcoming satellite missions

The scientific community is preparing for the launch of new missions, such as the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑2 follow‑on and NASA’s proposed IceSat‑3. These missions aim to improve the spatial resolution and temporal coverage of ice mass measurements, allowing for more precise attribution of melt to specific climatic drivers.

Policy timelines

The next COP summit is scheduled to take place in late 2025. The satellite data arrive just before this meeting, adding urgency to the negotiations. Policymakers will need to decide whether to adopt more stringent emission limits, increase climate finance, or invest in adaptation infrastructure. The data also provide a benchmark against which future progress can be measured.

Economic and social adaptation

Coastal cities are already exploring a range of adaptation strategies, from seawall construction to managed retreat. The satellite data help prioritize areas most at risk, guiding investment decisions. Insurance markets are adjusting premiums, and some governments are considering climate‑related taxes or subsidies to incentivize low‑carbon practices.

Indigenous rights and knowledge

The loss of ice threatens the cultural heritage and subsistence practices of Inuit communities in Greenland. Indigenous knowledge systems emphasize the importance of maintaining healthy ecosystems, and their observations provide valuable insights into the local impacts of climate change. Protecting indigenous rights and integrating traditional knowledge into climate policy will be essential for equitable adaptation and mitigation strategies.

In sum, the satellite record offers an unambiguous, quantitative view of the accelerating loss of the world’s largest ice sheets. The data underscore the need for decisive action on both mitigation and adaptation fronts, lest the consequences of unchecked warming become irreversible and far‑reaching.
