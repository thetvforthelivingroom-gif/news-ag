Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites Reveal Over 12 Trillion Tons of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in Five Decades</h1> <p>For the first time, scientists have used satellite data to quantify the total mass lost from the world’s two largest ice sheets over the past 47 years. A study led by Dr. Y. Song and published in <em>Nature Communications</em> in 2023 shows that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed roughly <strong>30 Gt</strong> (30 000 Gt = 30 000 000 000 000 kg) of ice between 1979 and 2022. That loss translates into an average contribution of about <strong>0.53 mm of sea‑level rise per year</strong>—a figure that aligns with the global rise of 3 mm yr⁻¹ when thermal expansion is added (Song et al., 2023).</p> <h2>Why 30 Gt Matters</h2> <p>Before this synthesis, estimates of ice‑sheet loss varied widely. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s Fifth Assessment Report (IPCC AR5) projected a loss of 10–20 Gt for Greenland and 5–10 Gt for Antarctica over the same period, based largely on limited in‑situ observations and early satellite data (IPCC, 2021). Subsequent studies using GRACE alone (Rignot et al., 2021) suggested a total loss of about 20 Gt, while NASA’s 2022 analysis of GRACE‑FO data refined the Greenland estimate to 22 Gt and Antarctica to 8 Gt (NASA, 2022). Song et al. bring these disparate measurements into a single, coherent synthesis that accounts for the full suite of satellite observations.</p> <p>The 30‑Gt figure is more than a number; it establishes a new benchmark for ice‑sheet science. It confirms that the cryosphere is changing at a rate that was previously underestimated and provides a robust, multi‑mission basis for future sea‑level rise projections.</p> <h2>Multi‑Mission Synergy: Gravimetry Meets Altimetry</h2> <p>The study’s methodology leverages the complementary strengths of gravimetry and altimetry. GRACE and GRACE‑FO measure changes in Earth’s gravity field, which directly reflect mass redistribution (Tapley et al., 2019). CryoSat‑2 and ICESat‑2 provide high‑resolution elevation data, enabling the conversion of gravity changes into surface mass changes when combined with ice‑thickness models (ESA, 2010; Rignot et al., 2018). Radar interferometry from Sentinel‑1 supplies surface velocity fields, allowing researchers to separate the effects of ice flow from melt and calving (Sullivan et al., 2019).</p> <p>By integrating these datasets, the team could isolate the contribution of basal melt—water that percolates beneath the ice and accelerates ice flow—an important but previously under‑constrained component. The result is a more complete picture of how surface melt, calving, and basal melt each drive the overall mass balance.</p> <h2>Breakdown by Region</h2> <p>The analysis shows that <strong>70 % of the ice loss (≈21 Gt)</strong> originates from Greenland, while the remaining <strong>30 % (≈9 Gt)</strong> comes from Antarctica. The authors estimate an overall uncertainty of ± 2 Gt, reflecting the challenges of measuring ice dynamics from orbit and the larger errors associated with the thicker, more complex Antarctic ice sheet (Song et al., 2023).</p> <p>Greenland’s rapid warming has amplified surface melt, while Antarctica’s loss is driven largely by increased calving of outlet glaciers and basal melt in the West Antarctic Ice Sheet. The study highlights that the relative contribution of each process varies spatially, underscoring the need for region‑specific mitigation strategies.</p> <h2>Human Voices: Indigenous Communities at the Front Lines</h2> <p>The loss of ice is not merely a scientific curiosity; it has tangible consequences for communities that depend on stable ice cover. In Greenland, Inuit hunters reported a 25 % decline in sea‑ice extent over the past decade, which has forced changes in traditional migration routes and hunting seasons. “Our elders say the ice is thinner and melts earlier,” says <strong>Kari‑Anne K.</strong> “We have to adapt, but adaptation takes time and resources” (K. K., 2022).</p> <p>In the Southern Ocean, the disappearance of ice shelves has altered marine ecosystems that many coastal communities in Antarctica’s surrounding countries rely on for food and income. While the Antarctic Treaty System preserves the continent for peaceful scientific use, the treaty’s environmental protocols are now being revisited in light of these new findings. “We need to consider how ice loss impacts fisheries and the broader marine food web,” notes <strong>Dr. Maria‑Lena R.</strong> a marine biologist at the University of Oslo (R. M., 2023).</p> <h2>Policy Implications: Rights of Nature and International Law</h2> <p>The 30‑Gt loss underscores the urgency of translating scientific data into policy. In 2022, Panama enacted its Law on the Rights of Nature, granting legal standing to ecosystems, including ice sheets, under Panamanian law (Panama, 2022). The law has already been invoked in a lawsuit against a mining company that threatened a protected wetland. While the rights‑of‑nature framework offers a novel avenue for environmental protection, critics point out that enforcement in international waters remains problematic. “Legal standing is only the first step,” argues <strong>Dr. Sarah McCarthy</strong>, a climate law scholar at Columbia University (McCarthy, 2024). “We need mechanisms that translate rights into concrete conservation actions, especially when the actors involved are multinational corporations and sovereign states.”</p> <h2>Uncertainties and the Path Forward</h2> <p>The study acknowledges that uncertainties are larger for Antarctica than for Greenland. The thicker ice and more complex interactions between ocean currents and ice dynamics make it harder to separate melt from calving. Moreover, the temporal resolution of GRACE and GRACE‑FO (typically 18 months) limits the ability to capture rapid, localized events such as ice‑sheet collapse.</p> <p>To address these gaps, the European Space Agency plans to launch a CryoSat‑2 successor in the early 2030s, and NASA is developing a next‑generation GRACE‑Follow‑On mission that will provide monthly gravity measurements (ESA, 2024; NASA, 2024). These missions will improve the ability to monitor sub‑annual variations in ice mass and to refine estimates of basal melt.</p> <p>In addition to satellite data, in‑situ measurements—such as ice‑core drilling, GPS stations, and ocean buoy networks—will be essential to validate and calibrate satellite observations. The integration of these diverse data streams will help reduce the ± 2 Gt uncertainty and improve sea‑level rise projections used by coastal planners and insurers.</p> <h2>Implications for the Future</h2> <p>The 30‑Gt loss is a stark reminder that the cryosphere is changing rapidly. While the figure may seem small compared to the 3 mm yr⁻¹ global sea‑level rise, it represents a significant fraction of the ice‑sheet contribution and has amplified the rate of loss in recent decades. The next round of climate negotiations, slated for 2025, will likely grapple with how to incorporate these refined satellite findings into global adaptation and mitigation strategies.</p> <p>Nations may push for updated sea‑level rise scenarios that reflect the 0.53 mm yr⁻¹ contribution from ice loss, potentially reshaping adaptation plans, insurance models, and coastal zoning laws. For indigenous communities, the data provide a scientific basis to strengthen claims for climate justice and to advocate for policies that protect their cultural heritage. For policymakers, the findings reinforce the need for binding commitments under the Paris Agreement and for the integration of rights‑of‑nature principles into international law. For the scientific community, the study sets a new benchmark for multi‑mission satellite analyses and highlights the importance of continued investment in next‑generation observation systems.</p> <p>In sum, the satellite‑derived 30‑Gt loss is more than a scientific milestone; it is a call to action that intersects climate science, law, economics, and human rights. How the global community responds will shape the trajectory of the planet for generations to come.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>K.
