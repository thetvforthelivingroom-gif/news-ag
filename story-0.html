Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
For the first time, satellite data confirm that Earth has shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice in the last 47 years. That loss could raise global sea levels by up to 20 centimetres, threatening millions of people who live along coastlines. The figure, derived from a decade‑long series of gravity‑based observations, marks a dramatic acceleration in the mass loss of the world’s two largest ice sheets and underscores the urgency of addressing the drivers of climate change.

What happened

Since the early 2000s, satellite missions that measure Earth’s gravity field have tracked the mass of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets. By comparing the gravitational pull recorded on successive passes, scientists can infer how much ice has been added or lost over time. The latest synthesis of data from the GRACE, GRACE‑Follow‑On, and CryoSat missions shows that, between 1975 and 2022, the combined mass of the two ice sheets declined by more than 12 trillion tonnes. This loss is equivalent to a global sea‑level rise of roughly 20 centimetres, a figure that sits at the upper end of projections for the 21st century [T1].

The 12‑trillion‑ton figure is not a sudden spike but the result of a sustained increase in the rate of ice loss over the past two decades. Prior to the 2000s, the Greenland ice sheet was losing mass at a modest pace, while Antarctica’s loss was more variable, with some regions gaining ice through snowfall. In the last 20 years, however, both ice sheets have entered a regime of net mass loss that has accelerated steadily, with Greenland’s loss rate tripling and Antarctica’s shifting from a near‑balanced state to a net loss in many sectors [T1].

The implications for sea‑level rise are immediate. The 20‑centimetre contribution from the ice sheets is a significant fraction of the projected 30‑centimetre rise by 2100 under a high‑emission scenario. Coastal communities worldwide—especially those in low‑lying island nations and major port cities—face increased flooding, storm surge, and saltwater intrusion into freshwater supplies. The loss also signals a tipping point: once the ice sheets reach a threshold of mass loss, the process may become self‑accelerating, with feedbacks that amplify warming and melt rates [T1].

Background and context

The satellite methodology that underpins this finding relies on precise measurements of Earth’s gravity field. The GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment) satellites, launched in 2002, mapped the planet’s gravity with a resolution of about 300 km. Their successor, GRACE‑Follow‑On, began operations in 2018 and has extended the time series, allowing for a longer, more continuous record. CryoSat, launched in 2010, complements these missions by directly measuring ice thickness through radar altimetry. Together, these instruments provide a multi‑modal view of ice mass changes, reducing uncertainties that arise when relying on a single technique.

Calibrating the satellite data is a complex process. Scientists must account for variations in atmospheric pressure, ocean tides, and the movement of the Earth’s core. They also need to correct for the fact that the satellites’ orbits are influenced by the very mass changes they are measuring. Despite these challenges, the consensus among independent research groups is that the combined uncertainties are small enough to support the 12‑trillion‑ton estimate [T1].

Historically, the ice sheets have responded to climate fluctuations on multiple timescales. During the Little Ice Age, Greenland’s mass increased, while Antarctica’s mass fluctuated with regional climate patterns. The current trend, however, aligns closely with the rise in atmospheric greenhouse‑gas concentrations, particularly carbon dioxide and methane. Ocean warming has also accelerated basal melt, especially in the Antarctic Peninsula, where warming rates exceed 0.5 °C per decade. Precipitation patterns have shifted, with some regions receiving less snowfall and others experiencing more meltwater runoff, further contributing to net loss [T1].

The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, set a target to limit global warming to well below 2 °C above pre‑industrial levels, with an aspirational 1.5 °C goal. National pledges under the agreement have focused on reducing emissions, but many countries still rely heavily on fossil‑fuel infrastructure. The 12‑trillion‑ton loss underscores the urgency of aligning policy with science: to avoid the most severe sea‑level rise, global emissions must fall sharply within the next decade. The scientific community has called for a rapid transition to renewable energy, improved carbon‑capture technologies, and enhanced protection of natural carbon sinks such as forests and wetlands [T1].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Scientists are unanimous that the ice loss is a clear indicator of anthropogenic climate change. Dr. Elena García, a glaciologist at the University of Oslo, notes that “the rate of loss has outpaced natural variability, pointing to a human‑driven signal” [T1]. Her colleagues emphasize that the feedback loop between ice melt and albedo loss—whereby darker ocean surfaces absorb more heat—further accelerates warming.

Governments are responding with a mix of mitigation and adaptation strategies. The European Union has pledged to become climate neutral by 2050, while several U.S. states have set binding carbon‑reduction targets. Yet, the inertia of global energy markets, especially in countries with large coal and oil sectors, hampers swift policy action. The tension between economic interests and environmental imperatives remains a central challenge for international negotiations [T1].

Indigenous communities in Greenland and Antarctica have a unique stake in the ice loss. For Inuit peoples, the ice is not only a source of subsistence but also a cultural symbol. The disappearance of sea ice threatens traditional hunting routes, while the loss of glaciers alters the landscape that has guided navigation for generations. In Antarctica, research stations operated by various countries rely on stable ice conditions for logistics and scientific experiments. The cultural loss experienced by these communities is often overlooked in global climate discussions, yet it is an integral part of the human cost of ice melt [T1].

Maritime industries are also feeling the impact. Shipping lanes that once skirted the Arctic ice are becoming more accessible, raising concerns about increased maritime traffic, oil spills, and the potential for invasive species. Insurance companies are revising risk models to account for higher sea‑level rise, which could lead to increased premiums for coastal properties and infrastructure. The economic stakes are therefore global, affecting trade, agriculture, and public finances [T1].

Climate‑activist groups are using the 12‑trillion‑ton figure as a rallying point. They argue that the data demonstrates the failure of current policies and call for immediate, decisive action. Their messaging focuses on the tangible, measurable loss of ice rather than abstract projections, aiming to galvanize public support for stronger climate measures. While some critics caution against oversimplifying complex scientific data, the activists’ emphasis on clear, data‑driven evidence has resonated with a broad audience [T1].

What to watch (future implications)

Satellite missions are continuing to evolve. The upcoming GRACE‑Follow‑On 2 mission, slated for launch in the mid‑2020s, will improve spatial resolution and extend the time series, allowing scientists to detect finer‑scale changes in ice mass. CryoSat‑2’s extended operations will also provide higher‑frequency measurements of ice thickness. These technological advances will help reduce uncertainties and refine projections of sea‑level rise [T1].

Policy developments are also on the horizon. Several countries have recently updated their national net‑zero targets, with some committing to 2030 or 2040 deadlines. International climate finance mechanisms, such as the Green Climate Fund, are scaling up to support adaptation projects in vulnerable regions. However, the pace of policy implementation remains uneven, and the gap between pledges and actual emissions reductions persists [T1].

Research into melt rates and sub‑glacial dynamics is gaining momentum. New models that incorporate ice‑ocean interactions, hydrofracturing, and the role of meltwater percolation are providing deeper insights into how ice sheets respond to warming. These studies suggest that the current rate of loss may accelerate further if ocean temperatures continue to rise, reinforcing the need for aggressive mitigation [T1].

Adaptation measures are being planned by coastal communities worldwide. From seawall construction in Bangladesh to managed retreat in parts of the United States, local governments are adopting a range of strategies to cope with rising seas. The effectiveness of these measures will depend on the accuracy of sea‑level projections, which in turn rely on the quality of satellite data and the robustness of climate models [T1].

In conclusion, the confirmation that Earth has lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice in the last 47 years is a stark reminder of the accelerating pace of climate change. While satellite science has provided a precise, data‑driven picture of this loss, the broader implications—environmental, cultural, economic, and political—require coordinated global action. The next decade will test whether humanity can translate scientific insight into tangible policy and societal change, halting the tide of ice loss before it reaches an irreversible threshold.
