Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
Satellite measurements now confirm that Greenland and Antarctica have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice in the last 47 years—an amount that could raise global sea levels by roughly 3–4 millimetres, putting millions of coastal residents and billions of dollars of infrastructure at risk.[T1]

What happened

From 1979 to 2026, a fleet of Earth‑observation satellites—GRACE, GRACE‑FO, CryoSat‑2, and others—has monitored subtle changes in the planet’s gravity field and the height of ice surfaces. By combining these data, scientists have quantified a loss of over 12 trillion metric tonnes of ice from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets. This loss averages about 0.25 trillion tonnes per year, far exceeding the roughly 0.04 trillion‑tonne annual loss recorded in the 1970s and 1980s. The cumulative melt translates to a sea‑level rise of about 3.6 millimetres, a figure that, while small in isolation, adds to other contributors such as thermal expansion and melt from glaciers worldwide.[T1]

The satellites measure ice loss in two complementary ways. Gravimetry, performed by the GRACE and GRACE‑FO missions, detects changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution. When ice melts, the mass shift reduces the local gravitational pull, a change that can be measured. Altimetry, carried out by CryoSat‑2 and other missions, records the elevation of the ice surface; a drop in altitude indicates thinning or retreat. Cross‑validation between these techniques, together with ground‑based mass‑balance studies, strengthens confidence in the results.[T1]

Background and context

For millennia, the ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica maintained a delicate balance between snowfall and melt or calving. In recent decades, global temperatures have risen by about 1.1 °C since the pre‑industrial era, amplifying surface melt, warming ocean currents, and altering atmospheric circulation. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) projects that, by 2100, Greenland could lose up to 50 % of its mass, while Antarctica may contribute an additional 0.5–1.0 metre to sea‑level rise under high‑emission scenarios. The 12‑trillion‑tonne figure falls within the lower end of these projections but underscores that the loss is already underway and measurable.[T1]

Uncertainties in the satellite data arise from orbital drift, instrument calibration drift, and atmospheric interference—particularly from water vapour and ionospheric disturbances. Scientists mitigate these issues with rigorous statistical corrections and by comparing satellite outputs with independent measurements such as GPS‑based ice‑sheet elevation changes and in‑situ mass‑balance records. Even with these adjustments, the confidence interval for the total loss remains within a few percent, reflecting the robustness of the overall finding.[T1]

The bigger picture

Climate scientists

Researchers emphasize that the data provide a clear, objective record of ice loss, reinforcing the urgency of limiting global warming. “The satellites give us a direct, unbiased window into the ice sheets’ health,” says one climatologist, noting that peer‑reviewed analyses are underway to publish the full dataset in a high‑impact journal. The scientific community calls for continued investment in satellite missions and the development of next‑generation gravimetry platforms to refine temporal resolution and reduce uncertainties.[T1]

Policymakers

At the policy level, the quantified loss informs national adaptation plans and international negotiations. Governments are increasingly integrating satellite‑derived sea‑level rise projections into coastal zoning laws, infrastructure resilience budgets, and disaster‑risk reduction strategies. The upcoming UN Climate Conference (COP) will likely feature discussions on translating these scientific findings into concrete commitments, such as enhanced funding for low‑lying island nations and the scaling of early warning systems.[T1]

Indigenous communities

For Indigenous peoples in Greenland, the Arctic, and the Antarctic Peninsula, the ice loss carries cultural and subsistence implications. Traditional hunting and fishing practices depend on predictable ice conditions; the retreat of sea ice disrupts migratory patterns of seals and polar bears, jeopardizing food security. Community leaders are advocating for the inclusion of Indigenous knowledge in monitoring programs and for policies that protect their livelihoods while addressing climate change.[T1]

Industry

The opening of Arctic shipping lanes, once blocked by thick sea ice, presents economic opportunities for shipping companies, energy firms, and insurers. However, the same melt also heightens the risk of maritime accidents, oil spills, and habitat loss. Insurance models are being recalibrated to account for the increased probability of extreme weather events and rising sea levels, influencing premiums and coverage policies across the globe.[T1]

What to watch

Upcoming UN Climate Conference (COP)

The COP will serve as a platform for nations to reassess their emissions targets in light of the latest ice‑sheet data. Expect proposals for more aggressive carbon‑price mechanisms, expanded financial support for adaptation, and strengthened commitments to the Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C goal. The 12‑trillion‑tonne loss will likely be cited as evidence that the current trajectory is unsustainable and that policy action must accelerate.[T1]

New GRACE‑FO data releases

GRACE‑FO continues to collect high‑precision gravimetric data, with planned updates every six months. Analysts anticipate that forthcoming datasets will refine the temporal resolution of ice‑sheet mass changes, allowing scientists to distinguish between interannual variability and long‑term trends. These updates will also improve the accuracy of sea‑level rise projections used by coastal planners and insurers.[T1]

Next‑generation satellite missions

Future missions, such as the planned GRACE‑Next and the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑3, aim to enhance sensitivity and reduce noise in gravity and altimetry measurements. By extending the observational record beyond 2026, these platforms will enable a more comprehensive assessment of ice‑sheet dynamics and their interaction with oceanic and atmospheric systems.[T1]

Policy debates on Arctic shipping and insurance

As the Arctic becomes more navigable, governments and industry stakeholders will negotiate the balance between economic benefits and environmental protection. Regulatory frameworks may evolve to impose stricter emissions standards on vessels, mandate ice‑breaking support, and require comprehensive environmental impact assessments. Concurrently, insurance companies will refine risk models to incorporate the new sea‑level rise data, potentially reshaping investment strategies in coastal regions.[T1]

In sum, the satellite‑derived evidence of a 12‑trillion‑tonne ice loss over nearly five decades provides a stark, quantifiable indicator of climate change’s tangible impacts. It underscores the need for coordinated scientific, policy, and community responses as the world navigates the unfolding challenges of a warming planet.
