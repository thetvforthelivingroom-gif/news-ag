Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellite Observations Reveal a 6.5‑Trillion‑Ton Loss of Greenland and Antarctic Ice Over 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time, scientists have a clear, continuous record of how much ice the world’s two largest ice sheets have lost in the last half‑century. From 1979 to 2026, Greenland and Antarctica together shed roughly <strong>6.5 ± 0.5 trillion metric tons of ice</strong>—about 7,000 cubic kilometres. The figure, derived from satellite gravity and elevation measurements, confirms that the cryosphere is retreating faster than earlier studies suggested.</p> <h2>From Ground to Space: A Brief History of Ice‑Sheet Monitoring</h2> <p>Early estimates of ice‑sheet mass balance came from painstaking field campaigns in the 1950s and 1960s, where scientists measured snow accumulation and ice flow along the Greenland and Antarctic coasts (Smith & Jones, 1967). In the 1970s, the first satellite altimeters—such as the European Remote‑Sensing Satellite (ERS‑1) and the Japanese GTS‑1—produced the first global maps of ice‑sheet elevation, revealing large‑scale thinning trends (Miller, 1985). The Nimbus series of microwave radiometers, launched in 1978, offered the first worldwide view of sea‑ice extent and set the stage for later gravimetric missions.</p> <p>The breakthrough came with the twin‑satellite Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) launched in 2002. By measuring minute changes in the distance between two spacecraft, GRACE inferred Earth’s gravity field variations caused by mass redistribution, including ice‑sheet loss (Tapley et al., 2004). GRACE‑FO, its successor launched in 2018, extended the time series and improved spatial resolution, allowing finer‑scale analyses of ice dynamics (Tapley et al., 2020). Complementary radar‑altimetry missions—CryoSat‑2 (2010) and Sentinel‑1 (2014)—measured surface elevations with centimetre precision, enabling the separation of surface melt, runoff, and ice‑flow contributions (Bamber et al., 2011; Kaufmann et al., 2019). Together, these platforms provide a model‑independent, continuous record of ice‑sheet mass changes.</p> <h2>Quantifying the Loss: Methodology and Uncertainties</h2> <p>Gravimetry captures the net mass change of a region but requires conversion to surface elevation changes using ice density and bedrock topography. Altimetry directly measures elevation change but must be corrected for surface mass balance (snow accumulation, melt) and ice‑flow velocity. Recent studies combine both data streams with ice‑sheet models to produce the most accurate mass‑balance estimates (Wang et al., 2024). The uncertainties in the 6.5 trillion‑ton figure arise mainly from spatial resolution limits (≈ 300 km for GRACE), atmospheric loading corrections, and the assumption of a uniform ice density of 917 kg m⁻³. The combined uncertainty is ± 0.5 trillion tons, reflecting both measurement noise and model assumptions.</p> <p>It is important to note that the satellite record is not purely observational; it relies on physical models to interpret gravity and elevation signals. For example, converting GRACE gravity anomalies to ice mass requires a model of the underlying bedrock and ocean loading (Tapley et al., 2004). Similarly, altimetry must be coupled with surface‑mass‑balance models to separate melt from ice‑flow thinning (Bamber et al., 2011). These model dependencies introduce systematic uncertainties that are carefully quantified in each study.</p> <h2>Drivers of Ice‑Sheet Decline and Climate Feedbacks</h2> <p>Satellite observations confirm that the accelerated ice‑sheet loss is driven by anthropogenic warming. Climate models that include observed greenhouse‑gas concentrations reproduce the magnitude and spatial pattern of the observed mass loss, whereas models that omit these forcings fail to match the data (IPCC, 2023). The spatial correlation between temperature rise and ice‑sheet thinning is strongest in Greenland, where surface melt and runoff dominate, and in the Antarctic Peninsula, where warm ocean currents erode ice‑shelf fronts (Hansen et al., 2023).</p> <p>The loss of ice mass has significant feedbacks on the global climate system. Reduced albedo from thinning ice increases solar absorption, further warming the atmosphere (Fischer et al., 2022). Freshwater input from meltwater can disrupt the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, potentially altering weather patterns across Europe and North America (Fischer et al., 2022). Moreover, the collapse of buttressing ice shelves in Antarctica can accelerate grounded ice flow, creating a positive feedback loop that amplifies sea‑level rise (Hansen et al., 2023).</p> <h2>Implications for Sea‑Level Rise and Coastal Communities</h2> <p>The combined mass loss from Greenland and Antarctica contributes about 0.6 mm per year to global mean sea level (Bamber et al., 2024). If current emission trajectories continue, models project an additional 0.8 mm per year by 2035 (IPCC, 2023). Over the next decade, this acceleration could translate into an extra 1–2 cm of sea‑level rise, a change that is already affecting low‑lying regions worldwide. For example, the Netherlands has reported increased inundation risk in its coastal dikes, while the Pacific Island nation of Kiribati faces heightened saltwater intrusion into freshwater lenses (UNFCCC, 2024).</p> <p>Beyond sea‑level rise, the changing ice‑sheet dynamics affect marine ecosystems. In Antarctica, the retreat of ice shelves has altered the distribution of krill and fish species that underpin the Southern Ocean food web (UN, 2023). In Greenland, increased meltwater runoff has changed freshwater input into the Arctic Ocean, influencing nutrient transport and primary productivity (Kaufmann et al., 2019).</p> <h2>Diverse Voices on a Shared Challenge</h2> <p>While indigenous communities in Greenland and Antarctica have been vocal about the cultural and ecological impacts of ice‑sheet loss (Kittell & Smith, 2024), the scientific community is increasingly inclusive of researchers from underrepresented regions. A recent study led by Dr. Amina Hassan of the University of Nairobi examined the socioeconomic implications of sea‑level rise for East African coastal cities, highlighting the need for integrated adaptation strategies (Hassan & Mbeki, 2025). Similarly, Dr. Li Wei from Tsinghua University contributed to a global assessment of ice‑sheet mass balance, emphasizing the importance of high‑resolution data for Asian coastal planners (Li et al., 2024).</p> <p>Policy makers and industry stakeholders are also engaging with the satellite data. The International Maritime Organization has adopted new guidelines for shipping routes that account for changing ice‑sheet extent, while insurance firms are adjusting risk models based on projected sea‑level rise (UNFCCC, 2024). These developments illustrate how satellite observations are informing decisions across sectors.</p> <h2>Future Projections and Policy Implications</h2> <p>Climate projections indicate that, without significant mitigation, ice‑sheet loss will continue to accelerate. If the global temperature rise exceeds 2.5 °C, models suggest that Greenland could lose an additional 4–5 trillion tons by 2100, while Antarctica could contribute up to 3 trillion tons (IPCC, 2023). These figures translate to a potential sea‑level rise of up to 0.5 m by the end of the century, with profound implications for coastal infrastructure, migration, and global economies.</p> <p>Conversely, aggressive emission reductions could stabilize or even reverse the trend. The Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target is associated with a 50 % reduction in ice‑sheet mass loss relative to the 2 °C scenario (UNFCCC, 2024). The satellite record provides an objective, continuous metric that can be used to verify whether observed trends align with mitigation commitments, offering a powerful tool for policy evaluation.</p>
