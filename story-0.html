Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>For the first time, satellite observations have quantified the loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years. The finding, published in <em>Nature Geoscience</em> by Smith, Jones, and colleagues (2023), underscores how rapidly the planet’s largest ice reservoirs are shrinking and highlights the urgency of understanding the consequences for sea‑level rise.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>Satellite missions launched over the past five decades have been essential for tracking the mass balance of the world’s ice sheets. By measuring changes in the Earth’s gravity field and the elevation of ice surfaces, scientists can infer how much ice is being lost or gained. The latest analysis, which draws on data from 1975 to 2022, shows that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed an estimated 12 trillion tons of ice. If all of that ice melted, it would raise global sea level by roughly 3–4 centimetres (Smith et al., 2023). The rate of loss has accelerated over the past two decades, shifting from a relatively steady state to a rapid decline.</p> <p>The authors derived the 12‑trillion‑ton figure using a combination of satellite gravimetry (GRACE and GRACE‑FO), radar altimetry (ICESat‑2 and Sentinel‑1), and satellite‑borne laser measurements (CryoSat‑2). These instruments provide a comprehensive view of ice dynamics, revealing both surface melt and basal melt driven by ocean heat. The data also show that the rate of ice loss has increased by roughly 50 % in the last ten years, a trend that aligns with rising global temperatures and changing atmospheric circulation patterns (Wang et al., 2022).</p> <h2>Background and context</h2> <p>Greenland and Antarctica contain about 90 % of the planet’s ice and are the largest reservoirs of freshwater on Earth. Their stability is crucial for global sea‑level rise, climate regulation, and ocean circulation. Historically, the ice sheets have responded to climatic fluctuations on timescales ranging from decades to millennia. The recent acceleration in ice loss, however, is unprecedented in the Holocene epoch.</p> <p>Satellite missions such as NASA’s Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑Follow‑On, ESA’s CryoSat‑2, and JAXA’s Sentinel‑1 have provided the high‑resolution data needed to quantify ice mass changes. These observations have revealed that both ice sheets are losing mass at a rate that exceeds the rate of ice accumulation in many regions. In Greenland, surface melt driven by warmer air temperatures and increased precipitation in the form of rain has become a dominant loss mechanism. In Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet is particularly vulnerable to oceanic warming that melts ice from below.</p> <p>The 12‑trillion‑ton figure fits into a broader pattern of climate‑driven change. It is consistent with other satellite‑based estimates that suggest global sea level has risen by about 20 cm since the late 19th century, with the majority of that rise attributable to ice melt (IPCC, 2021). Climate model projections predict continued ice loss under high‑emission scenarios, potentially leading to sea‑level rise of up to a meter by the end of the century (IPCC, 2023).</p> <h2>The bigger picture</h2> <p>While the data paint a stark picture of ice loss, scientists emphasize that the underlying processes are complex and regionally variable. In Greenland, the rate of surface melt has increased due to a combination of warmer temperatures, changes in cloud cover, and the presence of dust and black‑carbon aerosols that reduce albedo. Meltwater can also lubricate the ice base, accelerating glacier flow toward the ocean.</p> <p>In Antarctica, the situation is more nuanced. The East Antarctic Ice Sheet remains relatively stable, but the West Antarctic Ice Sheet is experiencing accelerated thinning and retreat. Oceanic heat erodes ice shelves that act as buttresses, allowing glaciers to accelerate. The loss of ice shelves can lead to a rapid increase in ice discharge, amplifying sea‑level rise (Wang et al., 2022).</p> <p>Policy makers and international bodies have taken note of these findings. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has highlighted the importance of monitoring ice sheets and incorporating their dynamics into sea‑level projections (IPCC, 2023). The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) has urged countries to reduce greenhouse‑gas emissions to limit further warming and mitigate ice loss (UNFCCC, 2022). Coastal communities worldwide are grappling with the practical implications of rising seas, from increased flooding risk to the potential displacement of millions of people.</p> <p>The scientific community also recognizes the need for improved data integration. Combining satellite observations with ground‑based measurements, such as GPS stations and ice‑core drilling, can refine estimates of ice mass balance and improve model accuracy. International collaborations, such as the Global Climate Observing System (GCOS), aim to standardise data collection and sharing to enhance our understanding of ice sheet dynamics (GCOS, 2021).</p> <h2>What to watch</h2> <p>Looking ahead, the trajectory of ice loss will hinge on several interrelated factors. First, the pace of global warming will determine how quickly temperatures rise and how much additional melt is induced. Second, the response of the ice sheets to increased meltwater runoff and basal lubrication will influence the rate of glacier acceleration. Third, ocean temperature changes will dictate the extent of basal melting in Antarctica.</p> <p>If the current trend continues, the 12‑trillion‑ton loss could translate into a sea‑level rise of several metres over the next millennium, but not within a single century. Even a modest 3–4 cm rise from the 12‑trillion‑ton loss would have significant impacts on low‑lying regions, island nations, and coastal megacities. The potential for abrupt changes, such as the collapse of ice shelves, underscores the need for continued monitoring and rapid response planning (IPCC, 2023).</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The story of Greenland and Antarctica’s ice sheets reminds us that the planet’s cryosphere is not a static backdrop but a dynamic system that responds sensitively to human‑induced climate change. Satellite observations have turned a speculative concern into a measurable reality, revealing a 12‑trillion‑ton loss that will shape sea‑level rise for centuries to come. By integrating satellite data with ground‑based measurements, refining climate models, and enacting robust mitigation policies, the international community can better anticipate and adapt to the evolving challenges posed by a warming world. The quiet, data‑driven moment captured by satellites is a clarion call: the ice is melting, and the consequences are already unfolding in the oceans and on the land.</p>
