Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Ice sheets have lost about 4½ trillion tonnes in the last 47 years, a loss that matches the rise in global sea level</h1> <p><em>By [Author]</em></p> <p>A comprehensive synthesis of satellite observations that covers almost five decades shows that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed roughly 4.5 trillion tonnes of ice since 1974. The figure is consistent with independent estimates of global sea‑level rise and with earlier satellite studies, but it provides the most complete, high‑resolution picture yet of how the cryosphere has changed over time.<sup>¹</sup></p> <p>The study brings together data from a suite of space‑based platforms—Landsat, CryoSat, Sentinel‑1, and the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO—alongside ground‑based mass‑balance measurements and airborne radar surveys. By harmonising these diverse data streams, the team corrected for systematic biases and extended the time series to the present, yielding a robust estimate of ice‑mass loss that is both spatially detailed and temporally continuous.<sup>²</sup></p> <h2>A history of satellite monitoring</h2> <p>The first systematic satellite images of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets came from the Landsat series in the early 1970s. These optical images allowed scientists to track changes in surface area and albedo. In the 1990s, the European Space Agency’s Envisat and the U.S. GRACE missions added gravimetric data, enabling researchers to infer changes in ice mass by measuring subtle variations in Earth’s gravity field.<sup>³</sup> Subsequent missions—such as CryoSat‑B, Sentinel‑1, and GRACE‑FO—improved spatial resolution and extended the time series. Earlier studies were limited by shorter data records or by the need to interpolate between disparate datasets. The current synthesis, by contrast, integrates all available observations into a single, continuous record that covers the full 47‑year period.<sup>⁴</sup></p> <h2>Regional patterns of loss</h2> <p>The loss is not uniform. Greenland’s western and central sectors have experienced the most rapid mass loss, with an average rate of 0.45 km³ yr⁻¹ in the 2010s—a sharp acceleration from the 2000s. The loss is driven by increased surface melt, which weakens the ice sheet’s structural integrity, and by the collapse of buttressing ice shelves that once slowed the flow of inland ice. In Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS) has thinned and retreated, contributing about 1.2 trillion tonnes of loss, while the East Antarctic Ice Sheet (EAIS) remains largely stable, with some localized areas of modest ice gain due to increased snowfall.<sup>¹⁵</sup></p> <h2>Drivers of change</h2> <p>Atmospheric warming has increased surface melt across both ice sheets, while ocean warming has eroded ice shelves from below, reducing their buttressing effect. The albedo feedback—where melting ice exposes darker ocean or land surfaces that absorb more solar radiation—further amplifies warming and melt. Episodic climatic events such as El Niño can trigger temporary spikes in melt and ice loss, adding to the long‑term trend.<sup>¹⁶</sup> The relative contributions of atmospheric versus oceanic warming remain uncertain, and internal climate variability can modulate the rate of ice loss on decadal timescales.<sup>¹⁷</sup></p> <h2>Voices from the front lines</h2> <p>In Greenland, Inuit communities such as those in Qaanaaq and Ilulissat have observed the retreat of sea ice and the thinning of glaciers that once fed their fishing and hunting economies. “We see the ice receding faster than any of us can remember,” says Nuna K. of the Inuit Circumpolar Council. “Our traditional knowledge tells us that the ice is a living thing, and it is dying.”<sup>¹⁸</sup></p> <p>Antarctica has no indigenous population, but it hosts a network of international research stations that monitor ice dynamics and climate. “The data from the South Pole and McMurdo Station confirm what the satellite observations show—ice loss is real and accelerating,” notes Dr. Elena Petrov, a glaciologist at the Russian Antarctic Research Institute.<sup>¹⁹</sup></p> <h2>Policy implications</h2> <p>The 4.5‑trillion‑ton figure will be a key data point at the 2025 Conference of the Parties (COP) and the 2027 United Nations Climate Conference. Negotiators can use the evidence to push for stronger emission‑reduction targets, higher financial commitments for adaptation, and the inclusion of cryospheric metrics in national climate reports. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) already recommends that national reports incorporate satellite‑derived ice‑mass loss as a standard indicator, and the new synthesis provides the most robust dataset to support that recommendation.<sup>²⁰</sup></p> <p>In addition to mitigation, the findings underscore the need for adaptation measures in vulnerable coastal and island communities. The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) has called for a “Cryosphere Adaptation Fund” that would channel resources to communities most at risk of sea‑level rise. The 4.5‑trillion‑ton figure provides a concrete, science‑based baseline against which to measure the effectiveness of such initiatives.<sup>²¹</sup></p> <h2>Investing in the future of ice‑sheet monitoring</h2> <p>The study highlights the importance of continued investment in satellite missions that can monitor the cryosphere with higher spatial and temporal resolution. Upcoming missions such as the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑3 and NASA’s IceSat‑3 will provide critical data to refine estimates of ice‑mass loss and to improve projections of sea‑level rise. Ground‑truth validation—through in‑situ GPS stations, mass‑balance measurements, and airborne radar surveys—remains essential to ensure the accuracy of satellite observations.<sup>²²</sup></p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The new synthesis confirms that Greenland and Antarctica have lost about 4.5 trillion tonnes of ice over the past 47 years, a loss that aligns with the observed rise in global sea level. The findings reinforce the urgency of reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions and of investing in adaptation strategies for vulnerable communities. They also demonstrate the power of satellite science to track the Earth’s most remote and dynamic systems, providing the evidence needed to inform policy and to protect the planet’s future.</p> <hr> <h3>References</h3> <ol> <li>Holland, R., et al.
