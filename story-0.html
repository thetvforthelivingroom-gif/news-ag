Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<article> **Satellites reveal Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years** The combined mass loss of Greenland and Antarctica has reached a staggering 12 trillion tons (12 × 10¹² t) between 1973 and 2020, according to the most comprehensive satellite record to date. This loss corresponds to a global mean sea‑level rise of roughly 3.5 centimetres (≈ 35 mm) and represents a dramatic acceleration of ice‑sheet retreat in the last half‑century. The figure is derived from a suite of independent techniques—optical imaging, radar interferometry, gravimetry, and laser altimetry—each calibrated against ground‑based observations, and carries an uncertainty of ± 5 % (≈ ± 0.6 trillion t) [1,2]. ---

The satellite record: From optical images to gravity waves

Early optical and radar observations

The Landsat series, launched in 1972, provided the first systematic optical view of the polar regions. Although its 30‑m resolution limited precise mass estimates, it revealed the retreat of outlet glaciers and the expansion of meltwater ponds, establishing a baseline for later, higher‑resolution missions [3]. In the 1990s, ESA’s Envisat and the U.S. National Reconnaissance Office’s radar missions (e.g., RADARSAT‑1) added high‑frequency radar backscatter data that helped track surface roughness changes associated with melt and refreezing [4].

Gravimetry: GRACE and GRACE‑FO

The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE), launched in 2002, measured spatial variations in Earth’s gravity field with centimetre‑level accuracy. Declines in the gravity signal over Greenland and Antarctica directly indicate mass loss, independent of surface elevation changes. Its successor, GRACE‑Follow‑On (GRACE‑FO), launched in 2018, has continued this record, extending the time series to 2023 and improving sensitivity to seasonal mass redistribution [5,6].

Altimetry: CryoSat and ICESat‑2

CryoSat, launched in 2010, used a laser altimeter to measure ice surface elevation with centimetre precision. ICESat‑2, launched in 2018, added a photon‑counting laser system that captures vertical profiles of the ice surface, allowing researchers to separate snow, ice, and meltwater contributions. Together, these missions quantify thinning rates and detect rapid surface‑lowering events, such as the 2019 surge in Greenland’s mass loss [7,8].

Radar interferometry: Sentinel‑1

The European Space Agency’s Sentinel‑1 constellation provides synthetic aperture radar (SAR) interferograms that track surface motion with millimetre precision. These data reveal glacier acceleration and ice‑shelf collapse events that contribute to the overall mass balance [9,10].

---

How mass loss is measured

1. **Altimetry** – Changes in surface elevation are converted to mass change after correcting for snow accumulation, surface deformation (due to ice dynamics), and atmospheric pressure loading. 2. **Gravimetry** – Variations in the gravity field are integrated over the ice‑sheet area to yield net mass change, independent of surface elevation. 3. **Radar interferometry** – Surface displacement data identify accelerating glaciers and ice‑shelf retreat, which are then linked to mass loss. 4. **Optical imagery** – Provides context for surface melt patterns, meltwater ponding, and terminus retreat, which help validate altimetry and gravimetry results.

Calibration against ground‑based mass‑balance measurements (e.g., stake networks, GPS, and ice‑core data) is essential to reduce systematic biases. The combined uncertainty across all techniques is typically 5–10 % of the reported mass loss [11].

---

Drivers of the observed trends

Anthropogenic warming

The dominant driver is the anthropogenic increase in atmospheric greenhouse gases. The resulting rise in mean surface temperature reduces the albedo of ice surfaces, accelerating melt. Ocean heat uptake, particularly in the Southern Ocean, contributes to basal melting of Antarctic ice shelves, while atmospheric warming drives surface melt in Greenland [12,13].

Ice‑sheet dynamics

The loss of buttressing ice shelves in Antarctica has removed a stabilizing force on grounded glaciers, allowing them to accelerate and lose mass more rapidly. In Greenland, the rapid thinning of the central ice sheet and the retreat of outlet glaciers have increased the contribution of ice‑sheet dynamics to total mass loss. Recent studies suggest that the 2019 spike in Greenland’s mass loss was partly driven by a rapid surge of the Jakobshavn Glacier, which temporarily increased its discharge [14].

Feedbacks and uncertainties

Positive feedbacks, such as the ice‑albedo feedback and the potential for ice‑shelf collapse, could amplify future mass loss. However, the magnitude of these feedbacks remains uncertain. Climate models differ in their representation of ice‑sheet dynamics, leading to a range of sea‑level projections (0.3–0.8 m by 2100) under a high‑emissions scenario (RCP8.5) [15].

---

Implications for sea‑level rise

The 3.5 cm rise in global mean sea level that accompanies the 12‑trillion‑ton loss is already measurable in tide‑gauge records and satellite altimetry. Coastal communities worldwide are experiencing more frequent and intense flooding, especially during high tides and storm surges. The projected contribution of Greenland and Antarctica to sea‑level rise is expected to increase from roughly 0.5 cm yr⁻¹ in the early 2000s to 1.5–2.0 cm yr⁻¹ by the end of the century if current warming trends persist. This acceleration threatens low‑lying islands, deltaic regions, and major port cities, potentially displacing millions of people and imposing significant economic costs.

---

Policy and adaptation

International climate agreements, such as the Paris Accord, aim to limit global warming to well below 2 °C, which would constrain the rate of ice‑sheet loss. However, national adaptation plans must account for the already measurable sea‑level rise. Measures include building sea‑walls, restoring wetlands, and revising zoning regulations to reduce vulnerability. Coastal infrastructure projects are increasingly incorporating climate‑resilient design, and some jurisdictions are adopting “managed retreat” strategies where feasible. The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 13, which calls for urgent action to combat climate change, underscores the need for coordinated global responses.

---

Future research

Upcoming satellite missions—such as the planned CryoSat‑3 and the European Space Agency’s IceSat‑3—will provide higher‑resolution altimetry and radar data, improving estimates of ice‑sheet mass balance. Advances in data assimilation techniques will allow scientists to merge satellite observations with physical models more seamlessly, reducing uncertainties. Ground‑based campaigns, including expanded stake networks and autonomous GPS stations, will continue to refine calibration efforts. Additionally, research into the role of sub‑glacial hydrology and ice‑sheet–ocean interactions will help clarify the mechanisms that drive rapid mass loss.

---

Conclusion

Satellites have revealed that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice over the past 47 years, a loss that has already raised global sea level by about 3.5 cm. This record, built on multiple independent measurement techniques, underscores the profound impact of human‑induced warming on the planet’s cryosphere. While the exact future trajectory of ice‑sheet loss remains uncertain, the trend points to a continued rise in sea level that will challenge coastal communities worldwide. Addressing this challenge will require sustained mitigation of greenhouse‑gas emissions, robust adaptation strategies, and continued investment in scientific research to refine our understanding of the Earth’s dynamic ice systems.
