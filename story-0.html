Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
The latest satellite analysis indicates that the Earth has shed more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years. This figure, derived from a combination of gravity‑ and altimetry‑based missions, is the most dramatic estimate yet produced from space‑borne observations. It suggests a rapid acceleration of mass loss that could reshape sea‑level projections, but the result is not yet peer‑reviewed and carries methodological uncertainties that temper its immediacy as a definitive benchmark. <br><br>

What happened

From the early 1970s to the present, a suite of space‑based instruments has tracked the changing mass of the world’s ice sheets. The most recent analysis, which aggregates data from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE), its successor GRACE‑FO, and the altimetry missions CryoSat‑2 and ICESat‑2, indicates that Greenland and Antarctica together have lost more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice since 1973. This loss translates to an average of roughly 250 kilotonnes per year, a figure that dwarfs earlier estimates of 10–20 kilotonnes per year for the same period. The new figure is derived by converting changes in Earth’s gravitational field—measured by GRACE—to mass loss, and by measuring surface elevation changes with radar and laser altimetry. <br><br>

The claim is framed as a potential new benchmark for the rate of ice loss, but it is not yet published in a peer‑reviewed journal. The authors note that the calculation hinges on several assumptions, including the conversion factor between mass and volume, the treatment of temporal gaps between satellite passes, and the correction for glacial isostatic adjustment. Until independent analyses confirm the figure, it remains a provisional indicator rather than a definitive law of nature. <br><br>

Background and context

Historical baseline

Earlier satellite records, starting with the original GRACE mission (2002–2017), have shown a steady decline in the mass of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets. Those data, combined with in‑situ measurements and satellite altimetry, led the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) to estimate that Greenland lost about 280 km³ of ice per year in the early 2000s, while Antarctica’s loss was roughly 80 km³ per year. The new >12 trillion‑tonne figure implies a roughly three‑fold increase in the rate of loss over the last few decades, a trend that aligns with the IPCC’s projected acceleration but exceeds earlier satellite‑derived averages. <br><br>

Measurement methodology

GRACE and GRACE‑FO measure variations in Earth’s gravity field, which change as mass redistributes. By tracking the relative motion of two spacecraft, the missions can infer how much mass has moved or disappeared. CryoSat‑2 and ICESat‑2, on the other hand, measure the height of the ice surface using radar and laser pulses, respectively. Converting these elevation changes into mass loss requires assumptions about ice density and the vertical displacement of the Earth’s crust (glacial isostatic adjustment). Systematic errors can arise from sensor drift, calibration issues, and the difficulty of separating ice mass loss from other mass redistribution processes such as groundwater extraction or ocean circulation changes. <br><br>

Primary drivers

The dominant driver of the accelerated ice loss is anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas warming. Rising atmospheric temperatures increase surface melt on both Greenland and Antarctica. In Greenland, meltwater can pond and infiltrate the ice, accelerating basal melt and weakening the ice sheet’s structural integrity. In Antarctica, warmer ocean currents—particularly the West Antarctic Circumglobal Current—heat the underside of ice shelves, leading to basal melt and the retreat of grounding lines. Natural climate variability, such as El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO), modulates short‑term fluctuations in melt rates but does not explain the long‑term trend. <br><br>

Feedback loops

As ice melts, the surface albedo—its reflectivity—decreases, allowing more solar radiation to be absorbed and further warming the surface. In Antarctica, the loss of ice shelves reduces the buttressing effect that slows ice flow, leading to a self‑reinforcing cycle of faster ice discharge. Ocean heat absorption also increases as the ice retreats, warming the surrounding waters and promoting additional basal melt. These feedbacks amplify the initial warming signal, making the ice loss a potent contributor to future sea‑level rise. <br><br>

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Climate scientists

Many climate scientists view the >12 trillion‑tonne figure as a wake‑up call that the ice sheets are losing mass faster than previously thought. “If this figure holds up under peer review, it would mean that sea‑level rise could accelerate more quickly than the current IPCC scenarios predict,” one researcher noted. However, the same scientist cautioned that the methodology’s uncertainties—particularly the conversion from gravity changes to mass loss—must be resolved before the figure can be used in policy models. <br><br>

Policy analysts

Policy analysts emphasize the implications for international negotiations. The Paris Agreement sets nationally determined contributions (NDCs) that aim to limit global warming to well below 2 °C. A confirmed acceleration in ice loss would tighten the window for achieving those targets and could prompt countries to revise their NDCs upward. Moreover, adaptation budgets—such as those for coastal infrastructure and managed retreat—would need to be scaled up to account for higher projected sea‑level rise. Some analysts argue that the new figure could become a catalyst for more ambitious emissions cuts, while others warn that it could also lead to a false sense of inevitability and reduced urgency. <br><br>

Coastal‑community representatives

Representatives from low‑lying island nations and coastal cities have expressed concern that the figure underscores the urgency of adaptation. “We have already seen the impacts of rising seas on our communities,” said a spokesperson from a Pacific island nation. “If the ice sheets are losing mass at this rate, the future is even more uncertain.” These voices call for immediate investment in resilient infrastructure, early warning systems, and climate‑justice mechanisms that address the disproportionate burden on vulnerable populations. <br><br>

Skeptics

A minority of skeptics question the reliability of satellite data, citing potential errors in the GRACE and altimetry measurements. They argue that without ground‑truth validation—such as mass balance studies from ice cores or GPS networks—the satellite‑derived figure could be overestimated. While acknowledging that the data show a clear downward trend, skeptics urge caution in interpreting the magnitude of the loss and recommend a more conservative approach in policy planning. <br><br>

What to watch (future implications)

Peer‑reviewed studies

The next step for the >12 trillion‑tonne figure is peer review. Several research groups are preparing independent analyses that will test the assumptions behind the mass‑loss conversion and compare their results with the preliminary figure. The outcome of these studies will determine whether the figure becomes a new standard for ice‑sheet mass loss or remains a provisional estimate. <br><br>

New satellite missions

Upcoming missions such as the European Space Agency’s Sentinel‑6 will provide higher‑resolution sea‑level measurements, while NASA’s IceCube mission aims to map the internal structure of ice sheets. These missions will improve the precision of mass‑balance estimates and help disentangle the contributions of surface melt versus basal melt. The continued operation of GRACE‑FO, scheduled to run until 2027, will also refine long‑term trends in gravitational changes. <br><br>

Next IPCC assessment

The forthcoming IPCC Sixth Assessment Report will incorporate the latest satellite data and model projections. If the >12 trillion‑tonne figure is validated, the IPCC may revise its sea‑level rise scenarios upward, potentially shifting the projected range for 2100 from 0.29–1.1 m to a higher value. This would have cascading effects on coastal planning, insurance markets, and international climate finance mechanisms. <br><br>

Policy and adaptation responses

Governments and international bodies may need to reassess their adaptation budgets in light of a higher sea‑level rise trajectory. Coastal protection schemes, flood defenses, and relocation plans would require recalibration. Additionally, the figure could influence the allocation of climate finance under mechanisms such as the Green Climate Fund, directing more resources toward the most vulnerable regions. <br><br>

In sum, the satellite‑derived estimate of more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica over 47 years signals a potentially accelerated trajectory of ice sheet decline. While the figure carries methodological uncertainties and awaits peer‑review confirmation, its implications for sea‑level rise, climate policy, and adaptation planning are profound. As new data streams arrive and analyses mature, the scientific community and policymakers will need to reconcile the urgency of the evidence with the caution warranted by the uncertainties that still surround this unprecedented loss of ice. <br><br>
