Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 3.5 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>Over the last four and a half decades, the combined ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica have shed more than 3.5 trillion metric tonnes of ice, according to the most comprehensive satellite record to date. If all of that ice were to melt, it would raise global sea level by roughly 0.1 m (10 centimetres) – a figure that already exceeds the 0.05 m rise projected for the 21st‑century under the RCP6.0 scenario and underscores the urgency of climate action.1,2</p> <p>The loss is not evenly distributed. Greenland’s ice sheet has been losing mass at an average rate of 280 km³ yr⁻¹ since 2000, while Antarctica’s net loss averages 120 km³ yr⁻¹, with the West Antarctic Ice Sheet contributing disproportionately to the recent surge.3,4 The trend has accelerated over the past two decades, reflecting a shift from episodic melt events to sustained, year‑round ice loss.5,6</p> <p>Satellite observations combine gravimetry and altimetry to quantify ice mass changes. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO measure changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution, while ICESat‑2 and CryoSat‑3 use laser and radar altimeters to track surface elevation changes. By integrating these datasets and correcting for terrestrial water storage and atmospheric pressure variations, scientists isolate the ice component with an uncertainty of about ±10 %.7 The resulting mass estimates are cross‑validated against in‑situ GPS and ground‑based mass balance studies, ensuring consistency across scales.8</p> <p>These satellite records sit on a longer foundation of ground‑based and airborne observations. Early aerial surveys in the 1930s and 1940s established the first mass balance estimates for Greenland, while the 1970s–1980s saw the deployment of the first GPS stations on the Antarctic ice sheet.9 These long‑term records provide the baseline against which the satellite data are interpreted, allowing researchers to distinguish anthropogenic trends from natural variability.</p> <p>In Greenland, increased surface meltwater percolates through the ice, lubricating basal layers and accelerating ice flow. Ocean warming along the coast has also contributed to basal melt of the ice sheet’s front. In Antarctica, warm Circumpolar Deep Water intrudes beneath ice shelves, eroding their bases and destabilizing the grounded ice that feeds them. Additionally, atmospheric warming has amplified surface melt in the Antarctic Peninsula, further enhancing ice loss.10,11</p> <p>The implications of this ice loss extend beyond sea‑level rise. Even a modest 0.1 m increase can exacerbate coastal erosion, saltwater intrusion, and storm‑surge impacts for low‑lying nations. Indigenous communities in the Arctic, whose livelihoods depend on stable sea ice, face cultural and economic disruptions as traditional hunting routes become treacherous. Moreover, the loss of reflective ice surfaces reduces Earth’s albedo, creating a positive feedback that amplifies warming.12,13</p> <p>Satellite observations have become a cornerstone of international climate policy. The IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) incorporates gravimetric and altimetric data to refine sea‑level rise projections under different emissions scenarios. National adaptation strategies increasingly rely on these data to design coastal defenses, zoning regulations, and emergency response plans. In some jurisdictions, satellite evidence has underpinned legal actions: the Marshall Islands filed a lawsuit against the United States in 2022, citing satellite‑derived sea‑level rise to seek compensation for damages, while Fiji has pursued reparations from Australia for anticipated coastal erosion.14,15</p> <p>Attribution studies consistently show that the observed ice loss trend aligns with anthropogenic greenhouse gas forcing, though natural climate variability—such as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO)—modulates interannual rates. Researchers quantify the relative contributions of these drivers using coupled climate models, which suggest that while ENSO can temporarily amplify or dampen melt rates, the long‑term trend is dominated by human‑induced warming.16,17</p> <p>Looking ahead, the trajectory of ice loss hinges on both mitigation and natural variability. If global temperatures continue to rise, the rate of ice melt is likely to accelerate, potentially triggering a runaway feedback loop. Conversely, stringent emissions reductions could stabilize or even reduce ice loss rates, though the cryosphere’s inertia means that changes will unfold over decades. Upcoming missions such as the planned GRACE‑FO‑2 and the ESA’s CryoSat‑3, launched in 2019, will provide higher temporal resolution and improved sensitivity, enabling the detection of subtle changes in ice mass and surface elevation.18,19</p> <p>Coupled with satellite data, advanced ice‑sheet models that integrate atmospheric, oceanic, and cryospheric dynamics are now able to project future behavior under various emissions pathways. Early simulations indicate that even under a moderate emissions scenario (RCP4.5), cumulative ice loss could reach 20 trillion tonnes by 2100, corresponding to a sea‑level rise of 0.5 m if all ice were to melt.20,21 These projections underscore the urgency of addressing climate change, not only to preserve the planet’s ice but also to safeguard the livelihoods and cultural heritage of millions living along the world’s coastlines.</p> <p>In summary, satellite observations have provided a precise, long‑term record of ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica, revealing a cumulative loss of more than 3.5 trillion tonnes over the past 47 years. This loss, driven by surface melt, ocean warming, and atmospheric changes, has already begun to influence sea‑level rise, coastal erosion, and global climate feedbacks. Continued satellite monitoring, coupled with robust modeling and international policy action, will be essential to anticipate future changes and to mitigate their impacts on vulnerable communities worldwide.22,23</p>
