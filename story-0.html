Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites Reveal Over 12 Trillion Tonnes of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time, scientists have measured the retreat of Earth’s two largest ice sheets with a precision that turns raw numbers into a stark warning. From 1979 to 2026, the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE), its successor GRACE‑FO, and the European Space Agency’s CryoSat have recorded a cumulative loss of more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica. If the present trend continues, that loss alone could raise global sea level by roughly 1.2 m, a figure that underscores the urgency of the planet’s warming trajectory.</p> <h2>How Satellites Measure Ice Loss</h2> <p>GRACE and GRACE‑FO fly in close formation, separated by about 220 km. By tracking the minute changes in their distance with laser ranging and microwave interferometry, scientists infer variations in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution. When ice melts or glaciers discharge, the mass of the ice sheet decreases, altering the local gravity field by a few microgals. Integrating these gravity anomalies over time yields the total mass change of the ice sheets with an accuracy of a few hundred gigatonnes.</p> <p>Complementing gravimetry is radar altimetry, the technique used by CryoSat. The satellite emits a radar pulse that reflects off the ice surface and returns to the instrument. By measuring the time delay and correcting for atmospheric delays, CryoSat determines the elevation of the ice surface with centimetre precision. When combined with the mass estimates from GRACE, the altimetry data allow scientists to separate surface melt (which lowers the ice surface) from ice discharge (which reduces the ice mass without necessarily lowering the surface, as ice streams can flow laterally).</p> <p>Together, gravity and elevation measurements provide a comprehensive picture of how the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are responding to a warming climate.</p> <h2>47 Years of Ice Loss: The Numbers</h2> <p>From 1979 to 2026, the combined satellite record shows a loss of 12.3 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes of ice from the two ice sheets. The uncertainty reflects both measurement noise and the model assumptions used to convert gravity anomalies into mass changes. This figure is the largest loss recorded for either ice sheet in the satellite era, surpassing the 9.1 trillion‑tonne loss reported for Greenland alone in 2019 (Rignot et al., 2023).</p> <p>To put the number into perspective, 12 trillion tonnes of ice is equivalent to the volume of about 1.5 million Olympic‑sized swimming pools. If all of that ice were to melt and enter the ocean, it would raise global sea level by roughly 1.2 m—an amount that would inundate low‑lying coastal regions worldwide.</p> <h2>Accelerating Loss: A Changing Trend</h2> <p>While the total loss over 47 years is striking, the satellite record reveals a more alarming pattern: the rate of ice loss has accelerated markedly over the past two decades. Between 2000 and 2010, the combined mass loss averaged 0.8 trillion tonnes per year. In the last five years alone, the rate has climbed to 1.9 trillion tonnes per year, more than double the early‑2000s pace (Kumar et al., 2021). This acceleration is evident in both Greenland and Antarctica, although the mechanisms differ.</p> <p>In Greenland, the surge is driven largely by increased surface melt, which is itself a response to higher summer temperatures and a reduction in seasonal snow cover. The meltwater then percolates into the ice, lubricating the base and accelerating ice flow. In Antarctica, the primary driver is the rapid discharge of outlet glaciers and ice streams, particularly in West Antarctica and the Antarctic Peninsula, where warming ocean waters melt the ice from below (Ligtenberg et al., 2022). The satellite data also show that the mass loss is not uniform across the ice sheets; localized hotspots of rapid change are emerging, especially in the eastern and western sectors of Greenland and the West Antarctic Ice Sheet.</p> <h2>Implications for Global Sea‑Level Rise</h2> <p>Sea‑level rise is the sum of several components: thermal expansion of seawater, meltwater from glaciers and ice caps, and mass loss from the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets. Over the past 30 years, global sea level has risen at an average rate of 3.3 mm per year (Kopp et al., 2020). The 12 trillion‑tonne loss from the ice sheets contributes directly to this rise, accounting for about 40 % of the observed increase in the last decade (Pritchard et al., 2022). If the current acceleration continues, the ice sheets could contribute an additional 0.5 m to sea level by the end of the century, a figure that would dwarf the 0.3 m projected by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Fifth Assessment Report (IPCC, 2013).</p> <p>Beyond the numeric contribution, the loss of ice also destabilizes the ice sheets themselves. As mass decreases, the ice becomes more susceptible to internal flow instabilities—such as the formation of ice streams and the development of meltwater channels—that can further accelerate ice loss. This positive feedback loop could lead to a tipping point, beyond which the ice sheets would retreat rapidly and irreversibly (Kopp et al., 2020).</p> <h2>Broader Climate Context</h2> <p>The satellite record aligns with the broader pattern of global warming. The Arctic has warmed more than twice the global average over the past 50 years, a phenomenon known as Arctic amplification (Serreze & Barry, 2011). This rapid warming has led to a decline in sea‑ice extent, a reduction in snow cover, and an increase in surface melt on the Greenland ice sheet. In Antarctica, the warming of the Southern Ocean has accelerated the melting of ice shelves that buttress the West Antarctic Ice Sheet, reducing the buttressing effect and allowing the ice to flow more freely into the ocean (Ligtenberg et al., 2022).</p> <p>Natural climate variability—such as volcanic eruptions, solar cycles, and El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events—can modulate short‑term fluctuations in ice loss. However, the long‑term upward trend in mass loss is consistent with the sustained rise in greenhouse‑gas concentrations driven by human activity. The satellite record therefore provides robust evidence that anthropogenic climate change is the dominant force behind the observed ice loss.</p> <h2>Societal Impacts and Policy Relevance</h2> <p>Coastal communities around the world face heightened risks of flooding, storm surges, and erosion as sea levels rise. The United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction estimates that by 2050, more than 300 million people could be displaced by rising seas (UNDRR, 2021). The loss of ice also affects ocean circulation patterns, particularly the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), which can influence weather systems, marine ecosystems, and fisheries that many societies depend on for food and livelihoods (IPCC, 2013).</p> <p>From a policy standpoint, the satellite findings reinforce the goals set out in the Paris Agreement, which aims to limit global warming to well below 2 °C and pursue efforts to keep it below 1.5 °C. The observed acceleration of ice loss suggests that current mitigation pathways are insufficient to keep sea‑level rise within the safe limits envisioned by the agreement. Policymakers must therefore accelerate the transition to low‑carbon energy, implement adaptation measures for vulnerable coastal regions, and invest in research to improve ice‑sheet models and satellite monitoring capabilities.</p> <h2>Future Directions and Technological Innovations</h2> <p>While GRACE and GRACE‑FO have provided a robust record of ice loss, the end of the GRACE‑FO mission in 2025 will leave a gap in the gravity data stream. To maintain continuity, the European Space Agency plans to launch the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment Follow‑On (GRACE‑FO‑2) in 2028, which will feature improved instrumentation and a longer mission lifetime (ESA, 2023). Additionally, the upcoming NASA mission, the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment Next Generation (GRACE‑NG), aims to provide higher spatial resolution and better sensitivity to smaller ice masses, such as mountain glaciers and ice caps (NASA, 2024).</p> <p>On the radar altimetry front, the European Space Agency’s IceSat‑2, scheduled for launch in 2025, will provide higher‑frequency observations of ice surface elevation, enabling more precise separation of melt and discharge processes (ESA, 2025). Combining these new datasets with existing satellite records will refine our understanding of ice‑sheet dynamics and improve projections of future sea‑level rise.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite record of the past 47 years paints a stark picture: more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice have vanished from Greenland and Antarctica, and the rate of loss is accelerating. This loss is not a distant, abstract phenomenon—it is a tangible contributor to the sea‑level rise that threatens coastal communities, ecosystems, and economies worldwide. The data underscore the urgency of limiting global warming and implementing robust adaptation strategies. Continued investment in satellite monitoring, coupled with aggressive mitigation policies, is essential to safeguard the planet’s future.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ul> <li>ESA. # Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

<h1>Satellites Reveal Over 12 Trillion Tonnes of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1>

<p>For the first time, scientists have measured the retreat of Earth’s two largest ice sheets with a precision that turns raw numbers into a stark warning. From 1979 to 2026, the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE), its successor GRACE‑FO, and the European Space Agency’s CryoSat have recorded a cumulative loss of more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica. If the present trend continues, that loss alone could raise global sea level by roughly 1.2 m, a figure that underscores the urgency of the planet’s warming trajectory.</p>

<p>GRACE and GRACE‑FO fly in close formation, separated by about 220 km. By tracking the minute changes in their distance with laser ranging and microwave interferometry, scientists infer variations in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution. When ice melts or glaciers discharge, the mass of the ice sheet decreases, altering the local gravity field by a few microgals. Integrating these gravity anomalies over time yields the total mass change of the ice sheets with an accuracy of a few hundred gigatonnes.</p>
