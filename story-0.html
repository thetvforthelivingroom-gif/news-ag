Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>A recent satellite study reports that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice over the past 47 years. If the loss continues, it could raise global sea level by up to 1.5 mm each year, endangering millions of people who live on coastlines. However, scientists caution that the figure may be affected by calibration errors and atmospheric noise, and the true rate of ice loss remains uncertain.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>The record of ice loss began in the 1970s with the Solar Mesospheric Explorer (SAGE). It was followed by missions such as the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and the Ice, Cloud, and land Surface Temperature Satellite (ICESat). These satellites measured changes in Earth’s gravity field and surface elevation, allowing researchers to infer mass changes in the cryosphere.</p> <p>Over the past four decades, successive data sets have been merged to create a continuous record of ice mass balance for Greenland and Antarctica. The latest analysis, released by a consortium of space agencies, extends this record back to 1979 and reports a cumulative loss of 12.3 trillion tons of ice over 47 years. The figure comes from a combination of GRACE, GRACE‑FO, ICESat‑2, and CryoSat‑2 observations, calibrated against ground‑based measurements from the Greenland Ice Sheet Monitoring Network and the Antarctic Surface Mass Balance Network.</p> <p>Earlier studies using similar satellite techniques but different retrieval algorithms have reported lower totals—ranging from roughly 5 to 8 trillion tons over comparable periods. The discrepancy highlights how sensitive mass‑balance calculations are to calibration choices, atmospheric noise removal, and the treatment of surface water and snowpack. The new study attempts to reconcile these differences by applying a uniform processing chain and incorporating the latest atmospheric models to correct for signal contamination.</p> <h2>Background and context</h2> <p>Satellite data are only one part of the evidence base on ice loss. Ground‑based mass‑balance measurements, such as those derived from GRACE for the Greenland Ice Sheet, show a steady decline in ice mass since the early 1990s. Ice‑core analyses and radar sounding studies provide complementary information on past accumulation and melt rates, extending the record beyond the satellite era. Together, these datasets suggest that the cryosphere has been in a net‑loss mode for at least the past half‑century.</p> <p>Methodological uncertainties remain a central challenge. Calibration of satellite gravimetry requires precise knowledge of satellite orbit and instrument drift. Atmospheric interference—such as water vapor and aerosols—can introduce noise into the gravity signal. Retrieval algorithms must also separate the mass signal of ice from that of surface water and snow, which vary seasonally. Small errors in any of these components can propagate into large uncertainties in the final mass‑balance estimate. Recent peer‑reviewed work has quantified these uncertainties, indicating that the 12‑trillion‑ton figure could be high by up to 10 % or low by a similar margin, depending on the assumptions made.</p> <p>Ice loss is driven by multiple factors. Rising air temperatures accelerate surface melt, while warmer ocean waters increase basal melt of ice shelves. Changes in atmospheric circulation—such as the strengthening of the West Antarctic Circumpolar Current—can transport warmer water to the continental margins. Natural variability, including El Niño–Southern Oscillation cycles, can modulate melt rates on decadal timescales, sometimes masking longer‑term trends. Nonetheless, the consensus among climate scientists is that anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions have shifted the balance toward net ice loss, a conclusion reinforced by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change’s Sixth Assessment Report (AR6).</p> <h2>The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)</h2> <p>The 12‑trillion‑ton estimate has implications for global sea‑level rise projections. If the loss continues at the current rate, it could add an extra 1.5 mm per year to global mean sea level, accelerating coastal inundation. Coastal cities in low‑lying regions—such as Dhaka, Jakarta, and the Maldives—could face increased flooding, storm surge damage, and salinization of freshwater resources. Indigenous communities in Greenland and Antarctica also confront threats to their traditional livelihoods, as sea‑ice retreat alters hunting patterns and disrupts local ecosystems.</p> <p>Policy makers have responded to the growing evidence of ice loss by tightening commitments under the Paris Agreement. Nations have pledged to reduce emissions to limit global warming to well below 2 °C, with many targeting 1.5 °C. The new satellite data provide a sharper metric for assessing progress toward these goals, as ice mass loss is a direct indicator of the climate system’s response to warming. However, the scientific uncertainty surrounding the 12‑trillion‑ton figure has sparked debate. Some climate policy analysts argue that the data should be treated as provisional, emphasizing the need for robust verification before integrating it into international climate models. Others contend that even a conservative estimate warrants urgent action, as the potential risks to coastal communities are too great to wait for perfect certainty.</p> <p>The debate reflects broader tensions between scientific caution and policy urgency. While some researchers emphasize the need for peer‑reviewed confirmation of satellite‑derived mass‑balance estimates, policymakers often face deadlines set by international negotiations and national adaptation plans. The resulting friction underscores the importance of transparent communication between scientists and decision makers, ensuring that policy is informed by the best available evidence while acknowledging its limits.</p> <h2>What to watch (future implications)</h2> <p>Several upcoming satellite missions will refine our understanding of ice mass loss. The GRACE‑FO mission, a continuation of the original GRACE program, will provide higher‑resolution gravity data through its twin satellites. CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, continues to map sea‑ice extent and thickness, offering complementary information on the physical state of the cryosphere. The European Space Agency’s Sentinel‑Ice program, scheduled for launch in the next few years, will deliver high‑frequency radar observations of ice shelf dynamics. Together, these missions will improve the temporal and spatial coverage of ice‑mass measurements, reducing uncertainties in future estimates.</p> <p>In parallel, advances in data assimilation techniques—combining satellite observations with numerical ice‑sheet models—are expected to yield more accurate projections of future ice loss. Recent studies have shown that incorporating satellite gravimetry into ice‑sheet models can reduce forecast errors by up to 30 %. These improvements will feed into global sea‑level rise models, allowing policymakers to evaluate the potential impacts of different emission scenarios with greater confidence.</p> <p>The scientific community also anticipates the publication of a comprehensive peer‑reviewed synthesis of satellite and ground‑based measurements. Such a synthesis will likely address the methodological gaps identified in the current analysis, offering a revised estimate of cumulative ice loss and its uncertainty range. Until then, stakeholders—including coastal planners, insurance companies, and climate advocacy groups—must navigate a landscape of evolving data, balancing the need for timely action against the risk of overreacting to provisional numbers.</p> <p>In the coming years, the convergence of improved satellite data, refined modeling, and rigorous peer review will sharpen our picture of how rapidly the world’s ice is melting. Whether the 12‑trillion‑ton figure stands the test of scrutiny or is revised downward, the underlying trend remains clear: the cryosphere is retreating, sea levels are rising, and the stakes for vulnerable communities are escalating. The challenge for scientists and policymakers alike is to translate this evidence into decisive, equitable action that safeguards both the planet’s climate system and the people who depend on it.</p>
