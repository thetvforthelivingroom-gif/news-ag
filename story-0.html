Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellite observations reveal a 9‑trillion‑ton loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica since 2002</h1> <p>Satellite measurements show that Greenland and Antarctica have shed 9 trillion tonnes of ice between 2002 and 2023—more than the entire loss recorded in the 20th century. The data, derived from the GRACE and GRACE‑Follow‑On gravity missions and complemented by CryoSat‑2 and ICESat‑2 altimetry, confirm that the pace of ice loss is accelerating and that the polar regions are a key source of rising sea level.</p> <h2>From ground surveys to global gravimetry</h2> <p>Before satellites, estimates of ice‑sheet change relied on sparse ground‑based mass balance measurements, aerial photography, and radar surveys. These methods covered limited areas and carried large uncertainties, especially in Antarctica’s interior. The 1990s brought satellite gravimetry, which detects tiny variations in Earth’s gravity field caused by changes in mass distribution. GRACE (2002–2017) and its successor GRACE‑Follow‑On (2018–present) provide the most precise, continuous record of ice‑sheet mass change over the past two decades. CryoSat‑2 (launched 2010) and ICESat‑2 (launched 2018) measure surface elevation changes, helping to separate surface melt from ice flow.</p> <h2>Quantifying the loss</h2> <p>Recent GRACE analyses indicate that Greenland has lost about 5.5 trillion tonnes of ice since 2002, while Antarctica has shed roughly 3.5 trillion tonnes. These figures come from the combined data of GRACE, GRACE‑Follow‑On, CryoSat‑2, and ICESat‑2, and agree with independent satellite altimetry studies and in‑situ mass balance measurements (see Table 1). Over the 22‑year period, the average annual loss is 0.2 % of the total ice mass of the two sheets, or roughly 300 million tonnes per year. The rate of loss has accelerated markedly in the last decade, with Greenland’s mass balance becoming increasingly negative and the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (WAIS) showing a pronounced surge in ice discharge.</p> <table> <tr><th>Period</th><th>Greenland (Gt)</th><th>Antarctica (Gt)</th></tr> <tr><td>2002–2012</td><td>≈1.5</td><td>≈1.0</td></tr> <tr><td>2013–2023</td><td>≈4.0</td><td>≈2.5</td></tr> </table> <p>These numbers are not a sudden spike but the culmination of a long‑term trend that has steepened over time. Early GRACE data from the 2000s indicated a loss of about 2 trillion tonnes over a decade, but the rate has more than doubled in the last five years. The acceleration aligns with the rapid increase in atmospheric and ocean temperatures driven by anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions.</p> <h2>Drivers of the accelerated melt</h2> <p>The primary driver of the accelerated ice loss is anthropogenic CO₂ forcing. Rising atmospheric concentrations of greenhouse gases trap more heat, warming both the atmosphere and the ocean. In Greenland, surface melt is enhanced by higher temperatures and the albedo feedback: as snow and ice melt, darker surfaces are exposed, absorbing more solar radiation and accelerating further melt. In Antarctica, ocean warming is a key factor, especially along the West Antarctic Peninsula, where warm Circumpolar Deep Water intrudes onto the continental shelf and melts ice from below. The combination of surface and basal melt, coupled with increased ice discharge into the ocean, amplifies the overall loss.</p> <h2>Uncertainties and calibration challenges</h2> <p>While satellite gravimetry offers unprecedented precision, several sources of uncertainty remain. First, long‑term calibration across multiple satellite missions is challenging; small drifts in instrument sensitivity can bias mass estimates by up to 5 %. Second, natural variability—such as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation—can modulate ice‑sheet mass on interannual timescales, complicating trend extraction. Third, converting gravity changes to mass requires assumptions about ice density and the distribution of mass within the ice sheet, which can introduce systematic errors. Researchers mitigate these issues by combining gravimetry with altimetry, surface mass balance models, and in‑situ observations, but uncertainties of 10–15 % remain for the total loss.</p> <h2>Regional variability</h2> <p>Not all parts of the ice sheets are losing mass at the same rate. Greenland’s interior, where ice is thick and cold, has shown a relatively steady mass loss, whereas the coastal margins—particularly the southeast coast—have experienced rapid surface melt. In Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet is the most vulnerable region, with the Amundsen and Ross Sea sectors showing the largest ice‑discharge rates. The East Antarctic Ice Sheet, by contrast, has remained largely stable, with some areas even showing slight mass gain due to increased snowfall. These regional differences are critical for refining sea‑level rise projections and for understanding the mechanisms of ice‑sheet response to climate change.</p> <h2>Implications for sea‑level rise</h2> <p>Ice‑sheet mass loss is a major contributor to global mean sea‑level rise (GMSLR). The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) projects that, under a high‑emissions pathway (SSP5‑8.5), GMSLR could reach 0.7–1.1 m by 2100, while a low‑emissions pathway (SSP1‑2.6) would limit rise to 0.3–0.5 m. The satellite‑derived loss of 9 trillion tonnes since 2002 already accounts for about 0.5 m of the observed rise, leaving a substantial margin for future acceleration. Even the lower bound of 0.3 m would inundate low‑lying coastal cities, disrupt infrastructure, and threaten ecosystems.</p> <h2>Economic costs and adaptation pressures</h2> <p>Economic analyses estimate that a 1 m sea‑level rise could impose costs exceeding $1 trillion over the century, factoring in coastal property damage, lost productivity, and adaptation expenditures. Insurance companies are already adjusting premiums in high‑risk regions, and municipalities are accelerating flood‑plain management and building‑code revisions. The cost of inaction, however, is projected to be far higher, especially as the pace of ice loss continues to accelerate.</p> <h2>Policy responses and the Paris Agreement</h2> <p>The satellite data reinforce the urgency of meeting the Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target. Carbon pricing mechanisms—such as emissions trading schemes and carbon taxes—are increasingly viewed as essential tools for reducing greenhouse‑gas concentrations. International cooperation on adaptation funding, through mechanisms like the Green Climate Fund, is critical for vulnerable nations facing rapid sea‑level rise. The data also highlight the need for robust monitoring systems, suggesting that continued investment in satellite missions and ground validation is essential for tracking future changes.</p> <h2>Research gaps and future directions</h2> <p>Despite the high precision of satellite measurements, several uncertainties remain. Long‑term calibration across multiple satellite missions is challenging, and distinguishing natural variability from anthropogenic trends requires further study. Improving the fidelity of ice‑sheet models—particularly in representing complex processes such as ice‑ocean interaction and sub‑glacial hydrology—is a priority for the scientific community. Continued interdisciplinary research, integrating satellite data with in‑situ observations and climate modeling, will be crucial for refining future projections and informing policy decisions.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Satellite observations provide a clear, quantitative record of the rapid loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica. The 9‑trillion‑ton loss since 2002 is a stark reminder of the climate system’s fragility and a call for decisive action. While uncertainties remain, the consensus is that continued emissions reductions and robust adaptation strategies are essential to limit sea‑level rise and protect vulnerable communities worldwide.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Rignot, E., et al.
