Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites Reveal Earth Lost More Than 12 Trillion Tons of Ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time, scientists have combined data from satellite gravimetry and laser altimetry to quantify the total mass loss of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets from 1979 to 2026. The result is 12.3 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes (t), a figure that represents a significant upward revision of earlier estimates and underscores the accelerating pace of polar retreat in a warming world.</p> <h2>Why the Numbers Matter</h2> <p>Ice sheets contain about 70 % of the planet’s freshwater. When they melt, the water that was once locked on land flows into the oceans, raising sea level. The 12‑trillion‑ton figure is equivalent to a global mean sea‑level increase of roughly 4.2 cm if all of that ice were to melt. Today’s oceans are already 0.3 m higher than in 1993, and the continued loss of ice is expected to accelerate this trend. The new estimate provides a more precise benchmark for climate models, coastal planners, and policymakers who rely on robust ice‑mass data to assess future risks.</p> <h2>Data Sources and Methodology</h2> <p>The analysis is based on two satellite missions:</p> <ul> <li><strong>GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment)</strong> (2002–2017) and its successor <strong>GRACE‑FO (Follow‑On)</strong> (2018–2026) measure minute changes in Earth’s gravity field. When ice melts, the mass that was once stored on land is transferred to the ocean, producing a detectable signature in the gravity field [1,4].</li> <li><strong>ICESat‑2</strong>, launched in 2018, uses a laser altimeter to track the elevation of the ice surface with metre‑level precision. By pairing gravity and elevation changes, scientists can isolate mass changes attributable to ice loss from other sources such as groundwater depletion or tectonic activity [2].</li> </ul> <p>The study combined all available GRACE/GRACE‑FO Level‑2 mass‑change products with ICESat‑2 elevation data, applying the latest atmospheric, oceanic, and hydrological corrections to reduce systematic biases. Ground‑based GPS stations and airborne radar surveys (e.g., NASA’s Operation IceBridge) were used to validate the satellite‑derived mass changes and to correct for ice‑flow velocity, ensuring that the observed mass loss reflects true ice melt rather than ice movement [5,6].</p> <h2>Key Findings</h2> <p>Over the 47‑year period, the Greenland ice sheet lost 5.1 ± 0.2 trillion t, while Antarctica lost 7.2 ± 0.3 trillion t. The spatial pattern of loss is highly heterogeneous: the fastest rates are now observed along the western margins of Greenland and the Antarctic Peninsula, where atmospheric warming and oceanic heat transport are most pronounced [7]. The rate of mass loss has accelerated by roughly 0.5 % per year, a trend that aligns with the increasing frequency of extreme weather events and the observed decline in seasonal snow cover.</p> <p>When converted to sea‑level equivalent, the combined mass loss corresponds to an additional 4.2 cm of global mean sea level. This figure is consistent with independent estimates from tide‑gauge networks and satellite altimetry, which show a cumulative rise of 0.3 m since 1993, largely attributable to ice‑sheet melt and thermal expansion of the oceans [8].</p> <h2>Uncertainties and Confidence Intervals</h2> <p>While the 12.3‑trillion‑t figure is robust, it is essential to acknowledge the inherent uncertainties in satellite gravimetry and altimetry. The dominant sources of error are:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Atmospheric loading</strong> – variations in atmospheric pressure can mimic mass changes; corrections are applied using reanalysis data, but residual errors remain at the 5–10 % level [9].</li> <li><strong>Ocean tide models</strong> – inaccuracies in ocean tide predictions can bias gravity measurements; the latest models reduce this bias to <1 % of the signal [9].</li> <li><strong>Ice‑flow velocity</strong> – uncertainties in ice‑flow models propagate into mass‑change estimates; GPS‑based velocity corrections reduce this error to <3 % [6].</li> </ul> <p>Combining these factors yields an overall uncertainty of ±0.4 trillion t (≈ 3 % relative). The error bars are shown in the accompanying figure and are consistent with the confidence intervals reported in previous GRACE studies (8–10 trillion t) [1]. Importantly, the new estimate is statistically indistinguishable from the upper bound of earlier studies, reinforcing the conclusion that the ice sheets are losing mass at a faster rate than previously thought.</p> <h2>Independent Verification and Cross‑Validation</h2> <p>The findings are corroborated by several independent lines of evidence:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Airborne radar surveys</strong> from NASA’s Operation IceBridge and ESA’s CryoSat missions have documented thinning and surface lowering in the same regions identified by the satellite gravimetry analysis [5].</li> <li><strong>GPS velocity measurements</strong> show that the observed mass loss cannot be explained by ice flow alone, confirming that melting is the dominant process [6].</li> <li><strong>Sea‑level rise records</strong> from tide gauges and satellite altimetry match the cumulative mass loss when converted to sea‑level equivalent, providing an independent check on the gravimetry‑derived figure [8].</li> </ul> <h2>Implications for the Future</h2> <p>These results have far‑reaching implications for climate science, engineering, and policy. First, they provide a more accurate target for climate models, allowing researchers to refine predictions of future sea‑level rise. Second, coastal planners can use the updated figures to reassess flood risk, infrastructure resilience, and evacuation strategies. Finally, policymakers can draw on the robust evidence to strengthen international commitments to reduce greenhouse‑gas emissions and to protect vulnerable communities.</p> <p>Looking ahead, the continued operation of GRACE‑FO and the launch of next‑generation gravimetry missions—such as the proposed GRACE‑NEXT—will enable even finer resolution of mass changes and help to disentangle the complex interplay between atmospheric, oceanic, and cryospheric processes. Coupled with advances in satellite altimetry and ground‑based observations, these tools will keep the scientific community—and society at large—well informed about the pace of ice loss and its global consequences.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The combined loss of more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past four and a half decades is a stark reminder of the rapid changes occurring at Earth’s poles. By integrating gravimetry, altimetry, and ground‑based data, scientists have produced the most comprehensive and precise estimate to date. This new benchmark not only confirms that the ice sheets are retreating faster than previously thought but also underscores the urgency of addressing the root causes of climate change to safeguard our planet’s future.</p>
