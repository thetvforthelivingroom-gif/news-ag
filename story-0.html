Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
**Satellites reveal Earth may have shed more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years** A recent analysis of data from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO suggests that the combined Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have lost an estimated 12 trillion tonnes of ice since 1973. The figure—more than three times the loss recorded over the last two decades—has sparked debate among scientists, policymakers, and indigenous communities. While the satellite observations themselves are robust, converting gravitational changes into ice‑mass estimates relies on complex models and assumptions that are still being scrutinised. The study has not yet undergone peer review, and its authors acknowledge that uncertainties could be as high as ±30 % for the cumulative loss estimate. Nonetheless, the potential implications for sea‑level rise, coastal communities, and climate policy are profound. ---

1. How the estimate was derived

GRACE, launched in 2002, and GRACE‑FO, launched in 2018, measure minute variations in Earth’s gravity field caused by mass redistribution. When ice melts, the mass that was stored on land is transferred to the ocean, altering the gravity signature that the satellites detect. By inverting these gravity changes, scientists can estimate the amount of ice lost or gained over time.

The new study extends the time baseline to 47 years by combining the GRACE record with earlier satellite altimetry and terrestrial mass‑balance data. Researchers applied a novel inversion technique that incorporates updated models of ice dynamics, bedrock response, and glacial isostatic adjustment. The method assumes a particular spatial distribution of melt and a linear relationship between surface mass balance and bedrock deformation. Compared with earlier GRACE‑FO studies that used different parameter sets, the new approach yields a higher inferred rate of ice loss, culminating in the 12‑trillion‑tonne figure.

The authors stress that the methodology introduces new assumptions that could overstate the magnitude of ice loss. Critics point out that the uncertainty bounds are large and that the analysis has not yet been validated by independent replication. Nevertheless, the satellite data themselves are widely regarded as reliable for detecting mass changes on a global scale.

---

2. Context: what we already know

Over the past two decades, GRACE and GRACE‑FO have consistently reported a net loss of 3–4 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica combined. This loss corresponds to a sea‑level rise contribution of about 0.1 mm per year, a figure that has been incorporated into global sea‑level projections [T1]. The primary drivers of this loss are atmospheric warming, which increases surface melt, and oceanic heat transport, which raises the temperature of ice‑contacting waters and accelerates basal melt. Runoff from the ice sheets also contributes to mass loss as meltwater drains into the ocean.

The new analysis seeks to capture a longer‑term trend that includes the early 1970s, a period when satellite gravimetry was not yet available. By integrating earlier altimetry and terrestrial data, the authors aim to reconstruct a more complete history of ice‑sheet mass change. However, this reconstruction relies on assumptions about the stability of ice‑sheet dynamics and the constancy of external forcings that are difficult to verify. Even with a ±30 % uncertainty, the cumulative loss estimate would still represent a substantial increase over previous figures, but it would not necessarily indicate a dramatic acceleration of melt.

3. Translating ice loss into sea‑level rise

If 12 trillion tonnes of ice were to melt completely, the resulting water would raise global sea level by roughly 1.5 meters, assuming uniform distribution. Even a fraction of this loss would have significant implications for low‑lying coastal communities and island nations. However, the actual sea‑level rise depends on many factors, including the rate of melt, the redistribution of water, and the response of ocean circulation. These factors introduce additional uncertainty into the conversion from ice‑mass loss to sea‑level rise.

4. Scientific debate

Within the scientific community, the 12‑trillion‑tonne claim has elicited a range of responses. Some researchers applaud the effort to extend the temporal scope of ice‑mass loss studies, noting that longer records can reveal trends obscured by interannual variability. Others caution that the methodological choices—particularly the treatment of bedrock deformation and the assumption of linearity in mass loss—may inflate the estimate. A small cohort of climate modelers has begun to incorporate the new figure into projections of future sea‑level rise, but they emphasize the need for independent verification before adopting it in policy scenarios [T1].

5. Indigenous and local community perspectives

Indigenous communities in Greenland and the Antarctic region have long depended on the ice for cultural identity, subsistence hunting, and navigation. The rapid loss of ice not only threatens their traditional ways of life but also alters the local climate and ecosystems. Reports from community leaders highlight concerns about the erosion of sea ice, which reduces hunting grounds for seals and narrows migration corridors for polar bears and other species. They also note that the loss of ice contributes to the warming of coastal waters, affecting fisheries that many depend on for food and income [T3].

In Greenland, some Inuit groups have organized to document the changing landscape through oral histories and participatory mapping. These efforts aim to preserve knowledge of past ice extents and to inform adaptation strategies such as building elevated infrastructure and diversifying livelihoods. Similar initiatives are emerging in the Antarctic region, where research stations and support crews are increasingly aware of the logistical challenges posed by shrinking ice shelves and the potential for increased storm activity [T3].

6. Policy and economic adaptation

Governments and international bodies have begun to incorporate satellite‑derived ice‑mass loss into national adaptation plans. The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) has cited GRACE data in its reporting on global sea‑level rise, and several coastal cities have used the data to justify investments in seawalls, flood gates, and zoning reforms. The 12‑trillion‑tonne figure, if confirmed, could accelerate the urgency of these measures and potentially unlock additional funding streams under mechanisms such as the Global Climate Facility.

However, the high uncertainty associated with the new estimate has led some policymakers to exercise caution. Industry groups involved in shipping, offshore drilling, and coastal real estate have expressed concerns about the potential for overestimation to drive premature or costly infrastructure changes. They argue that a balanced approach, integrating multiple lines of evidence—including satellite gravimetry, ground‑based mass‑balance measurements, and climate model projections—should guide investment decisions [T2].

7. Skepticism versus urgency

The broader public discourse reflects a tension between skepticism of the new figure and the urgency of addressing climate change. Some climate skeptics point to the methodological uncertainties and the lack of peer‑reviewed publication as reasons to question the claim. Others, including climate scientists and environmental advocates, emphasize that even a conservative estimate of increased ice loss underscores the need for rapid mitigation and adaptation. This debate mirrors long‑standing discussions about the role of scientific uncertainty in policy‑making and the challenges of communicating complex data to non‑experts [T3].

8. What to watch next

a. Peer review and independent replication

The next critical step for the 12‑trillion‑tonne claim is rigorous peer review. The authors have submitted their methodology and results to a leading journal, and independent research groups are attempting to replicate the analysis using alternative inversion techniques. Confirmation or refutation of the estimate will hinge on whether the results hold under different assumptions about bedrock response and ice‑sheet dynamics. The scientific community will also scrutinise the treatment of error propagation, particularly the handling of long‑term noise in the GRACE data [T1].

b. Integration with climate models

Climate modelers are already exploring how the new ice‑mass loss estimate could affect projections of sea‑level rise under various greenhouse‑gas emission scenarios. If the figure proves accurate, it would imply a higher baseline of sea‑level rise, potentially shifting the thresholds for coastal adaptation strategies. Models that incorporate the new data will need to adjust parameters related to ice‑sheet stability, ocean heat uptake, and atmospheric circulation patterns. The outcomes will inform international negotiations on adaptation financing and risk management [T1].

c. Indigenous knowledge and governance

Indigenous communities will continue to play a pivotal role in monitoring ice loss and advocating for adaptation measures. Their traditional ecological knowledge can complement satellite observations, providing ground‑truth data on ice thickness, melt rates, and ecosystem changes. Policymakers are increasingly recognising the value of co‑producing science with indigenous stakeholders, particularly in the context of climate justice and equitable resource allocation. Future governance frameworks may formalise partnerships that integrate satellite data with community‑led monitoring programmes [T3].

d. Economic and infrastructural responses

Should the 12‑trillion‑tonne figure be validated, coastal cities and island nations may accelerate investments in resilient infrastructure, such as seawalls, elevated housing, and improved drainage systems. Insurance companies and financial institutions may adjust risk models to reflect higher sea‑level rise probabilities, potentially affecting property values and investment flows. Conversely, if the estimate is found to be inflated, some adaptation projects might be re‑prioritised, underscoring the importance of accurate data for cost‑effective decision‑making [T2].

9. Conclusion

Whether the 12‑trillion‑tonne claim stands the test of scrutiny, the dialogue it has sparked—about uncertainty, urgency, and the interplay between science and society—remains a vital part of the global response to climate change. The new analysis pushes the envelope of what we can infer from satellite gravimetry, but it also reminds us that translating raw data into actionable knowledge requires careful modelling, transparent assumptions, and rigorous peer review. As the scientific community refines its tools for measuring ice loss, policymakers, communities, and businesses will need to translate those measurements into concrete actions that protect vulnerable populations and preserve the planet’s fragile polar ecosystems.
