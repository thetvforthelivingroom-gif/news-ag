Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
For the first time in human history, satellites have documented the loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years—a volume that could raise global sea levels by several meters and endanger millions of lives. The data, gathered by a decade‑long constellation of Earth‑observation satellites, reveal a silent, accelerating loss that far exceeds earlier estimates and underscores the urgency of climate action.

What happened

Satellite missions launched in the early 1990s, such as the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑Follow‑On, began measuring changes in the Earth’s gravity field with unprecedented precision. By tracking the subtle shifts in gravity caused by mass loss from ice sheets, scientists have been able to quantify the volume of ice melting and submerging into the oceans. Recent analyses of these datasets indicate that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice since 1975, a figure that dwarfs earlier projections based on limited ground‑based observations alone [T1].

The rate of loss has not been steady. While the first two decades of the satellite era showed a relatively modest decline, the last twenty years have seen a sharp acceleration. In Greenland, the mass‑loss rate rose from roughly 0.5 trillion tons per year in the 1990s to over 2 trillion tons per year in the 2010s. Antarctica’s contribution has also surged, with the West Antarctic Ice Sheet experiencing rapid thinning and the retreat of its floating ice shelves. The combined effect is a cumulative loss that now exceeds 12 trillion tons, a milestone that signals a tipping point in the cryosphere’s stability [T1].

Background and context

The satellite‑derived ice‑loss figures confirm and extend long‑standing theories about how greenhouse‑gas emissions drive polar melt. Rising atmospheric temperatures increase surface melt, while warmer ocean waters erode ice from below, especially in the fragile ice shelves of Antarctica. The loss of ice also reduces the Earth’s albedo—its ability to reflect sunlight—creating a positive feedback loop that accelerates warming. These processes were already hinted at in earlier climate models, but the satellite data provide a direct, quantitative measure of their real‑world impact.

Methodologically, the GRACE satellites measure the Earth’s gravity field by monitoring the distance between two orbiting spacecraft. As mass shifts—such as ice melting into the ocean—the gravity field changes, causing detectable variations in the spacecraft separation. By integrating these changes over time, scientists can infer the amount of mass lost from the ice sheets. Recent advances in data processing, including improved atmospheric and oceanic corrections, have reduced uncertainties and increased confidence in the 12‑trillion‑ton estimate [T1].

Despite the robustness of the satellite approach, uncertainties remain. The stability of ice shelves, for instance, is difficult to capture fully, as they can collapse abruptly, releasing large volumes of ice in a short period. Ocean‑warming impacts on basal melt rates are also complex, with regional variations that may not be fully resolved by current satellite resolution. Moreover, methodological differences between satellite missions and ground‑based measurements can lead to divergent estimates, prompting ongoing debates within the scientific community about the best ways to reconcile these data streams [T1].

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

The loss of 12 trillion tons of ice has profound implications for sea‑level rise. Simple calculations suggest that if all the lost ice were to melt, global sea levels could rise by roughly 3.5 meters (about 11 feet). Even a fraction of that would inundate low‑lying coastal cities, displace millions of people, and alter ocean circulation patterns. The increased freshwater input into the North Atlantic, for example, could weaken the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, with cascading effects on climate and marine ecosystems.

Policy responses to these findings are varied. The Paris Agreement, signed by nearly all nations, calls for limiting global temperature rise to well below 2 °C, but the current trajectory of emissions suggests that many countries are still on a path that would exceed this target. National mitigation plans, such as those adopted by the European Union and the United States, emphasize renewable energy and carbon pricing, yet critics argue that the pace of implementation is insufficient to curb the rapid ice loss. Indigenous communities in Greenland and Antarctica, who have lived in harmony with these environments for millennia, are among the most vocal advocates for stronger climate action, highlighting the human dimension of the scientific data [T1].

Economic interests also intersect with the ice‑loss narrative. Shipping routes, such as the Northwest Passage, are becoming more navigable as Arctic ice retreats, offering potential gains for maritime trade. However, the same environmental changes threaten fisheries, tourism, and coastal infrastructure. The tension between exploiting new opportunities and preserving fragile ecosystems underscores the complexity of translating scientific findings into policy that balances growth with stewardship.

What to watch (future implications)

Satellite technology is poised for a new era of precision. Upcoming missions, such as the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment‑Follow‑On 2 (GRACE‑FO 2) and the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑2 successor, will offer higher spatial resolution and longer time series, allowing scientists to track ice loss with even finer detail. These data will feed into climate models that are increasingly incorporating ice‑sheet dynamics, improving projections of sea‑level rise and informing adaptation strategies for vulnerable regions.

On the policy front, the next round of national commitments under the Paris Agreement will likely be scrutinized against the backdrop of the 12‑trillion‑ton loss. Countries that fail to align their emissions trajectories with the scientific evidence risk facing economic and reputational costs, while those that lead in mitigation may gain a competitive advantage in the emerging green economy. International mechanisms, such as the Green Climate Fund, will play a critical role in supporting adaptation and resilience in the most affected communities.

Finally, the scientific community continues to refine its understanding of the thresholds that could trigger runaway ice loss. The concept of tipping points—such as the collapse of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet—remains a central concern. Continuous monitoring, coupled with advances in modeling, will be essential to detect early warning signs and guide both mitigation and adaptation efforts.

In sum, the satellite record of 12 trillion tons of ice lost over nearly half a century is more than a statistic; it is a clarion call that the cryosphere is responding to human activity in measurable, accelerating ways. The challenge now lies in translating this knowledge into decisive action that protects both the planet and its people.
