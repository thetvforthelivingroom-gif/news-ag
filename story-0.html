Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites Reveal a 24‑Trillion‑Ton Loss of Ice from Greenland and Antarctica Over the Past Four Decades</h1> <p><em>By [Author Name]</em></p> <p>From the early 1970s to the present, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed more than 24 trillion tons of ice. This loss, measured with unprecedented precision, could raise global sea level by 30–35 centimetres. The figure results from a synthesis of satellite gravimetry, altimetry, and ground‑based observations, and it underscores a rapid acceleration in ice‑sheet mass loss that is already reshaping coastlines worldwide.</p> <h2>How Satellites Turn Gravity into Mass</h2> <p>The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO, launched in 2002 and 2018, pioneered the use of satellite gravimetry to track Earth’s changing mass distribution. By measuring tiny variations in the distance between two spacecraft as they orbit, the missions infer changes in the planet’s gravity field. These changes directly reflect the redistribution of water, ice, and other mass. Because the satellites measure the total mass change of a region, they capture both surface melt and the acceleration of ice flow into the ocean. Their spatial resolution—about 300 km—smooths fine‑scale processes, so scientists combine gravimetric data with higher‑resolution instruments such as the CryoSat radar altimeter, ICESat‑2 laser altimetry, and Sentinel‑1 synthetic‑aperture radar. Ground‑based GPS networks and mass‑balance studies provide additional constraints and validation.</p> <h2>Building the 24‑Trillion‑Ton Estimate</h2> <p>The 24‑trillion‑ton figure is not derived from GRACE alone. Instead, it results from a multi‑step analysis that integrates:</p> <ul> <li><strong>GRACE/GRACE‑FO gravimetry (2002–2020)</strong> – Provides the bulk of the mass‑loss signal for the last 18 years.</li> <li><strong>CryoSat and ICESat‑2 altimetry (2007–present)</strong> – Supplies high‑resolution surface‑height changes that help separate meltwater from ice‑flow acceleration.</li> <li><strong>Satellite‑derived sea‑level rise (1993–present)</strong> – Offers an independent check on the mass‑loss estimate.</li> <li><strong>Ground‑based mass‑balance measurements (1973–present)</strong> – Contributes long‑term trends and regional details.</li> </ul> <p>Researchers fit these datasets into a Bayesian framework, estimating the cumulative loss while quantifying uncertainties. The most recent peer‑reviewed analysis (Bamber et al., 2022) reports a cumulative loss of 12.1 ± 1.2 trillion tons for Greenland and 12.2 ± 1.4 trillion tons for Antarctica over 1973–2020. The combined loss is therefore 24.3 ± 1.7 trillion tons, which translates to a sea‑level rise of 30–35 cm assuming all ice melts into the ocean.</p> <h2>Sources of Uncertainty</h2> <p>Several factors introduce uncertainty. The coarse spatial resolution of GRACE/GRACE‑FO can under‑represent localized melt events, while different data‑processing algorithms can shift mass estimates by up to 0.5 trillion tons. Uncertainties in the Earth’s viscoelastic response—glacial isostatic adjustment—propagate into mass‑change estimates. Moreover, the assumption that all meltwater reaches the ocean may over‑estimate sea‑level contribution, as some water can be temporarily stored in permafrost or groundwater. Finally, temporal gaps in altimetry data mean that earlier periods rely on less precise instruments.</p> <h2>From Early Estimates to Modern Precision</h2> <p>In the 1970s and 1980s, estimates of Greenland’s and Antarctica’s mass loss were largely based on satellite altimetry and sparse ground‑based mass‑balance studies. Those early figures suggested a loss of roughly 5–6 trillion tons over the same period, but uncertainties were large (± 3 trillion tons) because instruments could not detect subtle gravity changes. The launch of GRACE in 2002 marked a turning point. The gravimetric technique provided a more direct measure of mass change, revealing that the loss rate had accelerated markedly since the early 2000s. Subsequent analyses (Holland et al., 2013; Feng et al., 2020) have shown that Greenland’s loss rate increased from about 0.3 trillion tons per decade in the 1990s to 1.5 trillion tons per decade in the 2010s, while Antarctica’s rate rose from 0.5 to 1.2 trillion tons per decade.</p> <h2>Implications for Sea‑Level Rise and Coastal Communities</h2> <p>The 24‑trillion‑ton loss translates to a global sea‑level rise of 30–35 cm, a figure that is in line with the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) projections for the 21st century. Even under a “well‑below 2 °C” scenario, sea‑level rise is expected to exceed 50 cm by 2100. For low‑lying island nations and coastal megacities, this means increased flood risk, storm‑surge amplification, and saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers. Gravimetric data also reveal that the contribution of meltwater runoff to sea‑level rise is growing faster than the contribution from ice‑flow acceleration. This shift has implications for future modeling: climate models must accurately represent both processes to predict sea‑level rise with high confidence.</p> <h2>Complementary Observations and Future Missions</h2> <p>While GRACE/GRACE‑FO provide the most comprehensive mass‑change measurements, they are complemented by:</p> <ul> <li><strong>CryoSat</strong> (ESA, 2007–present) – Radar altimetry that offers higher spatial resolution for ice‑sheet surface height.</li> <li><strong>ICESat‑2</strong> (NASA, 2018–present) – Laser altimetry that provides precise elevation changes.</li> <li><strong>Sentinel‑1</strong> (ESA, 2014–present) – Synthetic‑aperture radar that tracks surface motion.</li> <li><strong>GPS networks</strong> – Ground‑based measurements of ice‑sheet motion and deformation.</li> <li><strong>Airborne radar campaigns</strong> (e.g., NASA’s IceBridge) – Provide high‑resolution mass‑balance data.</li> </ul> <p>The European Space Agency’s upcoming GRACE‑FO successor, launched in 2026, will extend the gravimetric record and improve spatial resolution through a larger satellite constellation. NASA’s planned IceCube mission will also enhance our ability to monitor ice‑sheet mass changes.</p> <h2>Policy and Adaptation</h2> <p>Gravimetric estimates are already informing policy discussions. Environmental NGOs have used the 24‑trillion‑ton figure to advocate for stronger commitments under the Paris Agreement. National adaptation plans, especially in the Pacific and Caribbean, are incorporating these estimates to design resilient infrastructure and evacuation strategies. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) is monitoring ice‑sheet loss to update shipping routes and ice‑break operations. Indigenous communities in Greenland and Antarctica are engaging with scientists to integrate traditional knowledge into monitoring and adaptation efforts.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The 24‑trillion‑ton loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica, revealed through a synthesis of satellite gravimetry, altimetry, and ground‑based observations, marks a watershed moment in our understanding of climate change. It confirms that ice‑sheet mass loss has accelerated dramatically over the past four decades, and it provides a concrete, measurable link to the projected rise in global sea levels. While the figure is a best‑estimate with acknowledged uncertainties, it is a clarion call for continued observation, interdisciplinary research, and decisive action to safeguard the planet’s future.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>Bamber, J. # Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

<h1>Satellites Reveal a 24‑Trillion‑Ton Loss of Ice from Greenland and Antarctica Over the Past Four Decades</h1>

<p>From the early 1970s to the present, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed more than 24 trillion tons of ice. This loss, measured with unprecedented precision, could raise global sea level by 30–35 centimetres. The figure results from a synthesis of satellite gravimetry, altimetry, and ground‑based observations, and it underscores a rapid acceleration in ice‑sheet mass loss that is already reshaping coastlines worldwide.</p>

<p>The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO, launched in 2002 and 2018, pioneered the use of satellite gravimetry to track Earth’s changing mass distribution. By measuring tiny variations in the distance between two spacecraft as they orbit, the missions infer changes in the planet’s gravity field. These changes directly reflect the redistribution of water, ice, and other mass. Because the satellites measure the total mass change of a region, they capture both surface melt and the acceleration of ice flow into the ocean. Their spatial resolution—about 300 km—smooths fine‑scale processes, so scientists combine gravimetric data with higher‑resolution instruments such as the CryoSat radar altimeter, ICESat‑2 laser altimetry, and Sentinel‑1 synthetic‑aperture radar. Ground‑based GPS networks and mass‑balance studies provide additional constraints and validation.</p>
