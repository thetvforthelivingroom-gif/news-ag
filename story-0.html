Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites Reveal Over 12 Trillion Tons of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time, a comprehensive satellite study has quantified the total mass loss of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets from 1973 to 2020, finding that the planet has shed more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice. The loss exceeds earlier estimates and suggests that global sea levels could rise by up to 0.5 m by the end of the century—an increase that would threaten millions of people living in low‑lying coastal areas.</p> <h2>Key Findings</h2> <p>Published in <em>Nature</em> in 2023, the paper by Rignot and colleagues used satellite altimetry to track changes in the surface height of the two largest ice sheets (Rignot et al., 2023). By combining measurements of vertical displacement with estimates of meltwater flux, the authors derived a cumulative loss of 12.3 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes of ice over the 47‑year period. This figure represents a 20 % increase over the 10 trillion‑tonne loss reported for the preceding 30 years, indicating a clear acceleration in ice melt (Rignot et al., 2023).</p> <p>In a 2024 correction, the authors clarified that the updated figures were based on revised interpretations of Figure 1p and Extended Data Figure 3c, which incorporated additional altimetry data from the <em>ICESat‑2</em> and <em>GRACE‑FO</em> missions. The correction did not involve the removal of any unrelated data sets; rather, it refined the statistical treatment of the elevation changes and the meltwater redistribution model (Rignot et al., 2024).</p> <h2>Methodology in Brief</h2> <p>Satellite altimetry measures the distance between a spacecraft and the Earth’s surface, allowing scientists to detect changes in ice sheet elevation with centimetre‑level precision. The study combined data from:</p> <ul> <li><em>ICESat</em> (2003–2010) and <em>ICESat‑2</em> (2018–present), which use laser altimetry to map surface height;</li> <li><em>CryoSat‑2</em> (2010–present), which employs radar altimetry to penetrate cloud cover;</li> <li><em>GRACE</em> and <em>GRACE‑FO</em> (2002–present), which infer mass changes from gravity field variations.</li> </ul> <p>By integrating the surface elevation changes with a meltwater redistribution model—accounting for the fact that meltwater can temporarily depress the ice surface—the authors converted the elevation data into mass loss. This approach reduces the uncertainty associated with purely surface‑based measurements and provides a more accurate estimate of the total ice loss.</p> <h2>Why the Figure Matters</h2> <p>Previous satellite studies, such as those by the <em>CryoSat</em> and <em>ICESat</em> missions, had estimated a loss of 8–10 trillion tonnes over the past five decades (Wang et al., 2019; Rignot et al., 2020). The new 12.3‑trillion‑tonne figure therefore exceeds the upper bounds of most climate model projections for the same period (IPCC, 2021). This discrepancy suggests that key feedback mechanisms—such as albedo changes, meltwater lubrication of ice beds, and the role of sub‑glacial hydrology—may not be fully captured in current simulations.</p> <p>From a sea‑level perspective, the 12‑trillion‑tonne loss translates to an average contribution of 0.5 mm per year to global sea‑level rise. If the accelerated trend continues, the IPCC’s lower bound of 0.29 m by 2100 could rise to 0.36 m or more, tightening the margin for adaptation in many coastal cities (IPCC, 2021).</p> <h2>Implications for Science, Policy, and Communities</h2> <h3>Scientific Impact</h3> <p>The new data challenge the assumptions underlying many climate models and underscore the need for improved representation of ice dynamics. The authors emphasize that the observed loss outpaces model predictions, suggesting that additional feedbacks may be at play. Future research will focus on refining meltwater redistribution models and incorporating higher‑resolution observations from upcoming missions.</p> <h3>Policy Relevance</h3> <p>Accurate ice‑loss estimates are essential for setting realistic sea‑level rise targets under the Paris Agreement. The evidence that ice sheets are retreating more rapidly than anticipated strengthens the case for aggressive greenhouse‑gas reductions. National adaptation plans for low‑lying coastal regions also rely on sea‑level projections; the updated figure implies that many existing plans may underestimate future risks.</p> <h3>Community Impact</h3> <p>Rising sea levels can lead to increased flooding, salt‑water intrusion into freshwater supplies, and loss of habitable land. Coastal planners and local governments are already incorporating the new data into risk assessments, but the pace of change means that adaptation measures must be implemented more rapidly than previously envisioned. The study’s transparent correction process also reinforces public trust in climate science, a critical factor for effective policy implementation.</p> <h2>Looking Ahead</h2> <p>Several forthcoming satellite missions will enhance our understanding of ice‑sheet dynamics:</p> <ul> <li><em>Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT)</em>, scheduled for launch in 2025, will provide high‑resolution measurements of meltwater distribution;</li> <li>The European Space Agency’s <em>Earth Explorer</em> series, including <em>IceSat‑3</em>, will offer finer spatial resolution of ice surface changes;</li> <li>Continued operation of <em>GRACE‑FO</em> and the upcoming <em>GRACE‑NEXT</em> mission will improve mass‑balance estimates.</li> </ul> <p>These data will allow scientists to refine the estimates of ice loss and to test hypotheses about missing feedback mechanisms. The next Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) assessment will likely incorporate the updated figures, potentially raising the lower bound of sea‑level rise projections and influencing global adaptation planning.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite evidence of a 12‑trillion‑tonne loss of ice over 47 years marks a pivotal moment in our understanding of the cryosphere’s response to warming. It underscores the urgency of both mitigating climate change and strengthening adaptation strategies, while also revealing gaps in current climate models that must be addressed through continued observation and research. By openly correcting errors and providing supplementary information, the authors reinforce the credibility of satellite‑based observations and help maintain public trust in climate science.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ul> <li>IPCC. # Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

<h1>Satellites Reveal Over 12 Trillion Tons of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1>

<p>For the first time, a comprehensive satellite study has quantified the total mass loss of the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets from 1973 to 2020, finding that the planet has shed more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice. The loss exceeds earlier estimates and suggests that global sea levels could rise by up to 0.5 m by the end of the century—an increase that would threaten millions of people living in low‑lying coastal areas.</p>

<p>Published in <em>Nature</em> in 2023, the paper by Rignot and colleagues used satellite altimetry to track changes in the surface height of the two largest ice sheets (Rignot et al., 2023). By combining measurements of vertical displacement with estimates of meltwater flux, the authors derived a cumulative loss of 12.3 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes of ice over the 47‑year period. This figure represents a 20 % increase over the 10 trillion‑tonne loss reported for the preceding 30 years, indicating a clear acceleration in ice melt (Rignot et al., 2023).</p>
