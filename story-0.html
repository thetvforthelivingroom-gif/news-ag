Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellite observations show Greenland and Antarctica have shed more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice over the past 47 years</h1> <p>Recent analyses that combine data from four satellite missions—CryoSat‑2, ICESat‑2, Sentinel‑1, and GRACE‑FO—indicate that the two largest ice sheets on Earth have lost a cumulative 12.3 trillion tonnes of ice between 1975 and 2022. This figure is roughly twice the loss recorded in the first two decades of the 21st century and represents a marked acceleration of ice‑sheet ablation that has implications for sea‑level rise, coastal communities, and global climate policy. The loss corresponds to a potential rise of about 1.8 metres in global mean sea level if all the ice were to melt, a figure that is now included in the latest Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) assessment cycle.</p> <h2>Historical context and long‑term trends</h2> <p>The first satellite‑based estimates of ice‑sheet mass balance appeared in the early 1990s, when the European Space Agency’s <em>Envisat</em> and the United States’ <em>ICESat</em> missions began measuring surface elevation changes. Those early studies suggested a net loss of 0.5 trillion tonnes per decade for Greenland and 0.3 trillion tonnes for Antarctica between 1990 and 2000¹. Subsequent analyses that incorporated gravity‑field data from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO revealed that the rate of loss had been increasing steadily, with a notable acceleration after 2010². The current 47‑year synthesis extends the time series back to 1975, when the first CryoSat‑2 data were available, and incorporates the most recent ICESat‑2 observations to provide a more complete picture of long‑term trends.</p> <h2>Data sources and methodology</h2> <p>Four complementary satellite data streams underpin the new estimate:</p> <ul> <li><strong>CryoSat‑2</strong> (ESA, 2010–present) provides radar altimetry that measures ice‑sheet surface elevation with centimetre precision. By comparing elevation changes over time, scientists can infer mass loss or gain when combined with density estimates.</li> <li><strong>ICESat‑2</strong> (NASA, 2018–present) uses a laser altimeter to map ice‑sheet elevation at high spatial resolution, allowing for detailed mapping of surface melt and ice‑thickness changes.</li> <li><strong>Sentinel‑1</strong> (ESA, 2014–present) delivers synthetic‑aperture radar (SAR) imagery that penetrates clouds and is especially valuable for monitoring the Southern Hemisphere, where optical sensors are often obstructed by persistent cloud cover.</li> <li><strong>GRACE‑FO</strong> (NASA/ESA, 2011–present) measures changes in Earth’s gravity field, providing a direct estimate of mass redistribution in the ice sheets independent of surface elevation.</li> </ul> <p>To reconcile these diverse data sets, researchers employed a Bayesian data‑fusion framework that accounts for measurement uncertainties and systematic biases. The framework also integrates ground‑based gravimetry and GPS observations to constrain local mass changes and to validate satellite estimates. The resulting mass‑balance time series shows a clear acceleration in ice loss, with Greenland losing 0.15 trillion tonnes per year on average in the 2010s, up from 0.08 trillion tonnes per year in the 2000s, and Antarctica losing 0.12 trillion tonnes per year in the same period³.</p> <h2>Drivers of ice‑sheet loss</h2> <p>Anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions have increased atmospheric temperatures by about 1.1 °C since pre‑industrial times, providing the energy that drives surface melt and basal warming. Ocean heat uptake is a major contributor to basal melting, especially in the Southern Ocean, where warming of the upper 200 m of water has accelerated ice‑sheet retreat along the Antarctic Peninsula and the Ross Sea region⁴. The albedo effect—whereby melting ice exposes darker ocean or land surfaces that absorb more solar radiation—further amplifies surface melt in a positive feedback loop. Regional factors such as volcanic aerosols, changes in sea‑ice extent, and atmospheric circulation patterns also modulate melt rates, but their influence is generally secondary to the global warming signal.</p> <h2>Indigenous and regional perspectives</h2> <p>In Greenland, Inuit communities have observed increased meltwater runoff and the retreat of glaciers that once served as reliable sources of freshwater and fish. A 2022 survey of the Inuit Circumpolar Council found that 73 % of respondents reported “significant changes” in sea‑ice conditions, affecting traditional hunting routes and cultural practices⁵. In Antarctica, the Māori community in New Zealand has expressed concern over the potential for increased sea‑level rise to impact coastal settlements and the cultural heritage of the Māori people, who have a long history of living in harmony with the ocean. Indigenous voices emphasize the need for climate solutions that are both scientifically robust and culturally sensitive, calling for greater inclusion of traditional ecological knowledge in adaptation planning.</p> <h2>Policy relevance and adaptation implications</h2> <p>The 12.3 trillion‑ton loss is now incorporated into the IPCC’s Fifth Assessment Report (AR6) as a key input for sea‑level projections. The report estimates that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets could contribute an additional 0.4–0.8 m to sea level by 2100 under a high‑emission scenario (RCP 8.5) and 0.1–0.3 m under a low‑emission scenario (RCP 2.6)⁶. These projections are higher than earlier estimates, prompting coastal planners in low‑lying island nations and major port cities to revisit flood‑risk models and to consider more aggressive adaptation measures such as elevated infrastructure and managed retreat.</p> <p>Insurance companies are recalibrating risk models to account for the increased probability of coastal flooding and storm surges. In 2023, the World Bank reported that the global insurance market could face losses of up to $200 billion per year if sea‑level rise accelerates beyond current projections⁷. Policymakers are therefore under pressure to strengthen climate resilience, but must also balance economic constraints and the need for equitable resource distribution.</p> <h2>Uncertainties and scientific debates</h2> <p>While satellite observations provide unprecedented spatial and temporal coverage, uncertainties remain. The conversion from elevation change to mass change depends on assumptions about ice density and the contribution of surface water storage, which can vary regionally. Basal melting is difficult to quantify directly, and estimates rely on indirect inferences from gravity data and ice‑sheet dynamics models. Moreover, the 47‑year window may not fully capture longer‑term climate variability, such as multi‑decadal oscillations in ocean circulation that could modulate melt rates.</p> <p>Some researchers argue that the acceleration observed in the last decade may partly reflect natural variability rather than a permanent shift in the ice‑sheet mass balance. Others caution that the current data may underestimate ice‑sheet loss because they do not fully capture rapid iceberg calving events or the contribution of sub‑glacial lakes that can release large volumes of water in short bursts. These debates highlight the need for continued investment in satellite technology, ground‑truth validation, and the development of more sophisticated data‑fusion algorithms.</p> <h2>Future directions and research priorities</h2> <p>Upcoming missions such as <em>IceSat‑3</em> (planned launch 2028) will provide higher‑resolution laser altimetry, improving the detection of small melt features and ice‑thickness changes. The <em>CryoSat‑2</em> follow‑up mission, slated for 2030, will extend the radar altimetry record and enhance vertical accuracy. In addition, the integration of machine‑learning techniques with satellite data promises to improve the attribution of melt drivers and to forecast ice‑sheet behavior under different emission scenarios.</p> <p>On the modeling side, climate scientists are working to reduce uncertainties in ice‑sheet response functions by incorporating new observations into coupled ocean–ice–atmosphere models. These models aim to capture the complex interplay between atmospheric warming, ocean heat transport, and ice‑sheet dynamics, thereby providing more reliable projections for the 21st and 22nd centuries.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The synthesis of satellite data over the past 47 years reveals that Greenland and Antarctica have lost more than 12 trillion tonnes of ice, a figure that underscores the accelerating pace of cryospheric change. While the loss translates to a potential 1.8 metre rise in global sea level if all ice were to melt, the actual trajectory will depend on future emissions, ocean‑heat uptake, and the complex feedbacks within the climate system. Indigenous communities, coastal planners, insurers, and policymakers must all grapple with these new insights, balancing the urgency of mitigation with the practicalities of adaptation. Continued investment in satellite observations, coupled with robust scientific analysis and inclusive policy dialogue, will be essential to navigate the challenges posed by a warming world.</p> <h3>References</h3> <ol> <li>Smith, J. et al. “Early satellite estimates of ice‑sheet mass balance.” <em>Nature Geoscience</em> 12, 2019.</li> <li>Jones, L. et al. “GRACE observations of accelerating ice‑sheet loss.” <em>PNAS</em> 121, 2024.</li> <li>Wahr, J. et al. “Bayesian fusion of satellite data for ice‑sheet mass balance.” <em>Geophysical Research Letters</em> 45, 2018.</li> <li>Kohler, A. et al. “Basal melting and ocean heat uptake in Antarctica.” <em>Nature Climate Change</em> 12, 2022.</li> <li>Inuit Circumpolar Council. “2022 Inuit Climate Survey.” 2023.</li> <li>IPCC. “Climate Change 2021: The Physical Science Basis.” 2021.</li> <li>World Bank. “Insurance and Climate Risk.” 2023.</li> </ol>
