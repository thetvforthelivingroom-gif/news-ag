Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>For the first time in human history, scientists have been able to quantify the exact amount of ice that has vanished from the world’s two largest ice sheets. By combining data from satellite gravimetry, radar altimetry, and optical imaging, researchers now estimate that Greenland and Antarctica together have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice over the past 47 years. This loss—roughly 12,000 km³ of ice—would raise global sea level by about 3.3 centimetres if all of it melted. Although the figure may seem small compared with the sheer size of the ice sheets, the pace at which it is happening is unprecedented in the instrumental record and carries profound implications for coastal communities, climate policy, and the future of the cryosphere.</p> <h2>What happened</h2> <p>Satellite missions such as GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment) and its successor GRACE‑FO (Follow‑On) have revolutionised our ability to monitor ice mass. These spacecraft measure subtle changes in Earth’s gravity field that arise when large amounts of ice melt and redistribute mass across the planet. ICESat‑2, equipped with a laser altimeter, tracks the elevation of the ice surface, while Sentinel‑1 and other radar‑altimetry missions record both height and motion of the ice. By integrating these complementary observations, scientists have converged on a single, robust estimate: Greenland has lost about 200 gigatonnes (Gt) of ice per year on average since the early 1990s, while Antarctica’s loss has ranged from 30 Gt yr⁻¹ in the 1990s to over 400 Gt yr⁻¹ in recent years as the West Antarctic Ice Sheet accelerates (Bamber et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024).</p> <p>The drivers of this loss are complex and interrelated. Rising atmospheric temperatures increase surface melt, while warmer ocean waters melt the underside of ice shelves, a process known as basal melt. As ice retreats, darker land or ocean surfaces replace the bright, reflective ice, absorbing more solar radiation and further accelerating warming—a feedback loop that amplifies the overall loss. Satellite gravimetry captures the combined effect of surface and basal melt, whereas radar altimetry isolates surface elevation changes, allowing researchers to partition the contributions of atmospheric and oceanic drivers (Hansen et al., 2022).</p> <p>Despite the robustness of the data, uncertainties remain. Early GRACE data suffered from sensor calibration drift, which can introduce small biases. Moreover, attributing the relative contributions of atmospheric versus oceanic warming is inherently challenging. Cross‑validation among missions—comparing GRACE‑FO gravimetry with ICESat‑2 altimetry—helps constrain these uncertainties, but the complex physics of ice dynamics means that the 12‑trillion‑ton figure should be viewed as the best current estimate rather than a definitive final value (Smith et al., 2023).</p> <h2>Background and context</h2> <p>To put the loss into perspective, the combined volume of Greenland and Antarctica in the early 20th century was about 29.6 million km³ (Greenland ~2.6 million km³; Antarctica ~27 million km³). The 12,000 km³ lost over the past 47 years represents roughly 0.04 % of that total volume. While the absolute volume is small relative to the ice sheets’ size, the rate of loss—averaging 200 Gt yr⁻¹ for Greenland and up to 400 Gt yr⁻¹ for Antarctica—exceeds the natural variability observed over the last few millennia, underscoring the anthropogenic influence on the cryosphere (IPCC, 2021).</p> <p>Satellite gravimetry began in the 1990s with GRACE, providing the first precise measurements of ice mass change. Subsequent missions—GRACE‑FO, ICESat‑2, and Sentinel‑1—have refined these estimates and allowed for the separation of surface and basal melt components. The convergence of multiple measurement techniques strengthens confidence in the magnitude of the loss, yet the need for continued monitoring remains clear, as ice dynamics can accelerate or decelerate in response to evolving climate forcings (Bamber et al., 2022).</p> <h2>Implications for sea‑level rise</h2> <p>While the 12‑trillion‑ton loss equates to a sea‑level rise of about 3.3 cm, the ongoing trend is expected to add substantially to future rise. IPCC AR6 (2021) projects a global mean sea‑level rise of 0.3–0.5 m by 2100 under high‑emission scenarios, with the ice sheets contributing a significant fraction of that rise. Even a modest rise of 0.5 m would inundate low‑lying coastal regions, displace millions, and strain infrastructure. Coastal cities such as Miami, Jakarta, and Dhaka already experience regular flooding, and the additional sea‑level rise will exacerbate these challenges.</p> <p>Governments and planners are already responding. Many coastal jurisdictions are revising building codes, investing in sea‑walls, and exploring managed retreat strategies. International agreements, such as the Paris Agreement, aim to limit global warming to well below 2 °C, which would reduce the rate of ice loss and slow sea‑level rise. However, achieving these targets requires sustained reductions in greenhouse‑gas emissions and a global commitment to climate mitigation.</p> <h2>Future research and monitoring</h2> <p>Continued satellite observations are essential for tracking changes in the cryosphere and refining projections of sea‑level rise. Upcoming missions, such as the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment Next Generation (GRACE‑NG), promise higher spatial resolution and longer time series, which will improve our understanding of ice dynamics and the interplay between atmospheric and oceanic drivers. Ground‑based measurements, including GPS stations and ice‑core drilling, complement satellite data by providing high‑resolution, local insights into ice thickness, temperature, and meltwater pathways.</p> <p>In addition to monitoring, scientists are developing advanced models that integrate atmospheric, oceanic, and ice‑sheet physics. These models aim to simulate how ice sheets respond to different climate scenarios, helping policymakers assess the potential impacts of various mitigation pathways. By combining observational data with sophisticated modeling, the scientific community can offer more reliable guidance on future sea‑level rise and its societal consequences.</p> <p>In summary, the convergence of satellite gravimetry, radar altimetry, and optical imaging has provided a clear, quantitative picture of ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica. The loss of more than 12 trillion tons of ice over 47 years represents an unprecedented rate of change, with significant implications for sea‑level rise, coastal communities, and global climate policy. Continued monitoring, research, and international cooperation will be vital to understand and mitigate the impacts of this ongoing transformation of the Earth’s cryosphere.</p>
