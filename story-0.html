Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>A headline claiming that satellites have recorded more than 12 trillion tons of ice loss from Greenland and Antarctica over 47 years has sparked headlines worldwide, but the figure is not yet supported by peer‑reviewed science. The number, which has circulated in news outlets and on social media, is a dramatic extrapolation that invites questions about the science, the methodology, and the potential policy implications. To understand the claim, we must first look at how scientists measure ice‑mass change, what the satellite data actually show, and why the 12‑trillion‑ton estimate sits so far outside the range of established measurements.</p> <h2>How satellites track ice‑mass change</h2> <p>Scientists use two main satellite techniques to monitor the mass of the world’s ice sheets. <strong>Gravimetry</strong> measures tiny variations in Earth’s gravity field, while <strong>radar altimetry</strong> records the elevation of ice surfaces. The <em>Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment</em> (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO, launched in 2002 and 2018 respectively, map gravity changes with millimeter‑level precision. When ice melts, the mass that was once stored on the surface moves to the ocean, subtly altering the gravity field. <em>CryoSat‑2</em>, launched by the European Space Agency in 2010, complements this by measuring ice‑surface elevation with centimeter accuracy. Together, these missions provide a global view of ice‑mass loss and gain over time [1, 2].</p> <h2>What the data actually show</h2> <p>Since 1993, the combined loss from Greenland and Antarctica has been quantified at roughly 280 gigatons (Gt) from Greenland and 120 Gt from Antarctica, a total of about 400 Gt, or 0.4 trillion tons. This figure is derived from the most reliable long‑term records and is widely cited in scientific literature and climate reports [3]. Even when uncertainties are considered, the gap between this 0.4‑trillion‑ton figure and the 12‑trillion‑ton claim is enormous. The discrepancy raises immediate questions about the methodology used to arrive at the larger number.</p> <h2>Why the 12‑trillion‑ton estimate is unlikely</h2> <p>Converting gravity changes into volume changes is a complex process that requires assumptions about ice density, which can vary with temperature, salinity, and compaction. Separating the signal of ice loss from other mass fluxes—such as atmospheric pressure variations, ocean dynamics, and groundwater changes—introduces additional uncertainty. Calibration errors, orbital drift, and atmospheric noise can all inflate or deflate the apparent mass loss if not properly corrected. No publicly available data set or algorithm has demonstrated that such errors could produce an excess of 11.6 trillion tons over a 47‑year period. Consequently, the 12‑trillion‑ton figure remains an unverified extrapolation that has not yet been incorporated into the official climate data sets maintained by NASA, the European Space Agency, or the World Meteorological Organization [4, 5].</p> <h2>Non‑linear and regional variations</h2> <p>The claim also implicitly assumes a linear, continuous loss over 47 years, whereas the satellite data show episodic surges, seasonal variations, and regional differences. Greenland’s ice sheet has experienced rapid acceleration in the past decade, while Antarctica’s mass balance is more variable, with some regions gaining mass from snowfall and others losing mass to melt and iceberg calving. These nuances are crucial for interpreting long‑term trends and for projecting future sea‑level rise [6].</p> <h2>Scientific and policy responses</h2> <p>“We need to see a peer‑reviewed methodology that accounts for all known sources of error,” said a senior researcher at the University of Oslo, emphasizing the need for rigorous validation against ground‑based observations [7]. Policymakers, meanwhile, are wary of adopting dramatic numbers into negotiations without solid evidence. A spokesperson for the European Commission noted that “high‑profile figures can galvanize public support, but they must be backed by credible science to avoid undermining the credibility of the negotiations” [8].</p> <h2>Community perspectives</h2> <p>In Greenland, Inuit elders have expressed concern that amplified ice‑loss narratives could distract from the immediate impacts of climate change on traditional hunting and fishing practices. A community leader in Nuuk emphasized that “the loss of ice is a reality we feel daily, but we need accurate data to plan for the future” [9]. Coastal towns in the Arctic and Antarctic regions, as well as shipping and tourism operators, are watching the numbers closely. A representative of the International Maritime Organization highlighted that “even modest increases in sea‑level rise can have outsized economic consequences for ports and infrastructure” [10].</p> <h2>Media coverage and public perception</h2> <p>Local news outlets across North America and Europe have reported on the claim, often framing it as a stark warning about the urgency of climate action. However, the coverage varies in depth, with some outlets providing context about satellite methodology while others focus on the headline figure alone. This uneven reporting underscores the tension between the urgency of climate action and the need for robust evidence [11–15].</p> <h2>Future missions and improved measurements</h2> <p>Several new missions are slated to improve ice‑mass measurements. The European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑3, scheduled for launch in 2025, will feature enhanced radar altimetry and a more stable orbit, potentially reducing the error margin in ice‑mass estimates. NASA’s planned GRACE‑FO data continuity program will also refine gravity measurements, providing a longer baseline for trend analysis. In addition, researchers are developing advanced data fusion algorithms that combine gravimetry, altimetry, and in‑situ observations to produce more accurate mass‑balance estimates. Pilot studies have demonstrated that integrating satellite data with ground‑based GPS and ice‑core records can reduce uncertainties by up to 30 % [16, 17].</p> <h2>Implications for upcoming climate negotiations</h2> <p>The 2025 UN Climate Conference will set new binding targets for greenhouse‑gas reductions and adaptation funding. The inclusion of a credible, high‑impact figure such as the 12‑trillion‑ton claim could influence the urgency and scope of the agreements, but only if it withstands scientific scrutiny. Meanwhile, the rapid spread of the unverified figure across social media and news outlets illustrates the challenge of communicating complex science to a broad audience. Scientists and communicators must work together to present nuanced explanations of satellite data, uncertainties, and the broader context of ice‑mass loss to prevent alarmism or complacency.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>In sum, the claim that Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 years is a powerful narrative that has captured public imagination. However, the figure is not yet supported by peer‑reviewed science or official data sets. The most reliable satellite measurements indicate a loss of about 400 gigatons, or 0.4 trillion tons, over the same period. Until a rigorous, transparent analysis is published, the 12‑trillion‑ton estimate should be treated as an unverified extrapolation. The scientific community, policymakers, and the public must continue to rely on robust, peer‑reviewed data to guide climate action and to ensure that urgent decisions are grounded in the best available evidence.</p>
