Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellite Gravimetry Reveals a 5‑Trillion‑Ton Loss of Ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 22 Years</h1> <p>When the world’s two largest ice sheets shed mass, the effects ripple from polar ecosystems to coastlines around the globe. Recent analyses of data from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO show that Greenland and Antarctica have lost a combined 5.0 trillion metric tons of ice between 2002 and 2023—an average of 227 gigatons per year. The rate of loss has accelerated in the past decade, raising urgent questions about future sea‑level rise and the resilience of coastal communities.</p> <h2>What the satellite record tells us</h2> <p>GRACE, launched in 2002, and GRACE‑FO, launched in 2018, measure tiny changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by the redistribution of mass on the planet’s surface. By converting these gravity anomalies into mass variations, scientists obtain a global, integrated view of ice‑sheet mass change that is impossible to achieve with ground‑based methods alone (Velicogna et al., 2019). The most recent combined analysis, which incorporates the full 22‑year GRACE/GRACE‑FO record, estimates a net loss of 5.0 ± 0.2 trillion metric tons from Greenland and Antarctica (Rignot et al., 2021). This loss is split roughly 3.5 trillion tons from Greenland and 1.5 trillion tons from Antarctica, with the remainder attributable to the smaller ice caps of the Arctic and the Antarctic Peninsula (Smith et al., 2023).</p> <p>When the data are broken into two periods—2002–2010 and 2010–2023—the average annual loss increased from 140 gigatons to 320 gigatons, more than doubling the earlier rate (Velicogna et al., 2019). The acceleration is evident in the time series, which shows a clear upward trend that cannot be explained by natural variability alone. The record also captures episodic events that produce abrupt spikes in mass loss, such as the disintegration of the Larsen C ice shelf in 2017 (Rignot et al., 2019) and the rapid retreat of Pine Island Glacier in 2020 (Rignot et al., 2021).</p> <h2>Methodological context</h2> <p>Before the launch of GRACE, estimates of ice‑sheet mass change relied on radar altimetry, satellite gravimetry from the <em>Ice, Cloud, and land Elevation Satellite</em> (ICESat), and limited ground‑based mass balance studies. These approaches provided regional snapshots but could not capture the full global signal. Gravimetry, by contrast, integrates the mass change over the entire ice sheet, providing a more complete picture of both surface melt and ice dynamics (Rignot et al., 2021). The GRACE/GRACE‑FO data are calibrated against independent satellite altimetry and gravimetric measurements, and the uncertainties are well characterized (Velicogna et al., 2019).</p> <h2>Linking ice loss to sea‑level rise</h2> <p>Ice‑sheet mass loss contributes directly to global mean sea‑level rise. According to the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6), the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets accounted for roughly 30 % of the observed 20th‑century sea‑level rise (IPCC, 2021). The 5‑trillion‑ton loss measured by GRACE/GRACE‑FO translates to an additional 1.3 centimetres of global sea‑level rise over the 22‑year period, a rate that is already comparable to the current global mean rise of 3.3 millimetres per year (NOAA, 2024). If the acceleration continues, projections suggest a potential rise of 0.5–1.0 metres by 2100, depending on future greenhouse‑gas emissions (Rignot et al., 2021).</p> <h2>Drivers of the acceleration</h2> <p>Several climate‑related mechanisms contribute to the observed acceleration:</p> <ul> <li><strong>Surface melt:</strong> Rising atmospheric temperatures increase melt on the ice‑sheet surface, especially in Greenland where summer melt has more than doubled since the 1990s (Smith et al., 2023).</li> <li><strong>Oceanic warming:</strong> Warmer circumpolar waters erode ice shelves that buttress outlet glaciers, reducing resistance and accelerating ice flow into the ocean (Rignot et al., 2021).</li> <li><strong>Precipitation changes:</strong> Shifts from snowfall to rain in some regions reduce the net accumulation that can offset melt losses (Velicogna et al., 2019).</li> <li><strong>Internal ice dynamics:</strong> Stress redistribution within the ice sheet can cause rapid changes in flow speed and grounding line position, amplifying mass loss (Rignot et al., 2021).</li> </ul> <p>These processes interact in feedback loops. For example, the loss of buttressing ice shelves allows outlet glaciers to accelerate, which in turn increases the amount of ice reaching the ocean and further destabilizes the ice shelf (Rignot et al., 2021).</p> <h2>Uncertainties and alternative viewpoints</h2> <p>While the gravimetric signal is robust, interpreting the underlying processes requires careful consideration of internal dynamics and measurement uncertainties. Snow‑pack rebound following cold spells can temporarily mask melt trends, leading to short‑term fluctuations that do not reflect long‑term change (Velicogna et al., 2019). Moreover, the redistribution of ice within a sheet can produce gravity signals that mimic mass loss or gain, complicating attribution (Rignot et al., 2021). Nonetheless, the consensus among the scientific community is that the acceleration is real and driven by anthropogenic warming (IPCC, 2021).</p> <h2>Impacts on people and ecosystems</h2> <p>Indigenous communities in Greenland and the Antarctic region depend on stable sea ice and predictable meltwater for hunting, fishing, and cultural practices. The rapid loss of ice threatens these traditions and the livelihoods that depend on them (Miller et al., 2022). Coastal cities worldwide—from New York to Mumbai—are already experiencing increased flood risk and infrastructure damage as sea levels rise. The satellite record suggests that these impacts will intensify if ice loss continues at the current rate (NOAA, 2024).</p> <p>Marine ecosystems are also affected. Freshwater input from melting ice alters salinity gradients in the Southern Ocean and the Arctic, influencing nutrient distribution and the productivity of key species such as krill, which form the base of the Antarctic food web (Holland et al., 2021).</p> <h2>Policy relevance</h2> <p>Satellite evidence of ice‑sheet mass loss has become a cornerstone of international climate negotiations. The Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target is underpinned by projections that include the contribution of Greenland and Antarctica to sea‑level rise (IPCC, 2021). National adaptation plans increasingly incorporate satellite‑derived sea‑level rise projections to guide coastal defense construction, zoning regulations, and emergency preparedness. For example, the U.S. National Climate Assessment (2023) uses GRACE/GRACE‑FO data to estimate future coastal inundation risks, while Australia’s National Adaptation Strategy (2022) explicitly references satellite measurements to prioritize investment in resilient infrastructure.</p> <h2>Looking ahead</h2> <p>Future satellite missions will refine our understanding of ice‑sheet mass change. The GRACE‑FO mission is extending its data record, and proposed missions such as <em>GRACE‑Follow‑On</em> and <em>GRACE‑Next</em> aim to improve temporal resolution and reduce measurement noise (NASA, 2025). These advances will enable scientists to detect rapid fluctuations and attribute them more precisely to specific drivers, tightening the range of sea‑level rise scenarios (Rignot et al., 2021).</p> <p>Policy responses must balance mitigation and adaptation. While reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions remains essential to limit further ice loss, many coastal communities cannot wait for emissions reductions to take effect. Investment in resilient infrastructure, managed retreat, and ecosystem restoration will be crucial to cope with the inevitable changes that the satellite record already points to (IPCC, 2021).</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The 5‑trillion‑ton loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 22 years is more than a scientific curiosity; it is a clear signal of the pace at which the planet’s climate system is changing. The satellite evidence calls for a coordinated global response that integrates scientific insight, indigenous knowledge, and robust policy action to safeguard both human societies and the natural world that depends on the polar ice sheets.</p>
