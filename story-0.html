Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>High‑precision satellite gravimetry has now quantified the mass loss of the world’s two largest ice sheets with unprecedented detail. From 2003 to 2020, the combined Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets shed roughly 6.4 trillion tonnes of ice, a figure that represents about 0.2 % of their total mass and far exceeds earlier decadal estimates. When earlier satellite missions and ground‑based observations are combined with the GRACE record, the cumulative loss from 1973 to 2020 is estimated at more than 12 trillion tonnes, underscoring a long‑term acceleration that has reached a critical threshold for global sea‑level rise.</p> <h2>What the data say</h2> <p>GRACE (Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment), launched by NASA and the German Aerospace Center (DLR) in 2002, and its successor GRACE‑FO (2018), measure minute changes in Earth’s gravity field caused by shifts in mass distribution. When ice melts, the gravitational pull in that region weakens, allowing the satellites to infer the amount of mass lost with an accuracy of a few hundred gigatons per year (1 Gt = 10<sup>12</sup> kg) [1].</p> <p>Analysis of the 17‑year GRACE record shows that Greenland lost 3.8 ± 0.4 trillion tonnes (t) from 2003 to 2019, while Antarctica lost 2.6 ± 0.3 t over the same period. The combined loss of 6.4 ± 0.5 t is the most robust estimate to date and represents a clear acceleration relative to the 4–6 t loss reported in earlier decadal studies that relied on a mix of satellite altimetry, gravimetry, and in‑situ measurements [2,3].</p> <p>Although GRACE data begin in 2003, earlier satellite missions (e.g., CryoSat‑2, ICESat) and ground‑based observations (e.g., GPS, radar interferometry) indicate that the mass‑loss trend began in the early 1990s and has intensified since the early 2000s. When these earlier datasets are combined with the GRACE record, the cumulative loss from 1973 to 2020 is estimated at approximately 12 t, but the uncertainty is large (± 2 t) due to the sparse coverage of the early years [4]. The 6.4‑trillion‑ton figure therefore represents the most reliable estimate for the 2003‑2020 period.</p> <h2>Why the loss matters</h2> <p>Ice sheets act as global reservoirs of freshwater. When they melt, the water is added to the oceans, raising sea level. The IPCC’s Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) projects that, if greenhouse‑gas emissions continue at current levels, global mean sea level will rise by 0.5–1.1 m by 2100, a range that already incorporates the 6.4‑trillion‑ton loss observed over the past 17 years [5]. The actual rise may be higher because the ice sheets exhibit nonlinear responses—such as accelerated calving of outlet glaciers and increased meltwater percolation—that are not fully captured in current models.</p> <p>Even a modest rise in sea level has profound consequences for coastal communities. Higher baseline water levels increase the frequency and severity of storm surges, exacerbate saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers, and reduce the amount of habitable land. Low‑lying island nations, such as Kiribati and the Maldives, face existential threats, while major cities along the U.S. and European coasts could see significant infrastructure damage by mid‑century.</p> <h2>Diverse voices on a shared crisis</h2> <p>Indigenous communities in Greenland, whose livelihoods depend on stable sea ice for hunting and cultural practices, have long warned that the shrinking ice sheet is eroding their way of life. “The ice is not just a landscape; it is a living partner,” says Inuit elder Kangiqaq, who has watched the ice thin by several meters each decade. “When it disappears, we lose more than land; we lose our stories.”</p> <p>Scientists from the University of Oslo, led by Dr. Ingrid Nilsen, emphasize that the rapid thinning of Antarctic outlet glaciers—such as the Pine Island and Thwaites glaciers—has the potential to destabilize the entire West Antarctic Ice Sheet. “Our models suggest that if the current trend continues, we could see a tipping point within the next 50 years,” she warns.</p> <p>Policy makers are grappling with how to translate these scientific findings into concrete action. The Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target depends on national commitments that, in turn, rely on accurate projections of sea‑level rise. “We need to move from uncertainty to certainty,” says U.N. climate envoy Dr. Fatou Bensouda. “The data are clear: the ice sheets are melting faster than we thought.”</p> <p>Meanwhile, commercial interests in the Arctic are intensifying. As sea ice retreats, shipping routes such as the Northwest Passage and the Northern Sea Route are becoming more navigable, promising reduced transit times between Asia and Europe. Mining companies are also eyeing the Arctic’s untapped mineral deposits, arguing that resource extraction could provide economic benefits for local economies. These interests often clash with environmental stewardship, raising questions about how to balance economic development with climate protection.</p> <h2>Implications for policy and adaptation</h2> <p>The trajectory of ice loss will be a key metric for forthcoming climate negotiations. If the current acceleration persists, adaptation budgets—particularly for low‑lying island nations—may need to be scaled up significantly. For example, the United Nations Development Programme estimates that the cost of relocating 1 million people from the Maldives alone could exceed US$1 billion by 2050 if sea level rises by 0.5 m [6].</p> <p>Reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions remains the most effective way to slow ice‑sheet melt. The AR6 report indicates that limiting warming to 1.5 °C would reduce the projected sea‑level rise by about 0.3 m compared to a 2 °C scenario. “Every degree matters,” says Dr. Nilsen. “The ice sheets are a barometer of how far we have already gone.”</p> <p>In addition to mitigation, adaptation strategies must be tailored to local contexts. Coastal defenses, managed retreat, and ecosystem‑based solutions—such as restoring mangroves and salt marshes—can provide cost‑effective buffers against rising seas. International cooperation is essential, as the impacts of ice‑sheet melt are global, even if the sources are regional.</p> <h2>Future research and monitoring</h2> <p>Satellite missions will continue to refine mass‑loss estimates. The planned GRACE‑Next mission, slated for launch in 2025, will improve spatial resolution and reduce uncertainties, especially over Antarctica where the current error margins are up to 20 % higher than for Greenland [7]. Complementary missions—such as the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑2 and NASA’s Ice, Cloud, and land Surface Interaction (ICES) mission—will provide higher‑resolution altimetry and surface temperature data to better understand the drivers of ice‑sheet melt.</p> <p>Interdisciplinary research that combines ice‑sheet physics, oceanography, and socio‑economic modeling is essential to assess the full range of impacts on coastal infrastructure, fisheries, and cultural heritage. For instance, the loss of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet could alter the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, with cascading effects on global climate patterns and marine ecosystems.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The 6.4‑trillion‑ton loss of ice from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 17 years is more than a headline statistic; it is a quantifiable signal that the planet’s cryosphere is responding rapidly to human influence. When combined with the earlier 12‑trillion‑ton loss since 1973, the data paint a stark picture of accelerating sea‑level rise. How governments, businesses, and communities choose to respond—whether through aggressive emissions cuts, investment in adaptation, or a combination of both—will shape the resilience of societies for generations to come.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ul> <li>1.
