Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites Reveal That Greenland and Antarctica Have Lost About 6.5 trillion tonnes of Ice Since 1992</h1> <p>By Jane Doe, Climate Correspondent</p> <p>New analyses of data from NASA’s GRACE‑FO, ESA’s CryoSat‑2, and other space‑borne gravimeters and altimeters show that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed roughly 6.5 trillion tonnes of ice since 1992. That loss is equivalent to the weight of all the world’s coal reserves and will raise global sea level by at least 0.5 m by the end of the century, according to the latest IPCC projections. The numbers are sobering, but they also underscore the urgency of action by governments, businesses, and communities worldwide.</p> <h2>What the Numbers Tell Us</h2> <p>The combined ice loss of 6.5 trillion tonnes comes from two complementary measurement techniques. Gravimetry tracks changes in Earth’s gravity field, revealing how mass shifts as ice melts and flows. Altimetry measures the height of the ice surface, distinguishing thinning caused by melt from thinning caused by ice flow. Together, they provide a robust estimate of mass balance with an uncertainty of ±0.5 trillion tonnes (Huang et al., 2023). The trend is uneven: Greenland has lost about 3.2 trillion tonnes, while Antarctica has shed about 3.3 trillion tonnes. The loss has accelerated in the last decade, with Greenland’s melt rate doubling and Antarctica’s calving rate tripling since 2010 (Bamber & Aspinall, 2022).</p> <h2>From Ground Observations to Space‑Based Precision</h2> <p>Before satellites, scientists relied on ground stakes, aerial photography, and limited radar surveys to estimate ice loss. Those early measurements hinted at a steady decline but lacked the global coverage and temporal resolution needed to capture rapid changes. The launch of the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) in 2002 marked a turning point, providing the first full‑Earth gravimetric maps of ice mass change. CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, added high‑resolution altimetry that could detect subtle changes in surface elevation. By combining these datasets, researchers now have a continuous record of ice loss that spans more than two decades.</p> <h2>Unpacking the Uncertainties</h2> <p>While the 6.5‑trillion‑tonnes figure is robust, it is not without uncertainty. Gravimetric estimates can be confounded by changes in groundwater storage, ocean mass, and glacial isostatic adjustment. Altimetric measurements can be biased by cloud cover, snow accumulation, and the limited spatial resolution of some instruments. Researchers mitigate these issues by cross‑validating gravimetric and altimetric data, applying atmospheric corrections, and using independent ground‑based observations (Zhang et al., 2021). Even so, the error bars—±0.5 trillion tonnes—highlight that our knowledge is still evolving.</p> <h2>The Physical Drivers of Ice Loss</h2> <p>Accelerated melt and calving are driven by atmospheric warming, oceanic heat transport, and changes in atmospheric circulation. In Greenland, rising summer temperatures increase surface melt, while a warming ocean accelerates melt at the ice sheet’s margins. In Antarctica, the West Antarctic Ice Sheet is especially vulnerable because its bedrock lies below sea level, making it susceptible to marine ice sheet instability. Ocean heat, delivered by the Southern Ocean’s circumpolar currents, has thinned ice shelves and increased calving rates. These processes are amplified by albedo feedbacks: as ice recedes, darker surfaces absorb more solar radiation, further warming the region (Liu et al., 2020).</p> <h2>Policy Implications: From Science to Action</h2> <p>The scientific evidence is clear: the ice sheets are in decline, and the pace is accelerating. Yet translating data into policy is complex. The Paris Agreement sets a global temperature goal of well below 2 °C, but many countries still rely on fossil‑fuel‑based growth. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) links higher emissions scenarios (RCP8.5) to sea‑level rise of 0.61–0.98 m by 2100, while lower‑emission pathways (RCP2.6) predict 0.21–0.39 m (IPCC, 2022). These projections inform coastal planning, insurance risk models, and climate finance mechanisms such as the Green Climate Fund. However, a gap remains between the availability of satellite data and the implementation of adaptation measures. Many municipalities still use outdated sea‑level rise estimates, underestimating future risks (Wang & Liu, 2023).</p> <h2>Indigenous Perspectives and the Human Dimension</h2> <p>The loss of ice is not just a scientific curiosity; it is a human crisis. Indigenous communities in Greenland, the Canadian Arctic, and Antarctica’s research stations face changing landscapes, altered hunting patterns, and cultural erosion. In Greenland, the rapid retreat of glaciers threatens traditional fishing grounds and the integrity of cultural heritage sites. In Antarctica, the collapse of ice shelves could disrupt the ecosystems that support scientific research and global biodiversity. These communities are often at the forefront of climate advocacy, demanding that their voices be heard in international negotiations (Kern et al., 2021).</p> <h2>Looking Ahead: Tipping Points and Future Scenarios</h2> <p>The question of tipping points—points of no return—remains a focal point of research. The West Antarctic Ice Sheet’s marine ice sheet instability could trigger a rapid, irreversible rise in sea level if the ice shelf collapses. Models suggest that such an event could add 0.5–1.0 m to global sea level over the next few centuries (Rahmstorf et al., 2022). Meanwhile, the Antarctic Peninsula is already experiencing a steady retreat of ice shelves, a sign that the region is approaching a critical threshold. Scientists are calling for next‑generation missions, such as the proposed Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment–Next Generation (GRACE‑NG) and CryoSat‑3, to provide higher‑resolution data and reduce uncertainties (ESA, 2024).</p> <h2>The Role of International Agreements</h2> <p>International climate agreements provide the framework for reducing emissions and financing adaptation. The Paris Agreement’s “loss and damage” mechanism aims to address impacts that cannot be avoided. The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) has also established the Adaptation Fund to help vulnerable countries implement climate‑resilient projects. Yet the effectiveness of these mechanisms depends on robust scientific data. Satellites offer the most reliable, global, and continuous measurements of ice loss, making them indispensable for informing policy decisions.</p> <h2>Conclusion: A Call for Continued Observation and Action</h2> <p>The satellite record shows that Greenland and Antarctica have already lost 6.5 trillion tonnes of ice—a figure that dwarfs the world’s coal reserves and will raise sea level by at least 0.5 m by the end of the century. The data are clear, the uncertainties are manageable, and the stakes are high. Governments, businesses, and communities must use this evidence to accelerate emissions reductions, invest in adaptation, and support vulnerable populations. The next decade will be decisive: will we act on the data, or will the ice sheets continue to melt, reshaping our planet for generations to come?</p> <h3>References</h3> <ul> <li>Bamber, J. # Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

<h1>Satellites Reveal That Greenland and Antarctica Have Lost About 6.5 trillion tonnes of Ice Since 1992</h1>

<p>New analyses of data from NASA’s GRACE‑FO, ESA’s CryoSat‑2, and other space‑borne gravimeters and altimeters show that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed roughly 6.5 trillion tonnes of ice since 1992. That loss is equivalent to the weight of all the world’s coal reserves and will raise global sea level by at least 0.5 m by the end of the century, according to the latest IPCC projections. The numbers are sobering, but they also underscore the urgency of action by governments, businesses, and communities worldwide.</p>

<p>The combined ice loss of 6.5 trillion tonnes comes from two complementary measurement techniques. Gravimetry tracks changes in Earth’s gravity field, revealing how mass shifts as ice melts and flows. Altimetry measures the height of the ice surface, distinguishing thinning caused by melt from thinning caused by ice flow. Together, they provide a robust estimate of mass balance with an uncertainty of ±0.5 trillion tonnes (Huang et al., 2023). The trend is uneven: Greenland has lost about 3.2 trillion tonnes, while Antarctica has shed about 3.3 trillion tonnes. The loss has accelerated in the last decade, with Greenland’s melt rate doubling and Antarctica’s calving rate tripling since 2010 (Bamber & Aspinall, 2022).</p>
