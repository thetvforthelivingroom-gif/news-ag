Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<article> <h1>Satellites reveal the world’s largest ice sheets have shed over 12 trillion tons of ice in just 27 years</h1> <p>Since the late 1990s, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have been losing mass at an accelerating pace. Space‑borne gravimetry, laser altimetry, and radar interferometry now show that Greenland has lost roughly 3.8 trillion tons of ice, while Antarctica has shed about 8.5 trillion tons—totaling more than 12 trillion tons from 1997 to 2024. The loss is already nudging global sea level higher and threatens coastal communities worldwide.</p> <h2>What the Numbers Tell Us</h2> <p>The figure of 12 trillion tons comes from a synthesis of several space‑borne missions. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO measured minute changes in Earth’s gravity field, allowing scientists to infer mass loss from the ice sheets. Complementary data from ICESat‑2, which maps surface elevation with laser altimetry, and CryoSat‑2, which tracks ice thickness, provide cross‑validation and help correct for calibration drift and regional variability.1 Together, these datasets show Greenland’s loss at 3.8 trillion tons and Antarctica’s at 8.5 trillion tons over the period 1997–2024.2</p> <p>The decline is uneven. Greenland’s mass loss has accelerated since the late 1990s, with the ice sheet losing an average of 0.6 km of ice thickness per year in recent decades.3 Antarctica’s decline is more heterogeneous; the West Antarctic Ice Sheet has experienced rapid thinning and accelerated iceberg calving, whereas the East Antarctic Ice Sheet remains relatively stable but is not immune to warming.4</p> <h2>Historical Context and Earlier Measurements</h2> <p>Before satellite gravimetry, estimates of ice loss relied on ground‑based mass balance studies, radar interferometry, and satellite altimetry. These earlier observations already indicated a long‑term trend of retreat, but lacked the global coverage and precision of GRACE. The satellite record began in 1997, a few years after atmospheric CO₂ concentrations surpassed 400 ppm—a threshold associated with significant anthropogenic warming.5 Since then, global temperatures have risen by about 1.2 °C, with the Arctic warming twice as fast as the global average.6</p> <p>Atmospheric circulation changes, such as a weakening polar vortex, have also contributed to ice loss. A more erratic vortex allows warmer mid‑latitude air masses to spill into polar regions, increasing surface melt and reducing snowfall that would otherwise replenish the ice sheets.7 These mechanisms are intertwined; ocean heat uptake amplifies surface melt, while atmospheric changes modulate the distribution of precipitation and temperature over the ice sheets.8</p> <h2>Implications for Sea Level and Climate</h2> <p>Mass loss directly translates into sea‑level rise. Gravitational modeling indicates that the 12 trillion‑ton loss has already contributed roughly 0.3 m to global sea levels, and projections suggest that continued ice loss could raise sea levels by an additional 0.3–0.6 m by 2100, depending on future emissions pathways and ice‑sheet dynamics.9 The rate of rise is already outpacing many adaptation plans, underscoring the urgency of both mitigation and adaptation measures.</p> <h2>Perspectives from Indigenous Communities</h2> <p>For many Indigenous peoples of Greenland and the surrounding Antarctic region, the ice sheets are not merely geological features but cultural keystones. The disappearance of sea ice and the retreat of glaciers alter hunting patterns, disrupt traditional knowledge systems, and threaten the very identity of communities that have lived in harmony with these landscapes for millennia.10 Indigenous leaders have called for climate finance mechanisms that recognize these cultural losses and invest in adaptation measures such as resilient infrastructure and community‑led monitoring programs.11</p> <h2>Policy Context: Loss, Damage, and Adaptation</h2> <p>The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, obligates signatory countries to limit global warming to well below 2 °C. However, the agreement’s “loss and damage” clause remains a point of contention, as nations grapple with how to fund adaptation for those most affected by sea‑level rise.12 The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) reports underscore the urgency of both mitigation and adaptation, noting that without significant reductions in greenhouse‑gas emissions, the projected ice‑sheet loss will accelerate, exacerbating economic and social vulnerabilities in low‑lying nations.13</p> <h2>Scientific Uncertainty and the Need for Continued Monitoring</h2> <p>While satellite data provide robust evidence of ice loss, uncertainties persist. Calibration drift in GRACE instruments, the limited spatial resolution of laser altimetry, and the complex interplay of atmospheric and oceanic processes mean that projections carry error margins. Nevertheless, the consensus among climate scientists is that the trend is unequivocal and that policy responses must be commensurate with the magnitude of the threat. The scientific community emphasizes the need for continued monitoring and investment in next‑generation satellite missions to refine estimates and reduce uncertainties.14</p> <h2>Corporate Interests and Resource Extraction</h2> <p>The melting of polar ice opens new maritime routes and access to untapped mineral resources, attracting corporate interest in shipping, mining, and energy extraction. Indigenous groups and environmental NGOs warn that such activities could accelerate ice loss and undermine international climate goals. Balancing economic development with environmental stewardship remains a central policy dilemma for governments in the Arctic and Antarctic regions.15</p> <h2>Future Outlook: Missions, Policy, and Participation</h2> <h3>Ongoing and Planned Satellite Missions</h3> <p>GRACE‑FO, launched in 2018, continues to provide high‑precision gravimetric data and is expected to operate until 2025.16 ICESat‑2, launched in 2018, offers laser altimetry with unprecedented vertical accuracy, while CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, continues to deliver ice‑thickness estimates.17 Sentinel‑1, part of the European Copernicus program, provides radar interferometry that complements gravimetry by mapping surface deformation.18 In addition, NASA’s IceBridge airborne campaign, which has flown since 2009, fills gaps between satellite missions by collecting radar, lidar, and mass‑balance data.19</p> <h3>Policy Pathways</h3> <p>To address the dual challenges of mitigation and adaptation, policymakers must strengthen climate finance mechanisms, enforce stricter emissions limits, and support community‑led adaptation projects. The United Nations’ Loss and Damage Fund, established under the Paris Agreement, offers a framework for financing adaptation in vulnerable countries, but its governance and funding streams require further development.20</p> <h3>Engaging Indigenous Knowledge</h3> <p>Incorporating Indigenous knowledge systems into monitoring and policy design can improve the accuracy of ice‑sheet models and enhance community resilience. Collaborative projects that pair satellite data with local observations—such as glacial retreat measurements and traditional ecological knowledge—have already yielded richer insights into ice‑sheet dynamics.21</p> <h3>Corporate Responsibility</h3> <p>Companies operating in polar regions must adopt stringent environmental standards, conduct comprehensive impact assessments, and engage with local communities. International agreements, such as the Polar Code for shipping and the Antarctic Treaty System, provide regulatory frameworks that can be strengthened to mitigate environmental impacts.22</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>Satellites have made it clear that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are shrinking at an alarming rate, with more than 12 trillion tons of ice lost in just 27 years. This loss is already raising sea levels, threatening coastal communities, and reshaping ecosystems. Addressing this crisis requires coordinated action—scientific, political, and societal. Continued monitoring, robust policy frameworks, and the inclusion of Indigenous perspectives are essential to safeguard the planet’s future.</p> <h2>References</h2> <ol> <li>R. R.
