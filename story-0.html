Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
Satellites have recorded a staggering 12 trillion tons of ice lost from Greenland and Antarctica over the past 47 years—an amount that far exceeds earlier predictions and signals a rapid acceleration of sea‑level rise that could reshape coastlines worldwide.

What happened

From 1975 to 2022, a coordinated network of Earth‑orbiting satellites measured a cumulative loss of more than 12 trillion metric tons of ice from the planet’s two largest ice sheets. The data come mainly from the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO, as well as the laser altimetry of ICESat‑2. These missions provide the most precise, continuous record of ice‑mass changes to date.

The loss is not evenly distributed. Greenland has shed roughly 6.7 trillion tons, while Antarctica has contributed about 5.5 trillion tons. The difference reflects both the larger surface area of the Antarctic ice sheet and its more complex dynamics.

The satellite measurements reveal a clear, accelerating trend. In the early 1990s, the combined mass loss averaged about 0.1 trillion tons per year. By the 2010s, the rate had more than doubled to 0.4 trillion tons per year, and the most recent decade shows a further uptick to 0.6 trillion tons annually. This acceleration aligns with the observed rise in global temperatures and the increasing intensity of atmospheric and oceanic warming.

Background and context

The GRACE mission, launched in 2002, measured variations in Earth’s gravity field caused by changes in mass distribution, including the movement of ice. Its successor, GRACE‑FO, launched in 2018, extended the record and improved precision. ICESat‑2, also launched in 2018, uses a laser to map the surface elevation of ice sheets, providing complementary data on surface height changes. Together, these missions form a continuous, high‑resolution record that has become the gold standard for monitoring ice‑mass balance.

Historically, ice sheets were considered relatively stable over the past century. However, satellite data since the 1990s have shown a clear shift toward rapid decline. Earlier projections, such as those from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Fifth Assessment Report, estimated that Greenland could lose 0.1–0.3 trillion tons per year by 2100 under a high‑emissions scenario. The current satellite record indicates that the actual loss is already exceeding the upper bound of those projections, suggesting that the ice sheets are responding more quickly to warming than previously thought.

The drivers of this acceleration are multifaceted. Anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions have increased atmospheric concentrations of CO₂ and other heat‑trapping gases, raising global mean temperatures. This warming enhances surface melt on Greenland and the Antarctic Peninsula, while oceanic heat uptake amplifies basal melt beneath Antarctic ice shelves. Changes in atmospheric circulation patterns—such as the weakening of the polar vortex and the intensification of mid‑latitude storm tracks—also influence precipitation and melt patterns over the ice sheets.

The bigger picture (diverse perspectives)

Climate policy and sea‑level rise

The satellite‑derived ice loss translates directly into sea‑level rise. Global sea level has risen by about 3.3 mm per year over the past decade, with the contribution from ice sheets estimated at roughly 0.5 mm per year. If the current rate of ice loss persists, projections for 2100 sea‑level rise could exceed 1 meter, surpassing many earlier estimates and posing significant risks to low‑lying coastal cities. This has prompted policymakers to revisit adaptation budgets, infrastructure resilience plans, and insurance models.

Indigenous and local community impacts

For Greenlandic Inuit communities, the loss of ice is not only a scientific concern but also a cultural one. The ice sheet supports traditional hunting grounds and migratory routes for marine mammals. In Antarctica, research stations and the broader scientific community rely on stable ice conditions for logistics and safety. The accelerated melt threatens to alter the ecosystems that these communities depend on and to increase the frequency of hazardous events such as ice calving and glacial lake outburst floods.

Economic implications

Rapid ice loss opens new maritime routes, such as the Northwest Passage and Trans‑Antarctic routes, potentially reducing shipping times between North America and Asia. While this could lower fuel consumption and costs, it also raises environmental concerns about increased shipping traffic, potential oil spills, and disturbances to fragile polar ecosystems. Insurance companies are recalibrating risk models to account for the higher probability of extreme sea‑level events.

Scientific debates and data reliability

While satellite data provide unprecedented coverage, some scientists caution about the interpretation of gravity‑based mass changes, which can be influenced by groundwater fluctuations and changes in ocean mass. Ground‑based measurements, such as GPS stations and tide gauges, sometimes show discrepancies with satellite estimates, prompting ongoing efforts to reconcile datasets. Nonetheless, the convergence of multiple satellite missions strengthens confidence in the overall trend of rapid ice loss.

What to watch (future implications)

Continued monitoring and mission longevity

The GRACE‑FO mission is slated to continue through 2025, after which the scientific community is planning successor missions, such as the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment Next Generation (GRACE‑NG). Maintaining continuous coverage is essential for tracking the evolving mass balance and for refining sea‑level rise projections.

Integration into climate models

Climate models are increasingly incorporating satellite observations to improve their representation of ice‑sheet dynamics. By feeding real‑time mass‑loss data into models, scientists can better simulate future scenarios under different emissions pathways, informing policy decisions at national and international levels.

Policy responses and mitigation targets

The stark evidence of accelerated ice loss underscores the urgency of limiting global warming to 1.5 °C or 2 °C, as outlined in the Paris Agreement. Policymakers are using satellite data to justify stricter emissions reductions, to secure funding for adaptation projects, and to negotiate international climate finance mechanisms.

Indigenous knowledge and co‑management

Incorporating traditional ecological knowledge from Greenlandic Inuit and other polar communities can enhance monitoring efforts and improve the relevance of adaptation strategies. Co‑management frameworks that blend scientific data with local insights are emerging as a promising approach to address the complex challenges posed by ice loss.

Economic and infrastructural adaptation

Coastal cities worldwide are reassessing building codes, flood defenses, and evacuation plans in light of updated sea‑level rise projections. Insurance regulators are adjusting premiums and coverage requirements, while shipping companies are evaluating the feasibility and environmental impact of new polar routes.

In sum, the satellite record of more than 12 trillion tons of ice lost over 47 years is a sobering reminder of the rapid pace at which Earth’s cryosphere is responding to human‑induced warming. The data not only refine our scientific understanding but also provide a concrete basis for policy action, community resilience, and global cooperation in the face of a changing climate.
