Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<article> For the first time, scientists can quantify exactly how much ice has vanished from the world’s polar caps. Satellite gravimetry has turned the abstract idea of “ice loss” into a precise, measurable quantity. Over the past 47 years, the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice, a loss that could raise global mean sea level by 3–4 centimetres by 2100 and accelerate the climate crisis ahead of the next UN climate conference. The figure, derived from a continuous record of Earth’s gravity field, is the most accurate estimate available to date and underscores the urgency of limiting greenhouse‑gas emissions.

What the data show

The 12‑trillion‑ton loss is based on measurements from a series of gravimetric satellites that began in 1997 with the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and continued with its successor, GRACE‑Follow‑On (GRACE‑FO). Complementary missions such as CryoSat‑2 and GCOM‑W provide radar altimetry and sea‑ice data that help separate surface melt from dynamic ice‑sheet collapse. The satellites detect minute changes in the planet’s gravitational pull, which directly reflect mass variations on the surface. When ice sheets lose mass—whether through surface melt or the rapid disintegration of ice streams—the gravitational signature changes in a measurable way. The combined data from these missions indicate that Greenland and Antarctica together have lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice over the past 47 years, a loss that is now the most precise estimate available [1].

The loss is not uniform across the two polar regions. Greenland’s mass loss has accelerated in recent decades, driven largely by surface melt and the retreat of outlet glaciers. Antarctica’s loss is more complex, involving both surface melt in the West Antarctic Ice Sheet and dynamic ice‑sheet collapse in the East Antarctic Ice Sheet, particularly in the Ross and Filchner–Ronne sectors. The gravimetric data distinguish between these processes by separating the slow, steady loss of surface meltwater from the faster, episodic loss associated with ice‑sheet collapse. However, uncertainties remain, especially in regions where satellite coverage is sparse or where atmospheric loading can mimic mass changes. These uncertainties are quantified in the error bars that accompany the 12‑trillion‑ton figure [1].

The 47‑year record began with the launch of GRACE in 1997 and has been extended by GRACE‑FO, which launched in 2018. The continuity of the dataset has allowed scientists to track not only the cumulative loss but also the temporal variability of ice mass changes, revealing periods of accelerated loss that coincide with warmer global temperatures.

How the measurements work

Before the advent of gravimetric satellites, estimates of polar ice loss relied on a combination of satellite altimetry, in‑situ measurements, and model reconstructions. These methods yielded a wide range of values, often with large uncertainties, and tended to underestimate the true magnitude of ice loss. The satellite gravimetry record has dramatically refined these estimates, showing a clear jump in observed loss since the 1990s. The first GRACE data release in 2005 already indicated a significant acceleration in Greenland’s mass loss, a trend that has continued in subsequent releases [2].

Measurement methodology is grounded in the principle that a change in mass on the Earth’s surface alters its gravitational field. GRACE and GRACE‑FO measure this change by tracking the distance between two satellites in the same orbit as they pass over the same region. CryoSat‑2, on the other hand, uses radar altimetry to measure the height of the ice surface, while GCOM‑W provides complementary data on sea‑ice extent and thickness. By combining these datasets, scientists can separate the contributions of surface melt, ice‑sheet dynamics, and oceanic changes to the overall mass budget. The main sources of uncertainty include atmospheric pressure variations, ocean loading, and the limited spatial resolution of the gravimetric measurements, which can blur fine‑scale features such as individual glacier termini [3].

The 47‑year satellite record also provides a baseline against which to compare future changes. With the launch of GRACE‑FO and the ongoing operation of CryoSat‑2, the data stream will continue for at least the next decade, allowing scientists to monitor whether the rate of loss accelerates, stabilizes, or reverses.

The bigger picture

The 12‑trillion‑ton loss translates into a potential rise in global mean sea level of roughly 3–4 centimetres by 2100 under current emission trajectories. This estimate is derived by converting the mass loss into an equivalent sea‑level rise, accounting for the density of ice and the volume of the oceans. The projected rise is significant for low‑lying coastal communities, island nations, and major port cities, where even a few centimetres of additional sea level can increase the frequency and severity of flooding, erosion, and saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers. Ecosystems such as mangroves, salt marshes, and coral reefs are also vulnerable to the compounded effects of sea‑level rise and increased storm intensity, which are expected to accompany the loss of polar ice [4].

Policy relevance is immediate. The satellite data underpin the latest Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) assessment reports, informing the science that drives international climate negotiations. The figures also feed into national adaptation plans, guiding investments in sea‑defence infrastructure, coastal zoning, and emergency preparedness.

Why the numbers matter

The 12‑trillion‑ton figure is not just a headline; it is a tangible measure of the planet’s changing climate. Each tonne of ice lost represents a volume of water that will eventually join the oceans, raising sea level and altering ocean circulation patterns. The acceleration observed in the last two decades signals that the climate system is responding more rapidly than many models previously anticipated. This has implications for the timing of sea‑level rise, the frequency of extreme weather events, and the resilience of ecosystems that depend on stable shoreline conditions.

Moreover, the gravimetric approach offers a unique advantage: it is independent of surface observations and can capture mass changes in remote, inaccessible regions such as the interior of Antarctica. This reduces reliance on sparse field measurements and improves the robustness of global ice budgets.

Looking ahead

The next decade will be critical. GRACE‑FO will continue to provide high‑quality gravity data until at least 2030, while CryoSat‑2 and future missions like the European Space Agency’s upcoming CryoSat‑3 will refine surface height measurements. Together, these instruments will enable scientists to detect subtle shifts in ice dynamics, distinguish between melt‑driven and dynamic loss, and refine sea‑level rise projections. In parallel, climate models are incorporating these observations to improve their representation of ice‑sheet processes. Early indications suggest that, if greenhouse‑gas emissions continue on a high‑trajectory path, the rate of ice loss could double by mid‑century, pushing global sea‑level rise beyond 10 centimetres by 2100 [5].

Conclusion

Satellite gravimetry has turned the abstract idea of “ice loss” into a precise, measurable quantity. The 12‑trillion‑ton figure over the past 47 years is a stark reminder that the polar ice sheets are not merely passive reservoirs of frozen water—they are active participants in Earth’s climate system, and their rapid decline is reshaping the planet’s future. As the world prepares for the next UN climate conference, these numbers will be central to the discussions on mitigation, adaptation, and the urgent need to curb emissions.
