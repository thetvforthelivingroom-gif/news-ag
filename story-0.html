Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites Reveal More Than 12 Trillion Tons of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time, a continuous record from space has shown that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed more than 12 trillion tons of ice since the early 1970s. That loss could raise global sea level by roughly one metre if all the ice were to melt. The rate of decline has accelerated sharply over the past two decades, underscoring the urgency of climate action.</p> <h2>What the Satellites Tell Us</h2> <p>Ice sheets do not melt directly into the ocean. Instead, they lose mass through surface meltwater runoff and sublimation, which then flows to the sea. Satellites measure the resulting mass change indirectly. When ice melts, the mass that was stored on land is transferred to the ocean, slightly weakening Earth’s gravity in that region. By monitoring these subtle changes, scientists can calculate how much ice has disappeared (Ligtenberg et al., 2022).</p> <p>The <em>Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment</em> (GRACE), launched in 2002, and its successor, GRACE‑Follow‑On (GRACE‑FO), launched in 2018, have provided a continuous record of gravitational variations from 2002 to the present. CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, has supplied complementary radar altimetry of surface elevation. Together, these missions have yielded a high‑resolution, long‑term inventory of ice‑mass change that is consistent with independent ground‑based mass‑balance studies (Rignot et al., 2023).</p> <p>Over the 47‑year period, the ice sheets have steadily declined, with the rate of loss increasing in the final decade. The cumulative loss of 12 trillion tons—equivalent to a global mean sea‑level rise of about 1 m—indicates that the cryosphere is no longer in equilibrium with a warming climate. The processes driving melt—such as increased surface temperatures, ocean heat uptake, and changes in precipitation patterns—are intensifying (Schoof, 2021).</p> <h2>Historical Context and Methodological Uncertainties</h2> <p>The satellite record builds on a broader scientific effort that began with ground‑based mass‑balance studies in the 1970s, which used GPS, radio‑echo sounding, and mass‑balance models to estimate ice loss. These early studies laid the groundwork for the satellite era. The first satellite gravimetry mission, GRACE, began in 2002, not the 1990s, and its successor, GRACE‑FO, started in 2018. CryoSat‑2, launched in 2010, has been the primary radar altimeter for ice‑sheet elevation since 2010 (IPCC, 2021).</p> <p>Although the satellite measurements are highly precise, they are subject to several sources of uncertainty. Errors in gravity‑field inversion can arise from the limited spatial resolution of the satellite orbits. Assumptions about ice density, necessary to convert mass change into volume change, introduce additional uncertainty, especially in regions with complex sub‑glacial geology. Sparse in‑situ validation in remote polar regions limits the ability to fully calibrate satellite data. Researchers routinely cross‑validate satellite results with independent observations—such as GPS, ground‑penetrating radar, and airborne lidar—to constrain these uncertainties (Ligtenberg et al., 2022).</p> <h2>Implications for Climate Models and Policy</h2> <p>Climate models have long projected that warming would drive significant ice loss. The satellite record confirms these projections but also highlights the magnitude of the loss. The rate of ice‑mass loss has accelerated in recent years, suggesting that feedback mechanisms—such as the ice‑albedo effect and melt‑water lubrication of ice‑sheet basal layers—may be amplifying the response to warming (Schoof, 2021).</p> <p>Policy makers use satellite data to assess the effectiveness of mitigation and adaptation strategies. The empirical evidence of accelerating ice loss signals that current efforts may be insufficient to stabilize the cryosphere. International climate agreements, such as the Paris Agreement, rely on robust, verifiable data to set targets and monitor progress. The satellite record therefore plays a critical role in informing both national commitments and global climate governance (IPCC, 2021).</p> <h2>Stakeholder Perspectives</h2> <p><strong>Indigenous Communities</strong>—In Greenland, Inuit communities have reported changes in the timing of ice melt that affect hunting routes and cultural practices. Their traditional knowledge corroborates the satellite data, underscoring the urgency of addressing climate change (Kang et al., 2023).</p> <p><strong>Climate Modelers</strong>—Modelers use satellite data to validate and refine their ice‑sheet models. The high‑resolution, long‑term record helps constrain uncertainties in ice dynamics and melt‑water routing, improving projections of future sea‑level rise (Rignot et al., 2023).</p> <p><strong>Insurance and Finance Sectors</strong>—Insurance companies incorporate satellite‑derived ice‑loss metrics into risk models. Rising sea‑level projections influence property valuations, infrastructure investment decisions, and insurance premiums in coastal regions worldwide. The satellite data provide an objective basis for these economic assessments (Miller & Sullivan, 2022).</p> <p><strong>Local Governments</strong>—Coastal municipalities use satellite data to plan adaptation measures such as sea‑walls, managed retreat, and flood‑resilient infrastructure. The data help prioritize investments and monitor the effectiveness of adaptation strategies (Huang et al., 2021).</p> <p><strong>Critics and Skeptics</strong>—Some skeptics question the reliability of satellite measurements, citing potential calibration errors, orbital decay, and assumptions about ice density. While the missions have undergone rigorous validation against ground truth data, uncertainties remain, especially in remote polar regions where in‑situ data are sparse (Ligtenberg et al., 2022). These debates highlight the importance of continued methodological refinement and cross‑validation among different observational platforms.</p> <h2>Future Directions and Global Implications</h2> <p>If the current trajectory of ice loss continues, global sea level could rise by more than a metre by the end of the century. Low‑lying nations and island communities would face increased flooding, storm surge amplification, and saltwater intrusion into freshwater aquifers. Adaptation measures—such as sea‑walls, managed retreat, and flood‑resilient infrastructure—will require substantial investment. The satellite data provide a baseline against which to measure progress and assess the effectiveness of adaptation strategies (IPCC, 2021).</p> <p>Upcoming missions, such as GRACE‑FO and the CryoSat‑2 extension, aim to provide finer temporal and spatial detail. Coupled with advances in data assimilation and modeling, these efforts will enhance our ability to predict future ice loss and its cascading effects on the climate system (Rignot et al., 2023).</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite‑derived evidence of more than 12 trillion tons of ice lost in 47 years is a stark reminder of the pace at which the cryosphere is changing. It calls for a coordinated response that blends scientific insight, policy action, and community resilience. By integrating satellite observations with ground‑based studies, engaging diverse stakeholders, and refining our understanding of uncertainties, we can better anticipate and mitigate the profound impacts of ice loss on the planet.</p> <h3>References</h3> <ul> <li>IPCC. 2021. <em>Climate Change 2021: The Physical Science Basis</em>.
