Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<article> <h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 5.5 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 years</h1> <p>For the first time, a continuous record of space‑borne gravimetry and laser altimetry has allowed scientists to quantify the cumulative loss of ice from the two largest ice sheets on Earth. Between 1997 and 2024, Greenland and Antarctica together have shed about 5.5 trillion tonnes of ice—roughly 6 trillion cubic metres. When melted, this volume would raise global sea level by about 0.45 centimetres. The loss has accelerated sharply since the early 2010s, signalling that the polar regions are moving into a new, more vulnerable climate regime.</p> <h2>What the satellites reveal</h2> <p>Satellite missions have tracked the mass of Greenland and Antarctica since the late 1990s. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor, GRACE‑FO, launched in 2002 and 2018 respectively, have measured changes in the Earth’s gravitational field that are directly related to mass redistribution. Complementary laser altimetry from ICESat (2003) and CryoSat‑2 (2010) has provided precise surface elevation changes. Together, these datasets show a cumulative loss of about 5.5 trillion tonnes of ice over 47 years (Ligtenberg et al., 2023).</p> <p>During the 1990s, ice‑sheet mass loss was modest and roughly linear, at a rate of 0.1–0.2 trillion tonnes per year. Since 2010, the rate has roughly doubled, reaching 0.6–0.8 trillion tonnes per year. This acceleration is linked to rapid atmospheric and oceanic warming, which has intensified surface melt and triggered large ice‑sheet collapse events, such as the disintegration of the Larsen B ice shelf in 2002 and the rapid thinning of the Antarctic Peninsula (Rignot et al., 2023).</p> <p>Converting ice‑mass loss to sea‑level rise requires accounting for the density of ice (≈917 kg m⁻³) and the area of the world’s oceans (≈3.6 × 10¹⁴ m²). The 5.5 trillion‑tonne figure corresponds to about 6 trillion cubic metres of ice, which would raise global sea level by roughly 0.45 centimetres. While this is a small fraction of the projected 0.3–0.6 metre rise by 2100 under high‑emission scenarios (IPCC, 2022), it represents the cumulative loss that has already occurred and sets the baseline for future increases.</p> <h2>Methodology and data sources</h2> <p>GRACE and GRACE‑FO measure the Earth’s gravity field with centimetre‑level precision, allowing scientists to infer changes in mass distribution over time. When combined with laser altimetry, which tracks surface elevation changes, researchers can separate ice‑sheet mass loss into contributions from surface melt, ice‑flow dynamics, and basal melting (Huang et al., 2022).</p> <p>Early analyses of GRACE data suggested a modest, roughly linear decline in ice mass. However, subsequent studies that incorporated CryoSat‑2 observations revealed a pronounced acceleration beginning around 2010–2012. This shift coincides with the period of rapid global temperature rise and the onset of more frequent and intense melt‑water pulses (Ligtenberg et al., 2023). The combined use of gravimetry and altimetry also reduces uncertainties in the attribution of loss mechanisms, providing a more nuanced picture of how each ice sheet is responding to climate forcing.</p> <h2>Patterns of loss and acceleration</h2> <p>Greenland’s contribution to the cumulative loss is roughly 2.5 trillion tonnes, while Antarctica accounts for about 3 trillion tonnes. The acceleration is not uniform across the two sheets. In Greenland, the surge in surface melt is largely driven by atmospheric warming and the formation of melt ponds that accelerate ice‑sheet ablation. In Antarctica, ocean warming has led to basal melting of the ice shelves, especially along the West Antarctic margin, where the ice is floating on relatively warm ocean water (Wang et al., 2024).</p> <p>Satellite data also reveal episodic “melt‑water pulses” that can cause rapid, short‑term increases in mass loss. These pulses often coincide with heatwaves or anomalously warm ocean currents, and they can trigger the collapse of ice shelves that act as buttresses to the ice flow. The loss of such buttresses reduces the resistance to ice discharge, leading to a self‑reinforcing cycle of acceleration.</p> <h2>Implications for sea level and climate</h2> <p>While the 0.45‑centimetre contribution to sea level is modest relative to the long‑term projections, it is a tangible, observable change that can be used to validate climate models. The observed acceleration in ice‑sheet mass loss is now being incorporated into the latest generation of global climate models, which project that continued warming could trigger further rapid loss and potentially lead to tipping points such as the collapse of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet (Wang et al., 2024).</p> <p>In addition to sea‑level rise, the loss of ice mass alters the Earth’s albedo, leading to a positive feedback that amplifies warming. The reduction in reflective surface area also affects atmospheric circulation patterns, potentially intensifying extreme weather events in mid‑latitude regions. These cascading effects underscore the interconnectedness of polar processes with global climate dynamics.</p> <h2>Regional impacts and societal consequences</h2> <p>Coastal communities, particularly in low‑lying island nations and delta regions, face heightened risks of flooding, saltwater intrusion, and loss of habitable land. Indigenous peoples in the Arctic and Antarctic regions, whose livelihoods depend on sea‑ice and marine ecosystems, are already experiencing cultural and economic disruptions (Berkes et al., 2022). The rapid loss of ice also threatens the stability of permafrost, which can release stored methane—a potent greenhouse gas—further accelerating warming.</p> <p>Governments are responding by tightening commitments under the Paris Agreement and negotiating new targets at the COP meetings. The latest satellite data provide a concrete metric that policymakers can use to justify more aggressive emissions cuts and to justify funding for adaptation infrastructure, such as sea‑walls and flood‑gate systems (UNFCCC, 2023). <article> <h1>Satellites show Earth lost more than 5.5 trillion tonnes of ice from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 years</h1>

Between 1997 and 2024, Greenland and Antarctica together have shed about 5.5 trillion tonnes of ice—roughly 6 trillion cubic metres. When melted, this volume would raise global sea level by about 0.45 centimetres. The loss has accelerated sharply since the early 2010s, signalling that the polar regions are moving into a new, more vulnerable climate regime.</p>

Complementary laser altimetry from ICESat (2003) and CryoSat‑2 (2010) has provided precise surface elevation changes. Together, these datasets show a cumulative loss of about 5.5 trillion tonnes of ice over 47 years (Ligtenberg et al., 2023).</p>
