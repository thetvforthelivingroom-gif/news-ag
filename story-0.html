Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<article> <h1>Satellites Reveal Over 17 Trillion Tonnes of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1> <p>Since the first Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) satellite launched in 1997, a continuous, global record of Earth’s changing gravity field has shown that the planet’s two largest ice sheets have shed more than 17 trillion tonnes of ice. That loss corresponds to roughly 11 centimetres of global sea‑level rise—an amount that would inundate low‑lying coastlines, threaten critical infrastructure, and reshape the economic landscape before the next Paris Agreement review in 2030. The data, derived from precise satellite gravimetry, are now a benchmark against which national pledges and climate‑finance mechanisms are measured.</p> <h2>What the satellites have measured</h2> <p>GRACE and its successor GRACE‑FO have been monitoring Earth’s gravity field since 1997, while ESA’s CryoSat‑2 and NASA’s ICESat‑2 have provided complementary surface‑elevation data. Together, these missions have quantified the mass loss of Greenland and Antarctica with unprecedented precision. Current analyses show that Greenland alone has lost more than 12.5 trillion tonnes of ice since 1997, while Antarctica has contributed an additional 4.5 trillion tonnes, for a combined total of about 17 trillion tonnes [1, 2]. This mass loss translates to a global sea‑level rise of roughly 11 centimetres, with an uncertainty of ±0.5 centimetres that reflects both measurement noise and the complex process of glacial isostatic adjustment (GIA) [3]. The figure is far larger than the 12 trillion‑tonne estimate that appeared in earlier satellite‑only studies, and it underscores the accelerating pace of ice melt in the last half‑century.</p> <h2>How gravimetry works</h2> <p>The principle behind GRACE and GRACE‑FO is simple yet powerful: two identical satellites orbit Earth in tandem, separated by about 220 kilometres. As they pass over regions of varying mass—such as a thick ice sheet or a dense mountain range—the gravitational pull on each satellite changes by a few millimetres per year. By measuring the distance between the satellites with laser ranging to within a few micrometres, scientists can infer the local gravity field and, by integration, the total mass change over time. Because the satellites circle the globe every 4.5 days, the method provides a near‑real‑time, global record that is immune to the biases of ground‑based observations alone [4].</p> <h2>Why 1997 is the starting point</h2> <p>GRACE’s launch in 1997 marked the first time that the global gravity field could be monitored continuously with the precision required to detect millimetre‑level mass changes. Prior to GRACE, ice‑sheet mass loss was inferred from a combination of satellite altimetry, ground‑based mass balance studies, and radar interferometry, all of which suffered from limited spatial coverage and temporal gaps. The 47‑year period covered by GRACE and GRACE‑FO therefore represents the longest, most homogeneous record of ice‑sheet mass change available to scientists today. Earlier altimetry missions, such as the European Space Agency’s CryoSat‑2 (launched 2010), provide high‑resolution surface‑elevation data that, when combined with GRACE gravimetry, allow researchers to separate surface melt from basal melt and to improve estimates of ice‑sheet dynamics [5].</p> <h2>Drivers of the observed ice loss</h2> <p>Anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas emissions have increased atmospheric concentrations of CO₂, methane, and other radiative gases, raising global temperatures by about 1.1 °C since pre‑industrial times. The warming of the atmosphere and the ocean surface fuels two complementary melt pathways: atmospheric warming melts ice from above, while oceanic warming melts ice from below. The loss of ice also reduces Earth’s albedo, causing more solar radiation to be absorbed and further amplifying warming in a positive feedback loop. Recent peer‑reviewed studies have quantified these processes, showing that the rate of ice loss has accelerated in the last decade as surface temperatures rise and ocean heat content increases [6, 7].</p> <h2>Uncertainties and challenges</h2> <p>While the overall signal of mass loss is robust across multiple independent data sets, several sources of uncertainty remain. First, the satellites must be calibrated to avoid systematic errors; even a small bias in the laser ranging system can translate into a significant error in mass estimates. Second, glacial isostatic adjustment—the slow rebound of land that was previously compressed by the weight of ice—can mimic or mask ice‑sheet mass changes if not properly accounted for. Third, natural climate variability, such as the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO) and the El Niño‑Southern Oscillation (ENSO), can temporarily influence ice‑sheet mass balance, adding noise to the long‑term trend. Finally, data gaps caused by satellite maneuvers or instrument downtime require interpolation, which introduces additional uncertainty. Despite these challenges, the convergence of GRACE, GRACE‑FO, CryoSat‑2, and ICESat‑2 data gives confidence that the trend of rapid ice loss is real and accelerating.</p> <h2>Historical context</h2> <p>Before the satellite era, estimates of ice‑sheet mass loss relied on aerial photography, ground surveys, and limited radar measurements. <article> <h1>Satellites Reveal Over 17 Trillion Tonnes of Ice Lost from Greenland and Antarctica in 47 Years</h1>

<p>Since the first Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) satellite launched in 1997, a continuous, global record of Earth’s changing gravity field has shown that the planet’s two largest ice sheets have shed more than 17 trillion tonnes of ice. That loss corresponds to roughly 11 centimetres of global sea‑level rise—an amount that would inundate low‑lying coastlines, threaten critical infrastructure, and reshape the economic landscape before the next Paris Agreement review in 2030. The data, derived from precise satellite gravimetry, are now a benchmark against which national pledges and climate‑finance mechanisms are measured.</p>

<p>GRACE and its successor GRACE‑FO have been monitoring Earth’s gravity field since 1997, while ESA’s CryoSat‑2 and NASA’s ICESat‑2 have provided complementary surface‑elevation data. Together, these missions have quantified the mass loss of Greenland and Antarctica with unprecedented precision. Current analyses show that Greenland alone has lost more than 12.5 trillion tonnes of ice since 1997, while Antarctica has contributed an additional 4.5 trillion tonnes, for a combined total of about 17 trillion tonnes [1, 2]. This mass loss translates to a global sea‑level rise of roughly 11 centimetres, with an uncertainty of ±0.5 centimetres that reflects both measurement noise and the complex process of glacial isostatic adjustment (GIA) [3]. The figure is far larger than the 12 trillion‑tonne estimate that appeared in earlier satellite‑only studies, and it underscores the accelerating pace of ice melt in the last half‑century.</p>
