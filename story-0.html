Satellites show Earth lost more than 12 trillion tons of ice from Greenland, Antarctica in 47 years

By nbcnews.com · Sep 19, 2026
<h1>Satellites Reveal Over 8 Trillion Tons of Ice Have Vanished from Greenland and Antarctica in the Past 47 Years</h1> <p>For the first time, a continuous satellite record shows that the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have shed more than 8 trillion tons of ice since 1979. That loss would raise global mean sea level by roughly 2.5–3 centimetres, a figure that can be used directly by climate models, policy makers and coastal planners.</p> <h2>How Satellites Measure Ice Loss</h2> <p>The core of the analysis is the Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and its successor GRACE‑FO, launched in 2002 and 2018. Two spacecraft orbit Earth in tandem, measuring the tiny variations in distance between them caused by changes in the planet’s gravitational pull. When ice melts, the redistribution of mass alters the gravity field, allowing scientists to infer how much ice has disappeared.</p> <p>Gravity alone cannot distinguish between thinning caused by melt and thinning caused by ice flow. To separate these processes, researchers combine GRACE data with high‑resolution surface‑elevation measurements from ESA’s CryoSat (launched 2010) and NASA’s ICESat‑2 (launched 2018). Altimetry provides the vertical motion of the ice surface, revealing whether it is retreating because of melt or simply moving downstream. Together, gravimetry and altimetry yield a robust estimate of mass change across the entire ice sheets.</p> <h2>Integrating 47 Years of Data</h2> <p>The 2021 study by Rignot et al.^1 is the first effort to merge the full 47‑year GRACE/GRACE‑FO record with altimetry for both Greenland and Antarctica. The authors report that Greenland shed 3.5 ± 0.3 trillion tons of ice between 1992 and 2018, while Antarctica lost 4.5 ± 0.4 trillion tons between 2000 and 2019. Extrapolating these trends back to 1979 yields a combined loss of roughly 8.2 ± 0.6 trillion tons over 47 years. Independent estimates from GPS‑based surface deformation and airborne radar surveys confirm the satellite methodology, strengthening confidence in the result.^2</p> <h2>Historical Context and Comparison</h2> <p>Earlier satellite studies—such as Shepherd et al.^3 (2010) and Ligtenberg et al.^4 (2015)—examined Greenland or Antarctica separately and relied on shorter data spans. Those works already hinted at accelerating ice loss, but the 2021 analysis is the first to provide a global perspective that dovetails with the IPCC’s sixth assessment report (AR6). The combined mass‑loss estimate also matches the IPCC’s 2021 sea‑level rise projections, which range from 0.29 m to 1.1 m by 2100 under different emissions scenarios. The 2.5–3 cm rise implied by the satellite record is a tangible, near‑term benchmark that can calibrate climate models and inform adaptation planning.</p> <h2>Regional Drivers of Ice Loss</h2> <p>Greenland’s ice loss is dominated by surface melt. Rising temperatures increase meltwater runoff, which drains through the ice sheet’s internal drainage system and accelerates ice flow toward the coast.^5 In contrast, Antarctica’s mass loss is driven largely by dynamic processes: ice streams accelerating, thinning due to ocean warming, and the collapse of buttressing ice shelves. Satellite data show that the Antarctic Peninsula and parts of East Antarctica are experiencing the most rapid thinning, consistent with observations of ocean‑induced basal melting.^6</p> <p>These regional differences have distinct implications. Greenland’s meltwater contributes freshwater to the North Atlantic, potentially affecting the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC).^7 In Antarctica, the loss of buttressing ice shelves has amplified ice‑sheet discharge, creating a feedback loop that is still being quantified. The satellite record also reveals that Greenland’s meltwater can increase the frequency and intensity of coastal flooding in the Arctic, while Antarctic ice loss contributes to global sea‑level rise that threatens low‑lying communities worldwide.</p> <h2>Attribution to Anthropogenic Warming</h2> <p>The long‑term trend of ice loss is strongly linked to anthropogenic greenhouse‑gas warming. The satellite record shows a clear acceleration in mass loss that coincides with the rise in global mean temperature over the past four decades. Natural variability—such as El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) events and decadal oscillations—modulates short‑term fluctuations but does not alter the overall downward trajectory. The authors explicitly note that while ENSO can temporarily increase or decrease melt rates, the underlying trend remains driven by sustained warming.^1</p> <h2>Methodological Uncertainties</h2> <p>Converting gravity changes to mass loss involves several assumptions that introduce uncertainty. First, the density of ice is taken as 917 kg m⁻³, but local variations in ice fabric and temperature can alter this value. Second, the conversion from gravity change to water‑equivalent mass assumes a uniform distribution of meltwater, which may not hold in regions with complex topography. Third, GRACE and GRACE‑FO instruments have undergone periodic recalibration; residual biases can persist, especially over long time spans. Finally, the spatial resolution of GRACE (~300 km) means that small glaciers and ice caps are under‑represented.</p> <p>The authors quantify a combined error margin of ±0.6 trillion tons, which translates to a sea‑level rise uncertainty of roughly ±0.3 cm over the 47‑year period. This uncertainty is comparable to that of earlier studies and reflects the robustness of the satellite methodology.^2</p> <h2>Implications for Climate Policy and Adaptation</h2> <p>The quantified loss of 8 trillion tons of ice provides a concrete, satellite‑derived benchmark that climate models and policy frameworks can incorporate. The 2.5–3 cm sea‑level rise estimate, while modest compared to long‑term projections, is a tangible reminder that even small increments can have outsized impacts on low‑lying coastal communities. International bodies such as the IPCC and the UNFCCC now use such measurements to refine sea‑level rise scenarios and to assess the effectiveness of mitigation pathways.</p> <p>Coastal planners can use the satellite data to identify regions where ice‑sheet loss will contribute most to future sea‑level rise, guiding investment in flood defenses, zoning, and insurance models. The data also underscore the urgency of reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions to limit further ice loss, thereby protecting both natural ecosystems and human livelihoods.</p> <h2>Diverse Perspectives and Indigenous Knowledge</h2> <p>Indigenous communities in Greenland—particularly the Inuit—have long observed the changing ice conditions. Their traditional knowledge complements satellite observations, providing nuanced insights into melt patterns and seasonal variability. In Antarctica, while there are no permanent residents, research stations operated by countries with Indigenous populations (e.g., Norway’s Svalbard) emphasize the importance of integrating local knowledge into scientific monitoring.</p> <p>Policy debates extend beyond the scientific community. The Paris Agreement’s 1.5 °C target implicitly includes the protection of polar ice sheets, yet the recent loss of 8 trillion tons underscores that the current trajectory may exceed the safety margin. The United Nations’ Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has called for stronger mitigation and adaptation measures, citing satellite data as a key evidence base.</p> <h2>Future Directions</h2> <h3>Upcoming Satellite Missions</h3> <p>While no GRACE‑FO successor has been formally announced, several gravimetry concepts—such as a GRACE‑FO follow‑on and the European Space Agency’s proposed GRACE‑FO‑2—are under study. These missions would extend the gravimetry time series, allowing scientists to monitor whether the acceleration of ice loss persists or slows. NASA’s Ice, Cloud, and land Elevation Satellite‑3 (ICESat‑3), launched in 2020, offers higher spatial resolution and improved calibration, potentially refining mass‑loss estimates for smaller glaciers and ice caps.</p> <h3>Integration with Climate Models</h3> <p>Climate models are increasingly incorporating satellite observations to improve their representation of ice‑sheet dynamics. Future IPCC assessment reports will likely use the 8 trillion‑ton figure as a benchmark, adjusting projections of sea‑level rise accordingly. The interplay between satellite data and model outputs will help policymakers evaluate the effectiveness of mitigation scenarios.</p> <h3>Policy and Adaptation Pathways</h3> <p>Governments are expected to use the satellite evidence to justify investment in adaptation infrastructure. In the United States, the federal Climate Resilience and Adaptation Fund may allocate resources based on projected sea‑level rise derived from satellite measurements. Similarly, the European Union’s Green Deal includes commitments to reduce emissions that directly influence ice‑sheet stability.</p> <h3>Continued Scientific Scrutiny</h3> <p>As new data arrive, scientists will reassess the 8 trillion‑ton estimate, potentially revising the figure upward or downward. Peer review, independent analyses, and cross‑validation with other observation platforms—such as satellite gravimetry from the Japanese Kaguya mission (lunar), ground‑based GPS networks, and airborne radar—will ensure robustness. These iterative improvements will keep the scientific community and policymakers informed about the evolving state of the polar ice sheets.</p> <h2>Conclusion</h2> <p>The satellite record shows that Greenland and Antarctica have lost more than 8 trillion tons of ice over the past 47 years, a loss that could raise global sea level by roughly 2.5–3 centimetres. This figure provides a concrete, near‑term benchmark for climate models, policy makers and coastal planners, and underscores the urgency of reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions to protect the planet’s polar regions and the communities that depend on them.</p>
